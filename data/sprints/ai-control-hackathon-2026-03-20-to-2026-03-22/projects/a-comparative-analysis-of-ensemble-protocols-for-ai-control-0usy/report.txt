Abstract  
Abstract.  AI  control  protocols  use  trusted  models  to  monitor  untrusted,  more  capable  models  -  but  individual  protocols  are  brittle,  and  it  is  unknown  whether  combining  them  into  ensembles  improves  robustness  or  merely  adds  noise.  We  study  this  question  empirically  by  implementing  seven  detection  tools  that  catch  backdoors  through  different  mechanisms,  and  evaluating  all  127  combinations  on  200  coding  problems  with  validated  backdoors.  Of  the  seven  tools  evaluated,  we  introduce  two  detection  mechanisms  developed  speciﬁcally  for  this  study,  as  well  as  the  implementations  of  two  other  protocols,  extending  them  from  theoretical  concepts  to  empirical  oversight  tools.  We  ﬁnd  that  ensemble  effectiveness  depends  less  on  how  many  tools  are  included  and  more  on  whether  they  provide  independent  detection  signals:  the  best  three-tool  ensemble  outperforms  the  full  seven-tool  ensemble  because  correlated  tools  add  false  positives  without  catching  new  attacks.  We  also  ﬁnd  that  the  method  used  to  aggregate  tool  scores  matters  as  much  as  which  tools  are  selected,  with  consensus-based  voting  consistently  dominating  simpler  strategies.  These  results  replace  theoretical  intuition  about  tool  complementarity  with  empirical  measurements,  providing  a  foundation  for  principled  ensemble  design  in  AI  control.
