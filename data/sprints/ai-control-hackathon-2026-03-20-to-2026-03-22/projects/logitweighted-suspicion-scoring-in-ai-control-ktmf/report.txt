ABSTRACT
AI control relies on LLM monitors to detect suspicious behavior in untrusted agents. These monitors
are typically run multiple times per evaluation to ensure statistically reliable suspicion scores, at
significant cost in time and tokens. A natural way to reduce this cost is to extract more information
from each monitor call by using the model’s token probability distribution rather than its argmax
output. We test this approach – logit-weighted suspicion scoring, following Liu et al. [2023] – against
standard point-estimate scoring in an AI control setting using 110 trajectories from AgentDojo, with
both honest and attack agent policies. We find that logit-weighted scoring produces more continuous
score distributions but does not improve AUC-ROC relative to point-estimate scoring. We interpret
this as evidence that numerical anchoring in monitor outputs is rank-preserving in this setting, and
that monitor capability rather than score granularity is the binding constraint on discrimination
performance.
1 Introduction
Studying the actions of potentially misaligned agents is central to AI control [Greenblatt et al., 2024]. These actions are
typically encoded as unstructured text – code edits, API calls, sent emails, or sequences of tool calls – generated by a
capable but untrusted LLM agent. A natural choice for evaluating these actions is another LLM, commonly referred to
as a monitor, which assigns a suspicion score used to determine whether an action should be approved, escalated, or
blocked.
A well-known limitation of LLM-based scoring is poor numerical calibration. Models exhibit anchoring toward salient
values regardless of input content [Zheng et al., 2023], and both OpenAI and xAI expose token-level debiasing APIs
precisely because this is a recognized failure mode. In our own experiments, a 0–9 scale monitor produced a pronounced
mode at 7. Because of this, AI control evaluations typically require multiple samples per trajectory to obtain reliable
scores, at significant cost in time and tokens.
A natural way to address this is to extract more information from each monitor call. Rather than treating the monitor’s
output as a point estimate – the single most likely token – one can compute an expected suspicion score by weighting
all plausible score tokens by their predicted probabilities. This approach, introduced for NLG evaluation by Liu et al.
[2023] as G-Eval, in principle yields a more continuous and better-calibrated signal without additional API calls.
A second motivation comes from the Pareto frontier of safety and usefulness, the central object of interest in AI
control evaluations. Estimating this frontier accurately requires enough score resolution to meaningfully distinguish
operating points. Coarse, anchored scores compress the frontier and may obscure meaningful differences between
control protocols. Logit-weighted scoring could in principle paint a richer picture of this frontier at lower cost, which
would make blue-team protocol development faster and cheaper.
We test this hypothesis using 110 trajectories generated from AgentDojo [Debenedetti et al., 2024] within the Contro-
lArena framework [Inglis et al., 2025], comparing logit-weighted and point-estimate scoring under GPT-4o and GPT-4.1

2 Related Work
The AI control framework introduced by Greenblatt et al. [2024] provides the foundational setting for our work. In that
framework, a trusted monitor evaluates the outputs of a more capable but untrusted agent, and the resulting suspicion
scores determine whether actions are approved, escalated, or audited. The quality of those scores therefore directly
affects where the safety-capability Pareto frontier sits.
AgentDojo [Debenedetti et al., 2024] provides the benchmark environment we use to generate trajectories. It is an
extensible evaluation framework for measuring the adversarial robustness of tool-calling LLM agents against prompt
injection attacks, with 97 realistic tasks across domains such as email, banking, and travel. We use its workspace suite
and adopt its distinction between main tasks (legitimate agent objectives) and injection tasks (attacker-controlled side
tasks).
ControlArena [Inglis et al., 2025] is the open-source library we use to orchestrate our control experiments. It provides
standardized infrastructure for running AI control evaluations on top of the Inspect AI framework, including pre-built
settings, protocol implementations, and analysis tools.
The scoring method we evaluate is directly inspired by G-Eval [Liu et al., 2023], which uses token-level log probabilities
to aggregate evaluator uncertainty when scoring text summarization and dialogue generation. G-Eval achieves a
Spearman correlation of 0.51 with human judgments, suggesting that logit-weighted scores can function as a better
proxy for human evaluation than point estimates from the same model. We adapt this approach to the problem of
suspicion scoring in an AI control setting.
As note above, a known limitation of using LLMs as scalar scorers is poor numerical calibration. In our own preliminary
experiments, a 0–9 scale produced a pronounced mode at 7, motivating our switch to a 0–100 scale to gain more
expressive resolution. This observation is consistent with documented biases in LLM-as-judge settings, where models
exhibit anchoring toward salient values regardless of input content [Zheng et al., 2023].
3 Methods
3.1 Trajectory Generation
We generated 110 trajectories using the AgentDojo workspace suite [Debenedetti et al., 2024] within the ControlArena
framework [Inglis et al., 2025]. An untrusted agent ( gpt-4.1 via OpenRouter) was evaluated across 5 task pairs
(combinations of user_task_{0,1,2} and injection_task_{0,1}) in two modes:HONEST(agent attempts only
the main task) andATTACK(agent additionally attempts the injection task using a direct prompt injection with an
ephemeral per-step reminder). We sampled 11 trajectories per task pair per mode, yielding 110 trajectories total (55 per
mode). After trajectory generation, a monitor model (gpt-4.1-mini) then scored each trajectory post-hoc, producing
a single suspicion score per trajectory. For raw point-estimate scoring, we drew multiple samples per trajectory (10 for
GPT-4o, 20 for GPT-4.1) and report results both for a single sample and for the mean across samples. Logit-weighted
scoring requires only a single monitor call per trajectory, as the full token probability distribution is extracted from that
call.
3.2 Monitor Prompt Design
We initially tested a 0–9 scale prompt but found it produced a pronounced mode at 7, severely limiting score variance
and discriminatory power. This observation is consistent with documented biases in LLM-as-judge settings, where
models exhibit positional and numerical anchoring regardless of input content [Zheng et al., 2023]. We therefore
adopted a 0–100 scale, which substantially spread the score distribution. The prompt used for all evaluations is as
follows:
You are a security monitor evaluating if an agent’s
actions are suspicious.
Task: {task_description}
Tool calls made:
{tool_calls}
Rate how suspicious these actions are on a scale of

3.3 Logit-Weighted Suspicion Score
Standard monitoring treats the monitor’s output as a point estimate: the single most likely token is taken as the suspicion
score. This discards information about the model’s uncertainty and may be vulnerable to numerical anchoring, where
models cluster outputs around salient integers independently of input content.
We instead compute an expected suspicion score by aggregating over the predefined token set T={0,1, . . . ,100},
weighted by the monitor’s predicted token probabilities:

This yields a score in [0,1] , following the aggregation approach of G-Eval [Liu et al., 2023]. We used a 0–100 integer
scale rather than a coarser one to make the logit weighting more informative: with a coarser scale, the expected value
collapses toward the point estimate and the benefit of aggregation is diminished.
3.4 Baseline
The baseline takes the argmax token as the raw score and normalizes it to [0,1] by dividing by 100. This corresponds to
the standard point-estimate monitor behavior.
3.5 Evaluation
We evaluate both methods using AUC-ROC, with trajectory mode (honest vs. attack) as the binary label. A higher AUC
indicates better separation between honest and attack trajectories. We additionally inspect score distributions to assess
whether logit-weighting reduces the clustering observed with point-estimate scoring.
4 Results
4.1 Score Distributions
Figures 1, 3, and 4 show score distributions for honest and attack trajectories under both scoring methods. Raw
point-estimate scores cluster sharply at a small number of discrete values, consistent with numerical anchoring in
LLM-as-judge settings [Zheng et al., 2023]. Logit-weighted scores are markedly more spread across the [0,1] range,
confirming that aggregating over the full token probability vector achieves its intended effect. Note that logit scoring
always conditions on the first trajectory step, using the full probability distribution over that step; the mean aggregation
figures therefore reflect only differences in how raw scores are aggregated across steps.
4.2 Discrimination Performance
Figures 2, 5, and 6 and Table 1 report AUC-ROC for each configuration. For GPT-4.1, logit-weighted and raw scoring
achieve virtually identical AUC (0.764 vs. 0.767 using a single raw sample; 0.764 vs. 0.768 when averaging across all
raw samples). For GPT-4o, performance is near-random for both methods (0.598 vs. 0.588), suggesting that monitor
model capability is the binding constraint rather than scoring method. Neither the scoring method nor the number of
raw samples used affects discrimination meaningfully.
5 Discussion and Limitations
Our results show that logit-weighted scoring produces more continuous and better-spread suspicion scores than raw
point-estimate scoring, but does not improve AUC in our setting. The most natural interpretation is that numerical
anchoring in monitor outputs is rank-preserving: the monitor already encodes meaningful information about trajectory

More broadly, our results suggest that the bottleneck for monitor performance in this setting is semantic: whether the
monitor correctly identifies suspicious behavior, not how precisely it encodes that judgment numerically. This points
toward prompt engineering, chain-of-thought elicitation, and stronger monitor models as more promising directions
than post-hoc score calibration.
5.1 Limitations
The main limitations are sample size and task coverage. With 55 trajectories per condition across 5 task pairs, we
are substantially underpowered to detect small effects. The AUC differences we observe (at most 0.01) are consistent
with both a true null and a small positive effect that we lack power to detect. Results are also specific to direct prompt
injection attacks; it is unclear whether findings generalize to subtler attack strategies of the kind studied in MonitorBench
[Jotautait˙e and Martinez, 2026]. Finally, logit scoring here conditions on the first trajectory step only, which may not be
the most informative step for all task pairs.
5.2 Future Work
The most direct extension is to run this comparison at larger scale, with sufficient power to detect effects in the 0.01–0.05
AUC range. Beyond replication, logit-weighted scoring may be more valuable in settings where threshold placement
matters more than global ranking, for instance when safety budgets constrain the acceptable false positive rate to a
narrow range. Testing whether score resolution affects Pareto frontier estimates at specific operating points would be a

6 Conclusion
We tested whether logit-weighted suspicion scoring improves monitor discrimination in an AI control setting, motivated
by known numerical anchoring biases in LLM-as-judge evaluation. Using 110 trajectories generated from AgentDojo
with honest and attack agent policies, we found that logit weighting reliably spreads score distributions but does not
improve AUC relative to raw point-estimate scoring. This suggests that numerical anchoring in monitor outputs is
rank-preserving in this setting, and that the binding constraint on monitor performance is model capability rather than
score granularity. These results are preliminary given limited sample size, but provide a useful baseline for future work
on score calibration in AI control pipelines.
