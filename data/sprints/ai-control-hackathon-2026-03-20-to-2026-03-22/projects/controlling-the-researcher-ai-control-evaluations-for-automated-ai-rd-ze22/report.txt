Abstract  
As  AI  systems  take  on  increasingly  autonomous  roles  in  machine  learning  research,  it  becomes  critical  to  ask  if  their  outputs  can  be  trusted.  Existing  AI  control  evaluations  focus  on  software  engineering  threats  such  as  code  backdoors  and  data  exﬁltration,  leaving  the  distinctive  risks  of  AI  R&D  itself  unaddressed.  We  introduce  three  control  evaluation  settings  that  together  span  the  ML  development  pipeline:  safety  ﬁne-tuning  sabotage,  RL  post-training  sabotage,  and  backdoors  during  CUDA  kernel  optimization.  Each  setting  includes  both  non-orthogonal  side  tasks,  in  which  sabotage  is  embedded  within  the  main-task  artifact,  and  orthogonal  side  tasks,  in  which  the  agent  takes  discreet  out-of-scope  actions  alongside  an  otherwise  legitimate  task.  We  implement  red-team  attack  policies  for  all  three  settings  and  evaluate  monitors  of  varying  strengths,  from  a  static  LLM  reviewing  ﬁnal  artifacts  to  an  agentic  monitor  with  full  workspace  access.  Our  results  show  a  sharp  asymmetry:  orthogonal  side  tasks  are  trivial  to  complete  but  leave  anomalous  traces  that  static

2.  Related  Work  
AI  Control.  (Greenblatt  et  al.  2024) formalized  the  AI  control  problem:  given  a  capable  but  potentially  untrustworthy  model,  design  protocols  that  prevent  unacceptable  outcomes  even  under  adversarial  conditions.  Their  evaluation  used  the  APPS  benchmark  (Hendrycks  et  al.  2021),  with  subsequent  work  extending  to  bash  tasks  (Bhatt  et  al.  2025),  asynchronous  monitoring  (Stickland  et  al.  2025),  adaptive  attacks  (Terekhov  et  al.  2026),  and  real-world  agent  settings  (Kutasov  et  al.  2025).  ControlArena  (UKGovernmentBEIS/control-arena  2026) consolidates  these  into  a  standardized  platform  spanning  infrastructure-as-code,  data  poisoning,  and  interpretability  sabotage.  Anthropic's  sabotage  evaluations  (Benton  et  al.  2024) complement  this  with  targeted  tests  for  code  sabotage,  sandbagging,  and  oversight  undermining.  Redwood  Research  (Hebbar  2024) further  distinguishes  concentrated  threats  (few  highly  incriminating  actions)  from  diffuse  threats  (many  innocuous-looking  actions),  noting  that  AI  R&D  sabotage  falls  squarely  in  the  latter  category.  While  prior  work  has  addressed  isolated  ML-adjacent  threats  (Ward  et  al.  2025),  no  existing  control  evaluation  targets  end-to-end  AI  R&D  workﬂows,  leaving  a  critical  gap  in  the  
empirical
 
foundations
 
for
 
safe
 
delegation
 
of
 
ML
 
development
 
to
 
autonomous
 
agents.
  Automated  AI  R&D.  Several  benchmarks  measure  AI  agents'  capacity  for  autonomous  research.  Kwa  et  al.  (2026)  evaluated  agents  on  end-to-end  ML  research  tasks;  Wang  et  al.  (2026) studied  execution-grounded  automated  research;  and  PostTrainBench  (Rank  et  al.  2026) benchmarked  autonomous  post-training  workﬂows,  documenting  spontaneous  reward  hacking  behavior  such  as

3.  Method  
We  build  three  control  settings  for  AI  R&D  following  the  standard  structure  (Greenblatt  et  al.  2024;  UKGovernmentBEIS/control-arena  2026):  an  untrusted  agent  completes  a  main  task  and  may  pursue  a  side  task,  while  a  monitor  tries  to  catch  it.
