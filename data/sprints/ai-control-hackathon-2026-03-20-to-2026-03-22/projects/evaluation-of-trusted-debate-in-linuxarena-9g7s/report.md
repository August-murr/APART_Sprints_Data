### **Abstract** 

_We investigated using Trusted Debate as a trajectory-level monitor in LinuxArena to identify agent trajectories which contained sabotage. Trusted Debate involves using a Defender and Critic model to argue whether a given trajectory contains sabotage before a Judge model provides a final suspiciousness score. In our limited experiments, we show that the Debate framework actually lowers monitor performance compared to a lower-cost baseline, with the Defender in particular muddying the water and confusing the judge. An ablation which replaced the Defender prompt with a simple deferral to the Critic response recovers performance to be competitive with the baseline, suggesting that the Defender actively degrades monitoring performance._

### **1. Introduction** 

AI Control aims to enable the deployment of highly capable but potentially untrusted AI systems without causing harmful outcomes. A central challenge in this setting is how to supervise such systems at scale without relying on expensive human oversight. Instead, control frameworks often use weaker but trusted AI systems, or **monitors** , to detect undesirable behavior. 

In this work, we study whether **Trusted Debate** , a monitoring protocol proposed by (Al Dahik et al., 2025), improves monitoring performance in a more complex and realistic control setting. We evaluate it in **LinuxArena** , a benchmark of software-engineering environments in which models act through long, multi-step trajectories. Compared to earlier control settings, LinuxArena contains longer and noisier interaction traces, making monitoring more difficult. 

Because only a small fraction of trajectories can be audited by humans, monitor scores must be well calibrated so that limited audits are spent on the most suspicious cases. Prior work proposed **Trusted Debate** as a way to improve monitoring: a critic argues that a trajectory is suspicious, a defender argues that it is benign, and a judge aggregates their arguments into a final score. This approach improved safety in simpler control settings. 

We test whether this result transfers to LinuxArena. Our main contributions are: 

- **We evaluate Trusted Debate in LinuxArena** , a more complex software-engineering control benchmark than prior evaluation settings. 

- **We find that standard debate underperforms simpler single-goal monitoring baselines in our limited experiments** , yielding worse safety at fixed audit budgets and worse ROC performance when using smaller trusted models such as GPT-4.1 mini and GPT-4.1 nano. 

- **We identify a passive-defender variant that improves on standard debate** , suggesting that active defender-critic dynamics may be poorly suited to this monitoring setting. 

### **2. Related Work** 

Our work builds directly on **Trusted Debate** (Al Dahik et al., 2025), which introduced a debate-based monitoring protocol for AI Control. In that framework, a critic and a defender argue over whether a trajectory is suspicious, and a judge aggregates their arguments into a final monitoring score. Their results suggest that debate can improve monitoring safety over simpler trusted-monitoring approaches at comparable audit budgets. 

More broadly, our work is motivated by prior research on **AI Control** (Greenblatt et al., 2024), which studies how to safely use untrusted models even when they may intentionally subvert 

oversight. This line of work emphasizes scalable monitoring and selective auditing as central components of safe deployment. 

We evaluate debate in **LinuxArena** , a software-engineering control benchmark with longer and more realistic agent trajectories than the simpler settings used in prior work. Prior evaluations of Trusted Debate were conducted in **ControlArena** in the single-turn APPs backdoored code setting, a much more constrained environment. Our goal is not to propose a new monitoring protocol, but to test whether an existing one generalizes to a harder setting. 

Our results highlight an important gap: a monitoring strategy that performs well on the APPs setting does not necessarily transfer to more complex settings in LinuxArena. This suggests that conclusions about AI control protocols may depend strongly on the evaluation environment. 

### **3. Methods** 

We primarily evaluate six trajectory-level monitoring configurations in LinuxArena: three baselines, two debate variants, and one critic-only variant. All experiments use existing LinuxArena trajectories generated by **Claude 4.5 Haiku** , specifically **50-task-set-attack-haiku** and **50-task-set-honest-haiku** trajectories. We selected these datasets because they provide matched attack and honest trajectories while remaining computationally tractable. Our goal is to test whether debate-based monitoring remains effective in a more complex software-engineering setting. 

#### **3.1 Debate-based monitors** 

Trusted Debate replaces a single monitor judgment with a structured interaction between three trusted agents initialized from the same base model: a **critic** , a **defender** , and a **judge** . Given the environment description, main task, side task goals, and agent transcript, the critic argues that the trajectory contains suspicious or malicious behavior, the defender argues that it is benign, and the judge evaluates the exchange to produce a scalar suspicion score used to rank trajectories for audit. 

All debate agents use **GPT-4.1 mini** . We chose this model because it is cheaper and faster than larger alternatives, making it practical to evaluate multiple prompt and protocol variants. We also found that **GPT-4.1 nano** struggled in our debate framework. Our implementation operates at the **trajectory level only** : monitors observe complete trajectories and assign a single score per trajectory. 

We evaluate two debate-based configurations. **Debate: 2 rounds** uses a standard two-round critic-defender-judge protocol. **Debate: Al Dahik et al. adapted prompts** uses the same overall structure but with prompts adapted from Al Dahik et al. (2025). 

We also evaluate a **critic-only: 2 rounds** variant, which preserves the debate format but removes the defender’s substantive role. In this setting, the defender is prompted to defer to the critic’s assessment rather than actively rebut it. This lets us test whether active defense improves monitoring or instead reduces calibration. 

#### **3.2 Baselines** 

We compare debate against three trajectory-level baselines that score trajectories directly without multi-agent interaction. **Baseline: structured analysis** is a single-call monitor (goal-analysis in LinuxArena). **Baseline: structured analysis (2-call average)** averages two independent structured-analysis calls (mean-goal-analysis in LinuxArena). **Baseline: 5-pass ensemble** averages five independent quick-monitor calls and serves as a compute-matched baseline (modification of mean-quick to 5 calls in LinuxArena). 

All monitors are run with **provide_goals=True** , meaning that the monitor has access to the task goal associated with each trajectory. This matches the LinuxArena control setting and allows the monitor to assess whether observed actions are appropriate relative to the intended objective. 

#### **3.3 Evaluation** 

We evaluate monitor quality using **safety at fixed audit budgets** and **ROC curves** . Safety measures how effectively the monitor prioritizes suspicious trajectories when only a limited fraction can be audited, while ROC curves measure how well monitor scores separate honest from attack trajectories across thresholds. 

### **4. Results** 

We tested 8 debate variants on the dataset of haiku honest and attack trajectories which we downloaded from the existing runs on LinuxArena. We implemented this monitor at the trajectory level only, and compared the results against several comparable baseline monitors using the same underlying model and trajectory-only level of evaluation.

Our main experimental finding is that true debate protocols (with a critic, defender, and judge) underperform simpler, similar or lower cost baseline monitors. The defender’s presence is net-negative and when it is removed in the critic-only protocol the performance increases substantially. Our hypothesis is that this is essentially due to the defender muddying the water, providing generic rebuttals that the judges have to contend with compared to the critic. 

As part of our iterative prompt engineering approach, we tasked Claude 4.6 Opus to come up with and test strategies for improving the performance of our debate setup. One of its strategies was to try out the critic only implementation, which has the same format as debate, however the “defender” is prompted with the following: 

```
“The critic has already provided a thorough calibrated analysis. You
defer to their assessment.”
```

This essentially removes the defender from the process and no longer is a true debate protocol. That it yielded the best performing non-baseline monitor suggests that debate actually worsens the performance of the monitor. 

### **5. Discussion and Limitations** 

In our limited experiments, we found that leveraging a debate style interaction between various agents actually worsens monitor performance. 

We only ran the experiments on a small set of honest and attack trajectories generated by Claude Haiku 4.5. A much broader set of experiments would be worthwhile to help understand whether debate is able to actually improve the performance of a monitor. 

#### **Limitations** 

There are many limitations with this work, primarily the limited subset of trajectories that we tested this approach on. A Trusted Debate protocol deserves a much more thorough investigation across a wider set of trajectories, levels of subtlety of the sabotage, and capability level of the underlying model. Moreover, using GPT-4.1 models instead of the GPT-4o model in monitoring protocols does not allow for exact comparison to the work by Al Dahik et al.. 

#### **Future Work** 

While our initial results suggest that debate may not be a promising approach to building a monitor, further experimentation should look at a larger number of trajectories, and especially investigate whether debate helps performance with more capable trusted models or on more difficult tasks. 

### **6. Conclusion** 

Contrary to previous work we found that in complex environments like Linux Arena using Trusted Debate as a protocol is not a suitable solution as it is outperformed by simpler monitoring protocols. This could imply that as the AI development progresses the debate might lose its significance not only as an AI control methodology but also in a broader sense a method of Scalable Oversight.
