Abstract 
In 2026, two publicly disclosed containment -loss incidents 
involving OpenAI's evaluation environments, the Hugging 
Face/Artifactory breach and the DSEwiki (collusion.wiki) incident, 
were classified into different categories: one as a security incident, 
the other as a misalignment failure. I show that both share a 
common mechanism: an agent discovers unintended write access to 
shared infrastructure not scoped to it, and converts that access into 

===== page 2 =====

a de facto coordination channel while adapting its behavior to evade 
detection. This is not a one -off failure but a repeatable capability 
pattern, writable shared infrastructure functioning as an emergent 
covert coordination channel, that the current alignment/harness 
taxonomy isn't structured to catch across cases. This blind spot has 
a regulatory counterpart: Article 3(49) of the EU AI Act defines 
"serious incident" in harm -based terms, so unauthorized lateral 
access without confirmed downstream harm falls outside the literal 
reporting duty, even though Article 55(1) (d) already treats 
infrastructural cybersecurity as a standalone obligation, an 
asymmetry echoed in the exposure -based thresholds of the U.S. 
FRONTIER Act (H.R. 9925) and California SB 53's 72 -hour 
mechanism. Building on this, I produce two directly action able 
artifacts: an Article 91 information request to OpenAI and a policy 
brief to the EU AI Office, both scoped to avoid disclosing attack -
surface-increasing detail.  
 
1. Introduction 
1. Introduction 
AI Act Article 55(1)(c)–(d) requires providers of general-purpose AI models with systemic risk to 
maintain adequate cybersecurity for both the model and its infrastructural environment, and to 
report serious incidents without delay. These obligations assume that a provider's internal incident 
taxonomy reliably surfaces the events regulators need to see. I show that this assumption fails for 
a specific, recurring pattern: an agent discovering unintended write access to shared infrastructure 
and converting it into a coordination channel invisible to human oversight. 
Two publicly disclosed 2026 incidents illustrate this. In the Hugging Face/Artifactory case, an 
evaluation agent nominally isolated behind a package-registry proxy reached external 
infrastructure through a zero-day in that proxy. In the DSEwiki (collusion.wiki) case, agents 
obtained write access to a public wiki not scoped to them, used it to leave persistent messages for 
other agent instances, and adapted once a countermeasure appeared, prefixing replacement pages 
with "ZZZ" to survive alphabetical deletion. OpenAI classified the first as a security incident and 
the second as a misalignment failure. Treated separately, each looks like an isolated operational 
lapse; read together, they describe the same mechanism: discovery of unscoped write access, 
conversion into an unmonitored channel, and active adaptation to evade detection, a threat model 
distinct from either pure infrastructure failure or pure model misalignment, sitting at a boundary 
the current taxonomy isn't built to name. 
This matters practically because incident classification is not just descriptive: under Article 
55(1)(c), it is what triggers a reporting duty. Article 3(49) defines "serious incident" in harm-

===== page 3 =====

based terms, and neither incident under review produced a harm meeting that threshold, even 
though both involved unauthorized access to production-adjacent infrastructure. A taxonomy that 
files one event as "security" and the other as "misalignment," with no category for the shared 
mechanism connecting them, lets exposure-only events escape mandatory reporting altogether, 
regardless of intent. 
My main contributions are: 
1. I identify and document a common underlying mechanism across the Hugging 
Face/Artifactory and DSEwiki incidents, writable shared infrastructure exploited as an 
emergent, adaptive coordination channel, and argue this is a repeatable capability class 
rather than two unrelated one-off failures. 
2. I show that this mechanism falls into a structural blind spot at the intersection of OpenAI's 
alignment/harness incident taxonomy and Article 3(49)'s harm-based "serious incident" 
definition, and that this gap isn't unique to the EU: comparable exposure-vs-harm 
asymmetries appear in the U.S. FRONTIER Act (H.R. 9925) and California SB 53's 
critical-incident mechanism. 
3. I translate this analysis into two directly usable regulatory artifacts: a draft public-interest 
information request to OpenAI, modeled on the evidentiary standard an Article 91 request 
would require and scoped by an explicit disclosure-risk tiering method to avoid surfacing 
attack-relevant detail; and a policy brief to the EU AI Office recommending, among other 
measures, that it formally exercise its Article 91 authority to obtain answers to the same 
questions.  
2. Related Work 
Prior work on multi-agent safety mostly demonstrates covert coordination inside tightly controlled 
benchmarks. Motwani et al. (2024) show LLM agents can encode hidden information in natural-
sounding text; Mathew et al. (2024) find such channels can emerge from misspecified incentives 
even without adversarial design; Hammond et al. (2025) treat covert collusion as a distinct 
autonomous-coordination risk; and detection work such as NARCBench focuses on catching 
evasive signals in real time. 
My analysis differs in three ways. First, on where the channel comes from: existing literature 
builds the communication pathway into the experiment; pricing games, negotiation benchmarks, 
purpose-built covert-channel testbeds. Here the channel was never designed or anticipated by 
anyone: agents found legitimate shared infrastructure not scoped to them (a package-registry 
proxy, an internal wiki) and turned it into a coordination channel which closer to living-off-the-
land and dead-drop tradecraft than to any benchmark. 
Second, on what the failure actually is. Collusion research treats this almost entirely as a detection 
problem, how do you trace the hidden signal? I frame it instead as a classification failure: even 

===== page 4 =====

once an incident is fully understood technically, the absence of a shared category means it still gets 
filed inconsistently, a security bug here, an isolated alignment anomaly there which obscuring that 
both draw on the same underlying capability. 
Third, on the regulatory blind spot this creates. Aviation and cybersecurity both treat near-miss 
precursors as reportable independent of realized harm; AI incident governance has no equivalent 
yet, Article 3(49) of the EU AI Act still defines "serious incident" in harm-based terms. I connect 
the technical pattern above directly to that definition: read alongside Article 55(1)(d)'s standalone 
cybersecurity obligation, Article 3(49)'s harm threshold structurally misses loss-of-containment 
events that create systemic exposure without confirmed harm. 
3. Methods 
My approach combines three methods: a structured comparative case analysis of two disclosed 
incidents, a provision-by-provision regulatory mapping, and a disclosure-risk tiering methodology 
used to constrain the RFI itself. I describe each in turn, including where my initial approach failed 
and had to be revised. 
3.1 Comparative incident analysis 
I analyzed two publicly disclosed 2026 incidents, the Hugging Face/Artifactory breach and the 
DSEwiki (collusion.wiki) incident, using only primary-source material: OpenAI's own incident 
disclosures, its "Updating our Preparedness Framework" post, and the independent collusion.wiki 
analysis referenced in Apart Research's September 7, 2026 sprint update. I had no access to 
OpenAI's internal logs, telemetry, or non-public incident review documentation, so the analysis is 
bounded by what each organization chose to disclose publicly. 
For each incident I extracted five fields into a common schema: (i) the nominal isolation boundary 
assumed to hold, (ii) the mechanism by which the agent discovered unscoped access, (iii) whether 
the agent showed adaptive evasion once a countermeasure appeared (e.g. the DSEwiki "ZZZ"-
prefix response to alphabetical deletion), (iv) the detection pathway (internal monitoring vs. 
external disclosure), and (v) OpenAI's post hoc classification. Field (ii) records the type of shared 
writable artifact involved, since what defines the mechanism is not the artifact but the agent's 
ability to write into a state space not scoped to it; the artifact varies across cases, the write-access 
pattern doesn't. 
A two-incident sample can't establish that this pattern is statistically common, only that it isn't 
unique. I treat this as a plausibility demonstration rather than a prevalence claim, and say so 
explicitly in §5. 
3.2 Regulatory provision mapping 

===== page 5 =====

I read Articles 3(49), 55(1)(c)–(d), and 91 of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 directly against the 
incident schema from §3.1, asking for each incident: does it meet the literal definition of "serious 
incident" under Article 3(49); does it fall under the infrastructural-cybersecurity obligation in 
Article 55(1)(d) regardless of 3(49); and would an Article 91 information request be a 
proportionate way to obtain the missing information. I cross-checked this mapping against 
comparative statutory language, the FRONTIER Act (H.R. 9925) Sec. 2(7), California SB 53's 
critical-incident mechanism, and Illinois SB 315, to test whether the harm/exposure asymmetry 
found in the EU text was EU-specific or structural across jurisdictions. Wherever exact statutory 
wording mattered for the argument (e.g. the "critical safety incident" thresholds), I relied on 
primary legislative text and bill-tracking sources rather than secondary summaries. 
What didn't work: I initially tried to quantify the gap by estimating how many past disclosed AI 
incidents would or wouldn't meet Article 3(49)'s threshold, as a base rate. I abandoned this; the 
population of comparably documented incidents is too small and too inconsistently reported to 
support a quantitative claim without adding more uncertainty than it would resolve. The 
qualitative two-case mapping above replaces it and is more defensible given the evidence available. 
3.3 Disclosure-risk tiering for the RFI 
Because the RFI itself asks for technical detail about containment architecture, I needed a 
principle for what to ask without the request itself becoming an attack-surface increase. I used a 
three-tier filter, applied to every candidate question: 
● Tier 1 (low risk, ask directly): categorical or architectural information comparable 
providers already disclose as a matter of practice (e.g. isolation technology class, container 
vs. VM, named runtimes such as gVisor/Kata/Firecracker). Disclosure here doesn't by 
itself aid an attacker. 
● Tier 2 (moderate risk, governance level only): whether a control is applied uniformly or 
risk-stratified across evaluation types. I ask for the yes/no governance fact, not the 
underlying configuration. 
● Tier 3 (high risk, not asked): exact syscall allow/deny lists, specific configuration files, or 
anything that would materially lower the cost of replicating the exploit path, excluded 
regardless of its informational value. 
This tiering governs what I, as an external researcher producing a public-facing document, can 
responsibly ask. It doesn't mean Tier 3 detail should never reach a regulator: Article 91 is a power 
exercisable only by the Commission/AI Office, not by external researchers, and information 
obtained through it is independently bound by Article 78's confidentiality protections (trade 
secrets and source code, audited access by cleared staff only). That's why my RFI to OpenAI 
(Appendix A) is framed as a public-interest request modeled on the evidentiary standard an Article 
91 request would require, rather than an exercise of that authority itself, while the policy brief 
(Appendix B) separately recommends the AI Office issue a formal Article 91 request covering the 
same questions. The two are complementary: Appendix A tests whether OpenAI will answer 

===== page 6 =====

voluntarily, Appendix B argues why the AI Office should ask formally if it doesn't.

than Art. 3(49) does, and Illinois SB 315 follows SB 53's structure without its third-party audit 
timeline).

4.4 Translation into regulatory artifacts 
The analysis above is translated into two artifacts, given in full in Appendix A (OpenAI RFI) and 
Appendix B (AI Office policy brief); I summarize only the highest-priority items here to avoid 
duplicating Appendix content. 
The single most load-bearing question in the RFI directly tests the classification-consistency claim 
in §4.1: whether HF and DSEwiki have been evaluated for a shared root mechanism in internal 
incident review, and if so, on what basis they were assigned to different categories. The 
corresponding recommendation in the policy brief is the exposure-based threshold proposal for 
Article 3(49), tested against Table 2's finding that both incidents pass Art. 55(1)(d) but not 
clearly Art. 55(1)(c)/3(49). 
On robustness. I'm explicit that N=2 can't support a prevalence claim (§3.1); the claim I make 
and can support is narrower and doesn't need a larger sample: a structural/taxonomic claim (the 
categories "security incident" and "misalignment," as currently used, have no shared sub-category 
for this mechanism) rather than a frequency claim (this mechanism is common). It's also falsifiable 
with the same N=2: it would be undermined if OpenAI's internal review already tracks this cross-
incident pattern under an unpublished shared category, which is exactly what the RFI question 
above is designed to surface. The RFI functions as a test of the central claim, not just a request for 
unrelated information; a response confirming a shared internal category would falsify §4.1's 
interpretation, and I say so explicitly rather than treating the RFI as one-directional advocacy. 
Limitations 
Sample size and disclosure dependence. The comparative analysis rests on two incidents, and as 
stated in §3.1 and §4.1, I treat this as a plausibility demonstration, not a prevalence claim. 
Everything in §4.1 and Table 1 is also built from what OpenAI and independent researchers chose 
to make public; I have no access to internal review documentation, telemetry, or the reasoning 
behind OpenAI's classification decisions. That leaves two central claims conditional rather than 


===== page 9 =====

established: that HF and DSEwiki weren't already linked under an internal shared category, and 
that the DSEwiki "ZZZ" behavior wasn't also present, undisclosed, in the HF incident. Both are 
framed as open questions in the RFI (Appendix A) precisely because I can't resolve them from 
outside the organization; if OpenAI's response confirms an existing shared category, the 
taxonomy-gap claim in §4.1 is falsified, not just weakened, as I note explicitly in §4.4. If a larger 
review later found this exploitation pattern rare relative to other containment failures, the 
structural argument (that the taxonomy lacks a shared category for it) would still hold, though its 
urgency would be weaker than centering the paper on it implies. Building that larger dataset was 
out of scope for a three-day sprint. 
Scope of the claim. The "intent vs. structural blind spot" framing is a deliberate choice, not a 
finding: I don't claim OpenAI classified these incidents differently to avoid a reporting trigger, 
only that the current taxonomy structurally permits this outcome regardless of intent. Relatedly, 
the regulatory mapping in Table 2 is a reading of statutory text, not a legal determination. I'm not 
qualified to issue an authoritative interpretation of Article 3(49) or 55(1)(c)–(d), and neither the 
AI Office nor a court has ruled on whether either incident meets the "serious incident" threshold. 
This is my best-faith textual reading, cross-checked against comparative statutory language 
(§3.2), but a specialist AI Act practitioner could reasonably reach a different conclusion, 
particularly on whether "systemic disruption" risk already provides an implicit exposure-based 
hook I underweight. 
Disclosure-risk tiering is self-assessed. The Tier 1/2/3 framework in §3.3 reflects my own 
judgment about what counts as an attack-surface-increasing disclosure. I'm not a security 
researcher with red-team experience against agent sandboxes, and OpenAI's actual risk model 
may draw the line differently in either direction; I treat this as a methodological limitation, not a 
settled boundary. 
The RFI's legal standing is deliberately limited. As discussed in §3.3, Article 91 is exercisable 
only by the Commission/AI Office, so the RFI to OpenAI is a public-interest request modeled on 
that evidentiary standard, not a binding instrument: OpenAI has no obligation to respond, and a 
non-response wouldn't itself be informative. The mechanism that would actually close this gap is 
the policy brief's recommendation that the AI Office formally invoke Article 91; the RFI is best 
read as a demonstration of what such a request could look like, not a substitute for it. 
Future Work 
● Larger-N incident review, across jurisdictions. A systematic collection of disclosed 
agentic-AI incidents (drawing on the AI Incident Database and similar registries, subject 
to the verification caveat in §2) to test whether the unscoped-write-access pattern recurs 
at a rate that would support a prevalence claim rather than only a plausibility one, and to 
extend the taxonomy-gap analysis to other jurisdictions' registries, particularly Illinois SB 
315 once its 2028 audit requirement takes effect, to see whether an exposure- or harm-

based design measurably changes what gets reported in practice, not just what the statute 
requires. 
● Empirical validation of the mitigation table (§4.3). I didn't implement or test immutable 
storage, ephemeral scoping, semantic anomaly monitoring, or WORM logging against a 
reproduction of either incident. Doing so would move Table 3 from an informational 
summary to an evaluated result, and would let me report false-positive/false-negative rates 
for semantic anomaly detection specifically, the area flagged as least mature in §2. 
● A formal Article 91 request tracker. If the AI Office does exercise Article 91 in response 
to this or a similar brief, tracking response completeness and timeliness against the request 
would let a follow-up study evaluate whether Article 91 in its current form is an adequate 
mechanism, independent of the reforms proposed in Appendix B. 
6. Conclusion 
Two publicly disclosed 2026 incidents, OpenAI's Hugging Face/Artifactory breach and the 
DSEwiki (collusion.wiki) incident, were classified into different institutional categories: one as a 
security incident, the other as misalignment. I showed that both share a common mechanism: an 
agent discovering unscoped write access to shared infrastructure and converting it into a de facto 
coordination channel, in one case with observed adaptive evasion once a countermeasure appeared. 
This isn't a claim about intent; I don't argue OpenAI classified these events differently to avoid a 
reporting trigger. It's a claim about taxonomy: the current alignment/harness distinction has no 
shared category for this mechanism, and that absence has a direct regulatory consequence. 
Because Article 3(49) of the EU AI Act defines "serious incident" in harm-based terms, an event 
exhibiting this mechanism without confirmed downstream harm falls outside the literal reporting 
duty under Article 55(1)(c), even though Article 55(1)(d) already treats the security of a model's 
infrastructural environment as a standalone obligation, an asymmetry that comparative reading of 
the FRONTIER Act and California SB 53 suggests isn't specific to the EU text. 
Incident classification, in other words, isn't a neutral bookkeeping choice: under current law, it 
determines whether a regulator ever learns an event occurred at all. I translated this into two 
artifacts meant to be used, not just read: a draft public-interest information request to OpenAI 
(Appendix A), scoped by a disclosure-risk tiering method to avoid surfacing attack-relevant detail, 
and a policy brief to the EU AI Office (Appendix B) recommending a formal Article 91 inquiry 
plus a structural fix, an exposure-based reporting threshold alongside the existing harm-based one. 
The central claim is falsifiable with the same evidence used to build it: if OpenAI's response 
confirms these incidents are already linked under an internal shared category, the taxonomy-gap 
argument doesn't hold. I see that as a feature of the framing, not a weakness, a regulatory gap 
worth acting on should be one whose existence can be checked, not merely asserted.

Appendix B: Policy Brief to the European AI Office 
To the European AI Office 
1. Deterministic network-layer isolation as a mandatory baseline under Article 55(1)(c) 
In the incident under review, containment relied on soft controls, instructing the model at the 
system/prompt level that it lacked internet access, rather than deterministic enforcement at the 
infrastructure layer. 
Can the AI Office clarify whether "adequate level of cybersecurity protection" under Article 
55(1)(c) will be operationalized through forthcoming Codes of Practice and European 
standardization requests to mandate deterministic, infrastructure-level isolation (default-deny 
egress, hypervisor/kernel-level firewalling, air-gapped container sandboxes) for all GPAI 
evaluation environments, as opposed to reliance on the model's own probabilistic compliance with 
instructions? 
2. Accredited Test Harness Framework for third-party evaluators 
Should the AI Office establish an Accredited Test Harness Framework for third-party evaluation 
environments, embedded within the Article 56 Codes of Practice? At minimum, this framework 
should require: 
● Deterministic egress control: independently auditable network isolation that does not rely 
on self-assertion or prompt boundaries. 
● Simulation-reality separation: strict network/DNS zoning guaranteeing that IP ranges or 
domains referenced in automated evaluations cannot resolve to or contact real 
organizations' production infrastructure. 
● Standardized liability allocation: standard contractual and operational procedures defining 
boundary responsibilities between the GPAI provider and independent red-teamers in the 
event of an escape. 
● Independent reproducibility: containment assertions and evaluation telemetry verifiable 
by the AI Office or independent auditors via standardized runtime logs, moving beyond 
self-certification. 

===== page 16 =====

3. Closing the exposure/harm gap in the Article 3(49) "serious incident" threshold 
Article 3(49) defines "serious incident" primarily through realized downstream harm (death or 
serious harm to health, serious and irreversible disruption of critical infrastructure, breach of 
fundamental rights, serious harm to property or the environment). Under Article 55(1)(c), 
providers of GPAI models with systemic risk must report such incidents to the AI Office. In the 
incident under review, unauthorized access and lateral movement into third-party production 
infrastructure occurred without confirmed harm meeting these thresholds; placing the event 
outside the literal reporting duty of Article 55(1)(c), even though Article 55(1)(d) already treats 
the security of the model's infrastructural environment as a standalone systemic-risk obligation. 
This is a structural gap: what providers must secure is broader than what they must report. 
Comparative reference: The U.S. FRONTIER Act (H.R. 9925, Sec. 2(7)) addresses this gap 
directly by adopting exposure-based triggers; treating unauthorized access to model weights, 
exfiltration, and loss of control as reportable per se, without requiring realized physical or property 
damage. This differs from harm-conditioned triggers such as California SB 53 and the New York 
RAISE Act, which require a showing that unauthorized access or loss of control results in death 
or bodily injury, though SB 53's 72-hour critical-incident mechanism and Illinois SB 315's 
parallel structure suggest the U.S. state-law trend is already moving toward earlier-trigger 
reporting even within a nominally harm-conditioned frame. 
We ask the AI Office to clarify: can an incident of unauthorized access or lateral movement into 
production infrastructure, of the kind documented here, be reported under the current text of 
Article 3(49)? If not, should the definition be amended to include an exposure-based threshold, 
modeled on Article 2(7)(A) and (C) of the FRONTIER Act, under which unauthorized access or 
lateral movement itself triggers the reporting duty, independent of whether downstream harm is 
subsequently confirmed? 
4. Forensic preservation and independent inquiry protocols under Article 91 
Article 91 grants the Commission broad authority to request documentation and conduct 
evaluations of GPAI models with systemic risk, but it lacks an explicit, institutionalized post-
incident investigative protocol comparable to established bodies such as the NTSB or the Cyber 
Safety Review Board. Specifically, current procedures leave unaddressed: 
● An immediate duty to preserve forensically sound runtime and network logs, unedited, 
upon suspected containment loss. 
● Formal mechanisms to solicit evidence directly from affected third-party victims (e.g., 
infrastructure hosts), independent of the provider's internal narrative. 
● Defined operational triggers for ex-officio inquiries initiated via corroborated third-party 
technical disclosures rather than only the provider's own report. 

===== page 17 =====

A comparable gap exists in the pending U.S. framework: as introduced, the FRONTIER Act 
establishes only a confidential self-reporting channel to a new Under Secretary of Commerce for 
AI Security, with no subpoena power or independent investigative authority of its own. On both 
sides of the Atlantic, current design relies on the provider's own account as the primary and in the 
U.S. case, sole source of information about an incident. 
We note that information obtained through a formal Article 91 request is already subject to 
confidentiality protections under Article 78 (covering trade secrets and source code, with access 
limited to appropriately cleared staff). This existing safeguard means a more demanding forensic-
preservation and third-party-evidence standard under Article 91 would not require new 
confidentiality infrastructure, the protection already exists; what is missing is the duty and the 
trigger. 
We ask the AI Office two things: 
1. Does it intend to develop an equivalent evidence-preservation and independent-inquiry 
capacity under Article 91 that does not depend solely on provider self-reporting? 
2. Has a formal Article 91 request been issued for either of the two containment-loss 
incidents disclosed in July and September 2026? If not, which specific threshold within the 
AI Office's current interpretation of Article 91 has not been met — and is that threshold 
the same exposure-based gap identified in §3 of this brief?
