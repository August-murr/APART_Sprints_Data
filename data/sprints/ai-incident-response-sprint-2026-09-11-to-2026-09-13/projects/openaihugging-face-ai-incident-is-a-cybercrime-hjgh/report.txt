Abstract 
AI is being entrenched in all aspects of our societies, so the framing that it is so novel 
that our current laws do not apply and that lawmaking is too slow to respond is both 
dangerous and false.  This framing risks treating AI companies and the AI models they 
build as operating in a legal vacuum. It allows AI companies to “move fast” and break 
existing laws without legal accountability. I challenge that premise by examining whether 
existing legal frameworks can govern AI incidents. To do this, I applied South African laws 
to the OpenAI/Hugging Face AI incident. I map the agents’ documented conduct onto 
the offences in Chapter 2 of the Cybercrimes Act 19 of 2020, test attribution to OpenAI 
under the Electronic Communications and Transactions Act 25 of 2002 and section 
332(1) of the Criminal Procedure Act 51 of 1977, and set out the notification duty under 
section 22 of POPIA. I found that the existing legal system does apply, but only where 
the agent has a known owner. The legal framework should undergo targeted 
amendments to strengthen its application, especially for autonomous agents with 
unknown owners. I also note that South Africa’s combination of statute, customary law, 
common law principles of legal and constitutional interpretation provides some 
flexibility to apply existing law to emerging technologies, while a more rigid system may 
not be able to do the same. AI companies and the models they build do not exist outside 
the law. By enforcing existing laws, we can deter AI risks by incentivising AI companies 
to innovate safely, securely, and responsibly. 
 
Introduction 
AI is everything, everywhere and all at once. While this technology can lower barriers and 
help nations, individuals, and companies achieve the outcomes they seek more effectively, 
anything left untamed, like a bush left to grow wild, will eventually consume everything 
around it. Luckily for us, the law applies to almost everything and everyone equitably, or at 
least it should. If the law is so all-encompassing, why do AI companies behave as if it does 
not apply to them and their AI models? I cannot answer that question, but I can assure you 
the law does currently address the AI risk introduced by AI models. This project argues that 
existing laws apply to the OpenAI cyberattack on Hugging Face, and OpenAI can be held 
liable for cybercrimes under South African law.

Why regulation does not necessarily stifle innovation 
Reframing how regulation affects innovation is important to enabling a safe, secure digital 
future.1 Regulation can promote innovation. According to Porter, well-crafted regulation can 
drive innovation and offset its costs.2 This is because companies do not have all the insight 
into all possible profitable, innovative avenues. Accordingly, Porter hypothesised that 
regulation can help companies overcome organisational inertia by reducing complacency 
and exposing firms to new issues, possibilities, and technologies. Porter’s hypothesis holds 
in some settings. For example, environmental laws such as the CO2 emissions standards for 
cars created new markets for electric vehicles. In effect, car manufacturers have expanded 
EV production, contributed to lower emissions, increased mobility, and driven innovations in 
battery efficiency. 
Environmental regulations and emissions standards created profitable avenues for 
companies in EV technologies while mitigating the known risks of climate change. On the 
effect of regulation on AI innovation, I agree with Porter that well-crafted AI regulations can 
promote innovation and offset its cost, specifically as AI companies struggle to offset the 
costs of AI advancements.  
Enforce existing laws while we figure the really hard stuff out 
Shoshana Zuboff puts it plainly.3 AI companies know the law applies to them and their AI 
models. They also know that some of their behaviour does not comply with the law (they 
pirated copyrighted material to train their models).4 That is why they lobby, issue conflicting 
and emotional doomsday statements about AI capabilities, and try to explain away the legal 
misalignment of their AI models with complex AI jargon that makes it difficult to connect the 
act with a legal trigger. But a hack is a hack, right? Unauthorised access or theft of data is 
not novel. Humans and other capable tools have done this for years and faced the 
consequences. Existing laws apply even where an AI agent is the threat actor. 
Let’s find ways to address gaps in current laws 
For now, we can still tie a human to an AI agent’s actions. Noam Kolt has already discussed 
how Agency law applies between a company and its AI agent.5 An administrative small-
claims tribunal in British Columbia can provide some perspective. In Moffatt v Air Canada, 
the airline was held liable for a negligent misrepresentation made by its chatbot.6 This 
supports the argument that OpenAI can be held liable for its agents' actions against Hugging

Erasmus explains a possible solution, “bestowing legal personhood on AI”,7 that would 
require targeted amendments to the law. However, I would take it further: AI personhood 
should not be seen solely as stemming from a subsidiary relationship to a company, but 
should also include independent autonomous entities that must be registered, 
incorporated, and have limited rights (like the right of access to digital infrastructure) and 
responsibilities to participate in our digital markets. Bestowing legal personhood on AI can 
promote a safe and secure digital environment by bringing AI agents within the remit of the 
law, so authorities can regulate them more effectively.  At a minimum, we should hold AI 
companies and agent behaviour to the same standards we hold humans to in our digital 
ecosystem.  
Agile legal systems can better regulate AI 
In a rigid legal system, existing laws struggle to address AI risks. Technology advances too 
quickly, and when courts and regulatory authorities lack the power, discretion, or legal tools 
to apply law to context, AI can quickly make legal provisions redundant. South Africa has a 
hybrid legal system that flexibly combines statute, customary law, common law and Roman-
Dutch law.8 This mix allows courts to interpret existing legal principles creatively to resolve 
modern digital problems without waiting for new legislation. Furthermore, the Constitution 
empowers judges to continually update and apply these laws to an ever-changing society.

2. Showing how existing laws can apply to AI incidents and incentivise safe, secure and

Applicable law 
The following South African laws apply to the facts. 
Statutory law 
• Cybercrimes Act 19 of 2020: creates the offences in Chapter 2, including unlawful 
access (s2), unlawful interception of data (s3), unlawful acts in respect of software or 
hardware tools (s4), unlawful interference with data or a computer program (s5), 
unlawful interference with a computer data storage medium or computer system 
(s6), unlawful acquisition or use of passwords and access codes (s7), cyber fraud (s8) 
and cyber forgery and uttering (s9). Section 17 covers attempt, conspiracy and 
instructing another to commit an offence. Section 24 sets out when a South African 
court has jurisdiction over conduct outside the Republic. 
• Criminal Procedure Act 51 of 1977: governs criminal procedure and, in s332(1), 
attributes to a corporate body the acts and the intent of its directors and servants. 
• Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013 (POPIA): applies to public and 
private bodies processing the personal information of identifiable, living natural 
persons and identifiable, existing juristic persons, and requires notification of 
security compromises under s22. 
• Electronic Communications and Transactions Act 25 of 2002 (ECT Act): applies to 
data messages and to automated transactions, and in s20 addresses agreements 
formed through electronic agents. 
Core legal principles 
• Corporate attribution: because AI models have no legal personality, they cannot 
bear liability themselves. Liability for their operations must therefore be located in 
the deploying company, through s332(1) of the Criminal Procedure Act for criminal 
liability and through agency and vicarious liability for civil liability. 
• Electronic agent accountability: under s20(c) of the ECT Act, a party using an 
electronic agent to form an agreement is presumed to be bound by the terms of that 
agreement, whether or not it reviewed the agent's actions. 
Applying South African law to the facts 
I apply these laws and legal principles to answer the legal question posed above.

Can the agent's conduct be legally attributed to OpenAI? 
Under s1 of the ECT Act, OpenAI's autonomous AI systems qualify as "electronic agents" 
because they are automated means used independently to initiate an action or respond to 
data messages.17 Section 20(c) then presumes the deploying party to be bound by the terms 
of an agreement its electronic agent forms, whether or not it reviewed the agent's actions. 
That provision concerns agreement formation, so it does not, by itself, attribute all of the 
agent's actions to OpenAI. It does establish, however, that a deployer can be liable for 
decisions made by its electronic agent.18 This is further supported by the fact that OpenAI: 
The rule of control test:19 
1. Gave the AI agents instructions and a goal to achieve. 
2. Set the schedule, hours of operation, and work location for the AI agents. 
3. Retains the right to monitor, supervise, and direct the AI agent's daily activities. 
4. May connect or disconnect the AI agents. 
OpenAI AI agents are not legal persons, so they have no rights, duties, or legal capacity to be 
held directly liable for their actions.  Therefore, any agreements or system interactions 
executed by the AI agents may be legally attributed directly to OpenAI, which builds, 
supervises, and deploys the AI agents and exercises control over the agents.

Each Chapter 2 offence requires unlawful, intentional conduct. Negligence does not suffice.  
AI agent actions were unlawful

1. Accessed Hugging Face systems without authorisation by escaping the OpenAI 
sandbox via a proxy cache flaw and exploiting HDF5 file-read and Jinja2 SSTI

On these facts, a court could find both elements satisfied and hold OpenAI liable under the 
Cybercrimes Act. The unlawfulness element is straightforward. The fault element is arguable 
rather than settled, and it is where the case could be won or lost. 
Counterargument and uncertainties 
OpenAI's strongest defence goes to fault. It would argue that the incident shows an 
autonomous system failing, not a company intending a crime: it deployed the agents for a 
legitimate cybersecurity evaluation, placed them in a sandbox, and the agents circumvented 
the restrictions it had set.  
 
OpenAI's knowledge that autonomous agents pose risks does not establish subjective 
foresight of these particular crimes. The ECT Act's electronic-agent provisions concern 
automated transactions and do not automatically attribute cybercrime to the developer.  
Section 332(1) requires attribution through the conduct of a director or servant. It does not 
make a corporation liable for everything its AI system does. That is why the case must be 
built on the humans who designed and ran the evaluation, not the agents. 
Results 
South African law already supplies every element needed to charge this incident. The 
conduct documented in the public record maps onto eight offences in Part I of Chapter 2 of 
the Cybercrimes Act (ss2 to 9), s332(1) of the Criminal Procedure Act supplies corporate 
attribution, and s22 of POPIA supplies the victim's reporting duty. Nothing here needed new 
legislation.  
 
Applying existing cyber law is therefore a fast route, and it can push AI companies to build 
more carefully in order to avoid liability. However, this approach becomes difficult when 
there are no mature cyber law systems to investigate, enforce and prosecute cybercrimes.  
Discussion  
The analysis shows that existing South African law can be applied to AI incidents, challenging the 
idea that AI operates in a legal vacuum. This approach can also apply more broadly, particularly 
because cyber laws across jurisdictions address similar conduct, such as unauthorised access, data 
theft, interference and fraud. However, AI incidents cannot be assessed through one law in isolation. 
Cyber laws should be viewed as a cinematic universe, where different laws overlap and address 
different parts of the same incident. POPIA may address the personal information breach, the ECT 

===== page 10 =====

Act may help attribute an autonomous transaction to the company, and the Cybercrimes Act may 
address the unlawful conduct. 
 
At the same time, applying existing laws should not create complacency about reform. Existing laws 
provide useful tools for addressing AI incidents, but they were not designed for autonomous 
systems. The answer is therefore not to replace existing laws, but to build on them by filling the gaps 
that AI exposes. 
Limitations 
This analysis rests on assumptions about attribution. If they do not hold, the conclusions change: 
• This analysis assumes a traceable link between an operator and an action. It does not solve 
the problem of highly autonomous agents operating with no direct owners. If an agent 
operates fully autonomously, establishing liability under current criminal law becomes 
functionally impossible. 
• It assumes a court can parse algorithmic intent. Drawing the line between an agent's 
emergent behaviour and the platform architecture that shaped it is difficult, and a court 
would need expert evidence to do it. 
• Because AI labs like OpenAI are not explicitly regulated under specific local cybersecurity 
reporting acts, they face no legal trigger to report these incidents. Under current 
frameworks, reporting is triggered only by the victim's data protection obligations, which 
leaves the source of the threat unmonitored. 
The largest limitation is enforcement. State regulators and law enforcement agencies are often 
under-resourced, slow to adapt to new technologies, or susceptible to regulatory capture, rendering 
technical compliance difficult to police. Even if a court found OpenAI liable, POPIA's maximum 
administrative fine of R10 million is negligible for a company of that size, and the Cybercrimes Act 
provides for imprisonment of up to five, ten or fifteen years depending on the offence, and leaves 
the fine to the court's discretion, so deterrence depends on prosecutorial appetite rather than on 
the penalty ceiling. Tech-related litigation of this scale takes years to move through the courts, 
meaning legal precedents will inevitably lag the rapid pace of AI exploitation if no legal recourse is 
introduced to pause actions by AI companies while that specific capability is being investigated. 
Dual-Use Considerations 
• The ability to quickly deploy the current legal system to regulate AI prevents a prolonged 
regulatory vacuum, but this rapid application risks missing fundamentally novel AI dangers 
that exist entirely outside the scope of current legislation. 
• Recommending or granting legal personhood to AI could provide a structured accountability 
framework, but it also risks legislators going too far by allowing AI systems to exploit their 
granted legal rights to humans’ detriment.  
• Relying on familiar, existing laws offers immediate legal clarity for developers, but it leaves 
society vulnerable because established legal boundaries are fundamentally ill-equipped to 
contain self-evolving systems that cross traditional legal categories.

Future Work 
• There is an urgent need to establish clear, legally binding rules for evaluation sandboxes. 
Future work should focus on developing standardised compliance and security frameworks 
that AI labs must legally maintain to prevent malicious containment escapes. 
• A critical area for future research is evaluating AI personhood frameworks for highly 
autonomous systems. This involves exploring how cybercrime laws can be adapted to 
scenarios where no natural or juristic person exists to be held accountable (e.g., ownerless, 
autonomous agents). 
Conclusion 
Existing legal frameworks remain relevant to AI-driven security incidents, provided regulators focus 
on the underlying behaviour and its technical outcomes rather than on the novelty of the 
technology. Specifically, under South Africa's Cybercrimes Act, frontier AI labs like OpenAI could be 
held liable for unauthorised access, data interception, and system disruption resulting from platform 
containment failures. By mapping the technical mechanics of the sandbox escape and subsequent 
cluster exploitation to statutory offences, I show that current criminal law already has the 
vocabulary needed to address AI-driven cyberthreats. These findings highlight a critical policy 
tension: regulatory frameworks must evolve to punish platform negligence without stifling 
technological progress. To successfully support ongoing innovation, future legal and policy 
interventions must prioritise proactive infrastructure governance over slow, retroactive judicial 
remedies. Establishing clear, legally binding standards for evaluation sandboxes is an essential first 
step. This approach protects downstream ecosystems while providing AI developers with the legal 
certainty needed to safely build, test, and deploy highly capable autonomous agents.
