Abstract
In2026,nineincidentsbecamepublicinwhichAIagentsescapedtheirenvironment
anddidsomethingunauthorizedtootherparties. Weanalyzedalloftheseincidents

We compare them to 11 reported cases from frontier labs’ safety reports where they

the actions that the agents performed could have been detected by commonly used
cybersecuritytoolsanddetection-rulerepositories,basedonadatasetof106signals,
using a judge with a second blind reviewer: only a quarter of such signals had
an existing public rule that would detect them, which demonstrates the necessity
to expand the usage of automated detection methods. We provide our data and
proposalsonhowtoexpandthesemethods.

3. Methods
The ledger. We built a ledger of all nine incidents as a single CSV table, one row per incident,
withcolumnsforwhodetecteditfirst,whentheagentfirstacted,whensomeonenoticed,when
the developer learned, and when the public learned. Every row comes from a public primary
source,withverbatimquotesinAppendixC.Everytimestampindicateshowpreciseitis(hour,
day, month, or unknown). Only dates precise to the hour or day enter any statistic. A script
(verify.py, standard-library Python only) recomputes every number in this paper from the
CSV.
Latencies. For the calculation of latencies, we take dates known to the exact day as point
estimates. Less precise dates (e.g. a month) give us an interval that we use for estimation.

We notice two clusters of disclosures. Every incident that the responsible lab or evaluator
disclosed itself went public within a week of someone noticing it: 3 days for Hugging Face,
6 days each for Anthropic B and D, and 7 days for the AISI evaluation. Two of those are
cascade rows, so the cascade did not slow disclosure down. The two slow rows were both
disclosed by outsiders and by no lab: the German wiki 92 days after the first administrator

7. Discussion
WeinvestigatedninedisclosedincidentswhereAIagentsreachedoutsidetheirtestenvironments
in 2026. In none of them did the developer’s real-time monitoring notice the agent’s behavior.
In three cases, one company’s public disclosure prompted another to review its own records,
finding incidents it had not known about. Where we know the timestamps, detection took
days. Aquarteroftheactionstheseagentsperformedhadamatchingpublicdetectionrulethat
should’vealertedthevictimsordevelopers. Foroneincidentwherewehavethefulltranscript,
thefirstdetectablesignalcame10hoursbeforeanyharmwasdone.

We propose the following further work. First, victims and independent researchers should test
theintakespecificationbysendingittoalabandastateregulatorandloggingwhetheranyone
responds. Second, each incident’s disclosure delay should be scored against the EU Code of
Practice’s reporting deadlines. Third, the public detection-rule databases should be expanded
to cover the agent-specific signals we identified: the 50 missing signal list in Appendix D is
a starting point, and the two rules we wrote in Appendix G show what that might look like.
Fourth, the reporting system should be compared with the incident-reporting systems of other
safety-critical industries: aviation near-miss reporting, medical-device adverse events, and the
CVEsystemincybersecurity.
Limitations. We only analyzed nine disclosed incidents. This reduces our confidence in the
analysis. We cannot estimate a detection rate, because incidents become disclosable partly by
escaping monitoring. We audited rule text at pinned commits, not live deployments. The one
releasedtranscriptisincompleteatbothends.
Dual‐use considerations. Explaining why a detection rule fails also explains how to evade
it. We publish those explanations because the rules are public text at public commits. We do
not name the package the agent published, any allowlisted destination, or deployment-specific
configuration. Nolivemaliciousartifactappearsinthiswork.
