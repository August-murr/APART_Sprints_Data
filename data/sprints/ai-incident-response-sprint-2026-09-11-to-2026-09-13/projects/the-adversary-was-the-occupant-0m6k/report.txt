The Adversary Was The Occupant 
Reading the OpenAI and Hugging Face incident through IEC 62443, with the direction of threat 
reversed 
 
 
 
Srinivas Raayi 
Independent 
With 
Apart Research 
Abstract 
Everyone who has written seriously about the July 2026 incident agrees 
one thing - OpenAI’s evaluation sandbox should have been built on the 
assumption that it would be escaped. That point is correct and it has been 
made well by others. What nobody has offered is a method an evaluation 
team can use to find the escape paths before the agents find them. 
Industrial control has had one for over a decade. IEC 62443 requires you 
to divide a system into zone s, name every communication path between 
zones as a conduit, and set a target security level for each conduit against 
the adversary it will actually face. 
I apply that method to OpenAI’s evaluation environment using the 
timeline OpenAI published on 26 August 2026, with one deliberate 
change. In 62443 the adversary sits outside the zone. Here the adversary 
(the agent) was sitting inside the zone. Three conduits fall out of the 
exercise. Only one, package install, appears in the public record as a 
deliberately managed sandbox boundary. The other two emerge with the 
Artifactory’s connectivity and do not appear to have been treated in the 
published accounts as potential escape paths from the sandbox. I derive 
twelve checks from the conduit model, each answerable from a 
configuration export rather than a live test, sort them by effort, and run 
them against the record. Seven of the thirteen dated events on record fall 
before any third party was touched, and the checks reach all seven. Three 
were interrupted, two detected, one limited, and one forced to a decision. 
The July 8 rebuild is the strong evidence for the approach where OpenAI 
hardened the service and lost it again the same day, because hardening 
treats a conduit as a component and the standard treats it as a path.

1. Introduction 
An environment that evaluates a frontier model for offensive cyber capability has to be cut off from the rest 
of the world, and one that is genuinely cut off cannot install a package. So every real deployment leaves a 
few paths open, and the list is much the same everywhere – a package service, a container registry, 
telemetry out, a secrets service etc. These paths are designed, documented and signed off. They are also the 
only ways out, and in July 2026 one of them was used. OpenAI’s models, running an internal cyber 
capability evaluation and production safeguards switched off, escaped through Artifactory, the internally 
hosted package manager that was their sanctioned route to install software. From there they reached the 
internet, compromised a third-party application, broke into Hugging Face production, and came back to take 
administrator control of an OpenAI research cluster [1,2]. 
 
The lesson most people took is that the sandbox should have been designed on the assumption it would fail. 
That is right and it was said within days of the disclosure [5,6,7]. I am not going to argue with it. My 
concern is that the point stops where the work starts. Telling an evaluation team to assume compromise 
does not tell them which service to look at, what to ask about it, or how to know when they have asked 
enough. 
 
That gap has been solved before, in a field with a similar problem. Industrial control systems run processes 
that are dangerous if they get out, on equipment that cannot simply be patched, connected through a handful 
of deliberate paths. The standard that governs them, IEC 62443, focuses on security boundaries as 
explicitly modeled conduits rather than treating perimeter hardening alone as sufficient. It talks about zones 
and conduits, and it insists every conduit be assessed on its own terms against a stated adversary. It 
transfers to an evaluation sandbox with one change. 
 
In 62443, the thing inside the zone is trusted and the threat comes from outside. In an evaluation sandbox 
the thing inside the zone is the threat. I argue that reversing the direction allows the same machinery to give 
us a defensible answer about what the conduits need to withstand. 
 
My main contributions are: 
1. A zone and conduit model of OpenAI’s evaluation environment built from the published record, 
which identifies three conduits out of the sandbox, only one of which is explicitly described in the 
public record as a sandbox boundary. (section 4.1) 
2. A statement of the security level mismatch – the conduits were implicitly built to withstand a 
misbehaving script and were asked to withstand an adversary that chained zero-days for two 
months (section 4.2) 
3. Twelve checks derived from the conduit model, each traceable to a clause of IEC 62443, mostly 
answerable from configuration rather than a lab, and sorted by effort so a team knows where to 
start (section 4.3) 
4. A backtest of those checks against all thirteen dated events on OpenAI’s timeline, with an honest 
account of the four events they cannot reach (section 4.4).

2. Related Work 
The primary record for this paper is OpenAI’s incident report, Hugging Face’s technical timeline, and the 
METR/Redwood [3] independent investigation. METR focused primarily on agent behaviour during July 7-
13, whereas this paper focuses on the evaluation environment and the May-June precursor events. 
 
Practitioner analysis arrived fast. CSA [4] called for treating evaluated agents as capable insider 
adversaries and enforcing stronger egress controls. Other commentaries argued that sandboxes should be 
assumed compromisable and identified internet connected package services as escape paths [7,8]. Existing 
frameworks such as the CSA AI controls matrix [9] and CoSAI [10] incident response framework define 
objectives but do not provide a conduit-based assessment method. This paper applies the IEC 62443 zone 
and conduit methodology to fill that gap. 
 
3. Methods 
3.1 What is IEC 62443? 
IEC 62443 is the international standard series for securing industrial automation and control systems 
(power, water, manufacturing, anything where software drives a physical process). It was developed by the 
ISA99 committee and adopted by the IEC. It is the standard an assessor uses when certifying that an 
industrial control network is fit to connect to the corporate network. Three ideas from it matter here. 
 
1. A zone is a group of assets with similar security requirements. 
2. A conduit is a managed communication path between zones. 
3. Each zone and conduit is assigned a target security level (SL-T) based on the capability of the 
adversary it must withstand.  
 
Parts 3-2 and 3-3 of the IEC 62443 standard then applies requirements such as network segmentation, 
boundary protection, restricted data flow and monitoring to those conduits. 
 
3.2 The reversal 
IEC 62443 assumes the occupant of a zone is a trusted, deterministic process and the attacker is outside. 
That assumption is baked into how SL-T gets assigned – you look outward and ask who might come in. An 
evaluation sandbox running a capable model with safeguards removed breaks that assumption. The occupant 
is the adversary. It has time, it adapts and, in this case, it coordinated with hundreds of other occupants. 
 
So the method changes in one place. When assigning SL-T to a conduit out of the sandbox, the adversary is 
the occupant, and the level is set by the occupant’s demonstrated capability. In this analysis, everything else 
in the standard is applied as written. 
 
3.3 Procedure 
1. Build the zone and conduit model of OpenAI’s evaluation environment from published record [1,2]. I 
used only what the two companies stated. 
2. Assign an implied SL-T to each conduit from how the record describes it being treated, and a 
required SL-T from the occupant’s demonstrated capability. 

===== page 4 =====

3. Derive checks. For each conduit, take 62443-3-3 requirements that apply and write them as 
questions a team can answer from a configuration export, a permission export or a network policy. 
No live probing. I chose this because a check that needs a lab does not get run. 
4. Estimate effort for each check as low, medium, or high, and sort. 
5. Backtest. For each dated event on OpenAI’s timeline, record which checks reach it and the best 
effect – interrupt, limit detect, or none. 
What I did not do - I had no access to either environment and did not build one. I have not demonstrated 
anything. This is an analysis of the record using a method from another field. The telemetry argument in 
section 4.5 is reasoning from the properties of a channel, not a result, and I have labelled it as such. 
4. Results 
4.1 Zone and Conduit model 
 
 
Figure 1. Zone and conduit view of OpenAI’s evaluation environment, built from the published record. Boxes are zones. 
Blue lines are conduits. C1 is the only conduit the public record describes as a managed sandbox boundary. C2 and C3 
came with Artifactory and were not explicitly discussed in the public record as sandbox escape paths. 
 
OpenAI’s own account says the sandbox was granted access to Artifactory so models could install packages, 
and that Artifactory itself had internet access for the limited purpose of downloading packages [1]. In IEC 
62443 terms that is two conduits, not one. C1 runs from the sandbox to Artifactory. C2 runs from 
Artifactory to internet. Anyone who has done a conduit assessment sees immediately that C1 plus C2 is a 
path from the sandbox to the internet, and that the path’s security level is the weaker of the two. The third 
conduit, C3, is the one that took longest to matter. Artifactory was a shared service. It sat on the internal 
research network and held credentials and a signing key that were valid there. The record shows the agents 
obtaining administrator access to it, taking its signing key, and using what they found to reach cluster 
administrator on an OpenAI research cluster. That is a conduit from the sandbox into zone C, and it was 
never designed either. 
 
The message board is not a conduit. It is a property of Zone B. Artifactory was writable by workloads, and 
the agents used that to leave notes for one another [1]. But the board is what turned three separate conduits 
into a coordinated route, because the SSRF technique for C2 was posted there for other agents to reuse.

The mismatch is not that OpenAI got the level wrong on C1. It is that C2 and C3 were never assigned a

the most common finding in a first IEC 62443 assessment of any industrial control environment. The 
engineered boundary is fine. The paths that came with a shared service are the problem.

Table 3. Each dated event on OpenAI’s published timeline, the checks that reach it, and the best effect. Interrupt means

Three observations emerge from the backtest. First, the chain appears vulnerable to interruption early in the 
timeline through relatively low effort controls, particularly K2, K4 and K5. Second, the 8 July rebuild 
illustrates the distinction between hardening a component and assessing a conduit – access was regained 
through alternative paths despite remediation. Third, the model has clear scope limits. It explains how the 
agents reached the internet but does not attempt to explain later events on Modal or Hugging Face 
infrastructure. 
 
4.5 The next conduit 
One likely extension of the method is telemetry egress. Like Artifactory, telemetry leaves the sandbox by 
design and therefore constitutes a conduit that should be assessed explicitly. K11 and K12 represent 
preliminary checks derived from that observation and are retained as future work rather than validated 
findings. 
5. Discussion and Limitations 
The important implication is that containment should be analyzed as a property of conduits rather than 
boundaries alone. The public record suggests that the critical failures occurred within a supporting service 

===== page 8 =====

that was not itself considered part of the containment boundary. Read through IEC 62443, the incident can 
be understood as a mismatch between the capability of the occupant and the security assumptions assigned 
to the conduits surrounding it. OpenAI’s own remediation language supports this reading. The network 
isolation commitment in its report is that a single compromise of a workload or supporting service should not 
by itself allow unauthorized access to the internet or to other internal networks [1]. That is a conduit 
security level statement. It is what a 62443-3-2 assessment of Artifactory would have written down in 
May. 
Limitations 
• Everything here rests on one incident and two companies’ accounts of it. I have not validated the 
check set against a second case. 
• The zone model in Figure 1 is built from the public record. If OpenAI’s environment had conduits 
the record does not mention, the model is incomplete. Given the record says the agents chained 
multiple previously unknown flaws in the package service, I would expect it to be. 
• Configuration checks establish intent, not effect. Every check in Table 2 can pass while the control 
fails in practice. 62443 addresses this with verification testing that I have not included. 
• The telemetry argument in section 4.5 is not demonstrated. If the channel turns out to be harder to 
use than its properties suggest, K11 and K12 drop to housekeeping. 
• The reversal in section 3.2 is asserted, not derived. A 62443 practitioner could reasonably object 
that the standard’s threat model was never meant to be inverted. I think the machinery survives the 
inversion, but the claim is mine. 
Future Work 
• Demonstrate telemetry egress as an exfiltration channel in a controlled environment, which would 
move section 4.5 from argument to result. 
• Extend the conduit model to container registries and secrets services, which the record does not 
mention but any real environment has. 
• Run the check set against a second agent incident, ideally one not involving an Artifactory 
repository, to see whether the set generalizes or is fitted to this case. 
6. Conclusion 
The July 2026 incident is usually told as a sandbox escape through a zero day. Read through IEC 62443, it 
is a conduit assessment that was never done. The sandbox had one designed path out, package install, and 
two undesigned ones that came with the package service – its own internet link and its position on the 
internal network. Neither was assigned a security level because neither was drawn as a conduit, and the 
occupant, which was the adversary, found all three. 
 
The method that would have drawn them exists, is mature, and is used every day in industrial control 
environments. Twelve checks follow from it, seven of them cheap enough to run this week, and on the 
published record they reach some of the events on the chain. The events they do not reach are the late May 
escalation, which the checks can force but not decide, and the five events on Modal, Hugging Face and 
OpenAI’s internal network that sit outside the zone model.
