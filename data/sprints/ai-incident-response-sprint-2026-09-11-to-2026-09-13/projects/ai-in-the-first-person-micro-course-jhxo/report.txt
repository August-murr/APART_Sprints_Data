AI in the First Person: A Community Critical-Literacy 
Lab for Strengthening Human Oversight of AI Systems 
Track:  5 — Open Track: Education and Human Oversight 
Authors:  Erick Santos

Abstract  
The rapid expansion of generative artificial intelligence is creating an agency gap between people who can 
understand, evaluate, and guide these systems and those who only receive their outputs. This gap is particularly 
relevant for young people, non-technical users, and communities with diverse educational trajectories, who may 
interpret fluent AI responses as true, neutral, or authoritative. We present AI in the First Person , a community 
micro-course for critical AI literacy and assisted creation. The course teaches participants to explore, create, 
question, verify, and decide with AI through seven learning stations that combine interaction with AI tools, 
offline activities, information verification, bias recognition, data protection, and human oversight. The first 
implementation is proposed for educational and community spaces in La Candelaria and Santa Fe, Bogotá. The 
project relates to the sprint incident by treating human oversight as an educational capability: participants learn 
to identify doubtful outputs, avoid unauthorized or high-risk actions, protect sensitive information, and escalate 
situations requiring human review. We propose an exploratory pilot to assess comprehension, autonomy, 
verification, privacy, inclusion reasoning, and skills transfer. At this stage, we do not claim long-term impact 
results. 
1. Problem  
1.1. The agency gap in generative AI  
The digital divide is not limited to access to devices or connectivity. A second gap exists between people who 
can formulate clear instructions, interpret outputs, detect errors, recognize bias, protect data, and decide when to 
trust or escalate a situation, and those who only receive AI-generated answers. 
This distinction matters because language models can produce fluent and persuasive text without guaranteeing 
truth, understanding, communicative intent, or responsibility for their claims. Apparent coherence can lead users 
to attribute authority or knowledge to systems that can still produce false, outdated, biased, incomplete, or 
contextually inappropriate outputs (Bender et al., 2021). 
For people with limited digital experience, interrupted educational trajectories, low technological confidence, or 
limited access to formal training, this can create a form of agency inequality : AI may be available, but its 
benefits, limits, and risks are not equally understandable or manageable. 
1.2. Relationship to the sprint incident  
This project does not claim that education can replace technical containment, secure infrastructure, evaluations, 
policies, or incident-response procedures. Instead, it addresses a complementary layer of the problem: Human 
oversight is also an educational capability.  People who understand that AI can make mistakes, act within

limits, require verification, and should not receive sensitive information are better prepared to question outputs, 
stop unsafe actions, ask for human support, and avoid blind trust in automated systems. 
The course translates these ideas into basic community competencies: 
 An AI response may be wrong even when it sounds confident. 
 A system should not receive sensitive information without prior reflection. 
 An output may omit perspectives or reproduce harmful assumptions. 
 Important decisions require verification and human responsibility. 
 When a system behaves unexpectedly, a person should stop, seek help, and escalate the case rather than 
continue automatically. 
1.3. Why education is a safety intervention  
Generative AI can improve immediate task performance without producing learning. The OECD warns that 
systems providing direct answers may reduce active participation and weaken deep learning when they replace 
rather than support a person’s cognitive effort (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development 
[OECD], 2026a). PISA 2025 also highlights curiosity, perseverance, cognitive adaptability, help seeking, goal 
setting, critical interpretation of information, and opportunities to evaluate AI-generated content as relevant 
dimensions of future-ready learning (OECD, 2026b). Therefore, this project is not a prompt-writing course or a 
tool demonstration. It is an educational artifact that makes people practice a sequence of creation, questioning, 
verification, and decision-making. 
2. What We Built  
2.1. The artifact  
We designed AI in the First Person: A Community Lab for Exploring, Creating, Questioning, and Deciding with 
Artificial Intelligence. 
The artifact is a community micro-course that can be implemented in educational institutions, community 
houses, libraries, youth organizations, or other spaces with basic digital access. It is initially designed for La 
Candelaria and Santa Fe, Bogotá, with future adaptation for rural and low-connectivity contexts. 
The artifact includes: 
1.  A 120-minute core curriculum. 
2.  Seven learning stations. 
3.  Connected and offline activities. 
4.  A facilitation guide. 
5.  Formative assessment instruments. 
6.  A responsible assisted-thinking rubric. 
7.  A participant reflection log. 
8.  A privacy and safe-use protocol.

9.  A project premortem and mitigation matrix. 
10.  A territorial scaling plan. 
2.2. Core competency  
The central learning outcome is responsible assisted thinking . 
Responsible assisted thinking is the capacity to use an AI tool to expand human understanding, 
creativity, and action while retaining critical judgment, privacy awareness, respect for others, and 
responsibility for final decisions. 
A person demonstrates this competency by explaining a need or objective, providing relevant context to an AI 
tool, reviewing and improving generated outputs, recognizing that fluent text may still be wrong, verifying 
important information, identifying sensitive data that should not be shared, recognizing bias or omission, and 
explaining which decision remains under human responsibility. 
2.3. Pedagogical cycle  
Each station follows a consistent cycle: 
Connect → Explore → Create → Question → Transfer  
Phase  Function  Guiding question  
Connect  Start from a real interest or need  What matters to you or what do you want to solve?  
Explore  Test an interaction with AI  What happens when you ask this?  
Create  Produce a useful response or artifact  What can you do with this result?  
Question  Review errors, bias, gaps, and risks  What could be wrong, missing, or harmful?  
Transfer  Apply the skill in a new context  Where else could you use this strategy?  
 
The design treats AI as learning support rather than an answer machine. It may act as a mirror, assistant, source 
of contrast, and object of analysis; it should not become an unquestioned authority within the activity.

2.5. Accessibility and contextual design  
The micro-course supports different levels of literacy and digital confidence through short texts and plain 
language, audio and read-aloud options, visual cards and icons, step-by-step instructions, oral participation, 
examples connected with local interests, occupations, and needs, peer support, offline activities, and facilitator 
mediation. The systemic approach proposed by Reimers et al. (2026) emphasizes that AI education must adapt to 
local resources, languages, teacher capacity, curriculum, community needs, and historical inequalities rather than 
importing a generic technological solution. 
3. How We Will Test It  
3.1. Pilot design  
We propose an exploratory pilot with 50–100 participants in La Candelaria or Santa Fe. The group should 
include young people and adults with different levels of digital experience and participation in formal education. 
The pilot does not seek to establish causal impact or long-term behavioral change. Its purpose is to test clarity, 
accessibility, usability, participation, feasibility, and the possibility of observing responsible assisted-thinking 
behaviors within a short intervention. 
3.2. Procedure  
1.  Apply a short initial diagnostic. 
2.  Implement the seven learning stations. 
3.  Record facilitator observations. 
4.  Collect participant products and reflection logs. 
5.  Apply a brief exit assessment. 
6.  Conduct a feedback conversation. 
7.  Review the findings and produce version 0.2 of the course. 
3.3. Measures

3.4. What we do not claim yet  
At this stage, the pilot will not demonstrate that the course eliminates misinformation, permanently closes digital 
or AI-literacy gaps, protects against all AI-agent failures, changes behavior in the medium term, works 
identically in all communities, is validated for rural use, or replaces technical, institutional, legal, or 
infrastructure-level AI-safety controls. 
4. Main Finding  
4.1. Preliminary design finding  
Because the pilot has not yet been conducted, the project presents a preliminary design finding rather than an 
empirical result. 
A community AI-literacy intervention can operationalize human oversight as an observable educational 
competency when it requires people not only to use AI, but also to review outputs, detect errors, consider 
exclusion, protect data, verify information, and explain which decisions remain under human responsibility. 
The artifact differs from tool-centered training because it treats responsible oversight as a sequence of actions: 
create, question, verify, and decide. In this way, it translates abstract AI-safety ideas into practices that non-
technical people can rehearse. 
4.2. Why it matters for incident response  
The sprint incident shows that systems can act beyond expected boundaries and fail to notify responsible people 
reliably. Technical safeguards are necessary, but they are not the only relevant safety layer. Users, facilitators, 
communities, and institutions also need competencies to recognize when an AI output or action requires caution, 
intervention, escalation, or rejection. 
This project understands community AI literacy as a complementary containment layer. It does not transfer 
responsibility solely to individual users; it prepares them to recognize when responsibility should pass to 
teachers, facilitators, institutions, developers, or formal reporting mechanisms. 
5. Limitations and Dual-Use Considerations  
5.1. Limitations  
 The first prototype has not yet been evaluated with a large or representative sample. 
 The course does not replace foundational literacy, sustained digital education, or advanced technical AI-
safety training. 

===== page 6 =====

 Some activities may initially depend on computers and connectivity. 
 AI platforms can change their capabilities, policies, interfaces, and outputs. 
 The course cannot guarantee detection of all bias, error, misinformation, or unsafe content. 
 Rural adaptation requires independent validation and should not be assumed from an urban pilot. 
 A short intervention cannot establish durable skills or long-term behavior change. 
 The artifact does not directly address technical containment of production systems. 
5.2. Dual-use risks and unintended effects  
The course itself could produce harm if implemented inadequately: 
 People may become more confident in AI without becoming more critical. 
 They may learn to generate content without properly verifying information. 
 Tools may expose participants to biased, offensive, or unsafe responses. 
 Facilitators may accidentally collect sensitive personal information. 
 General examples may reinforce dominant assumptions if they are not contextualized. 
 Presenting oversight as an individual skill may conceal institutional and developer responsibilities. 
 Content-generation skills could be used to produce deceptive or manipulative material. 
6. What Follows  
With one additional month of work, we will develop four lines of work. 
6.1. Pilot and iteration  
 Deliver the course to 50–100 participants. 
 Compare participants with and without prior AI experience. 
 Identify barriers in comprehension, navigation, accessibility, and verification. 
 Review materials with local facilitators. 
 Produce version 0.2 of the course. 
6.2. Educational test bank  
Develop a localized case bank to test: 
 Hallucinations and false claims. 
 Omitted context. 
 Biased or stereotyped language. 
 Contextually inappropriate recommendations. 
 Ambiguous instructions. 

===== page 7 =====

 Privacy risks. 
 Situations in which a person should stop, ask for help, or escalate a case. 
6.3. Facilitator training  
Design an 8–12-hour train-the-trainer program for teachers, youth leaders, community mediators, and local 
organizations. The program would include pedagogical sequences, accessibility, privacy, ethical facilitation, 
formative assessment, offline activities, and adaptation to local needs. 
6.4. Low-connectivity and rural adaptation  
Develop a layered strategy: 
1.  Printed guides, audio, and offline activities. 
2.  Downloadable resources for available computers. 
3.  Local server or Wi-Fi resource library. 
4.  Voice interface and low-data materials. 
5.  Local small-model deployment only if pedagogical and technical feasibility is validated. 
The offline-hardware concept is not the central contribution of this project. It is a possible distribution 
architecture for a later phase, after validating the curriculum and facilitation model in urban community spaces.
