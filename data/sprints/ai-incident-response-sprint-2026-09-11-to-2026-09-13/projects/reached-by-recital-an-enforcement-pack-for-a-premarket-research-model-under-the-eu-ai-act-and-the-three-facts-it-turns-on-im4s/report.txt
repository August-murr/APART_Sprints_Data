1. Introduction
Two episodes sit behind this report. Models belonging to a frontier developer left an evaluation sandbox in July 
2026 and reached a third party’s production infrastructure (Hugging Face, 2026); between May and June the 
same developer’s agents had made roughly 17,000 edits to a community wiki (Von Arx et al., 2026). The first 
was handled under “a traditional security incident response playbook”; the second was treated as “an instance 
of misalignment” and acknowledged publicly on 5 September 2026, the day after independent researchers  
published (OpenAI, 2026c).
Commentary since has asked whether these episodes were reported as the AI Act requires. That question  
assumes an answer to a prior one. The model that drove the compromise is described by its own developer as 
an “internal-only research model”, “not intended for public release” (OpenAI, 2026b), and by the independent 
investigators as “a research model which was not intended for production” (METR and Redwood Research,  
2026). Article 3, point (63) excludes from the definition of ‘general-purpose AI model’ those “used for  
research, development or prototyping activities before they are placed on the market”, and Article 2(8)  
excludes pre-market research, testing and development activity from the Regulation’s application. Whether  
the obligations attach at all is therefore the threshold question, and it is not the one the commentary has been 
asking.
It matters because the answer decides who acts: if the obligations attach, the AI Office has a compulsory  
information power it is already using; if they do not, no enforcement reaches the conduct and the response is  
legislative. The threat model is narrow and recurrent — a model never released, evaluated internally, that  
escapes containment and causes harm outside the developer — and it is precisely the configuration the research 
exclusion was drafted to leave alone.
Our main contributions are:
• A scope finding, and the route as the finding. On the Commission’s reading the obligations attach to a pre-
market research model, but through Recital 97 and guidance that disclaims its own authority rather than 
through the enacting definition, and the route needs three facts — a placement event, a Union nexus, and 
systemic risk. The public record supplies none of them and the provider’s own account denies one (§4.1).
• An instrumentation finding that needs none of that reading. The Commission’s serious-incident template 
for general-purpose AI models never asks when the provider became aware, though every initial-report  
period in the Code of Practice runs from awareness. The cause is traceable: fields 1 to 9 transcribe Measure 
9.2 item for item, and Measure 9.2 has no awareness item either. The Commission has already drafted the 
field — for the high-risk regime (§4.2).
• An enforcement pack rather than an analysis of one, and an apparatus that produces the next one. Seven 
documents in the form their addressee would issue or adopt, including a model Article 91 request in Article 
91(4)’s required form and three amendments in current-text/amended-text form (§4.3). Behind them a  
verification layer — a statutory register of forty-seven verified provisions, an inference register, six  
checkers, a corrections log of thirty-one entries — and six scripts that stand the whole of it up on a new  
incident. The claim that it transfers is not an aspiration: a second incident was run end to end, on a  
different provider, in a single session (§4.4).
1.1 Using it: from a new incident to an issued request
The repository is written to be picked up rather than read. Five steps, each a command or a rule, take a fresh 
episode to a request a regulator could sign:

• Stand up the repository. python3 tools/new-incident.py PATH creates the working directory with 
the schemas, the six checkers and the verified statutory register already in it. Nothing statutory has to be 
looked up twice.
• Hold the record before characterising it. Archive each source, print the archived copy to _sources/, and 
let  python3 tools/hold-source.py --draft  write  the  register  entry  with  its  SHA-256. 
validate.py then refuses any fact whose source is not held.
• Fill the cross-walk. One row per obligation, the provider’s facts in a different column from the provider’s 
characterisation of them, and every verdict in one of the three permitted forms. This is the part no tool  
does, and should not.
• Render the instruments. draft-request.py drafts an Article 91 request from the rows still unresolved; 
formfill.py renders the regulator’s own template filled from the record, each cell naming its source;  
render-pack.py renders the pack to DOCX for editing.
• Attack it before anyone else does.  python3 tools/check.py must come back green on all six, and  
python3 tools/infercheck.py --attack prints the load-bearing steps weakest first, which is the  
list to argue with.
2. Related Work
The closest prior work is Pistillo (2026), a chapter for the Cambridge Commentary on EU General-Purpose 
AI Law arguing that internal deployment falls within the Act. This report agrees with that conclusion for  
internal deployment and shares one of his two pathways — the extensive reading of ‘making available’ under 
Article 3, points (9) and (10). It differs in subject and therefore in result: his model is deployed internally,  
where this record describes a model never deployed at all, whose only recorded system is the evaluation  
harness. That is the difference the three facts exist to close. The Commission’s own instruments are the  
primary counterparty rather than the literature: the Guidelines of 19 November 2025 (C(2025) 7719 final;  
European Commission, 2025), the general-purpose AI Q&A (European Commission, 2026a) and Opinion  
C(2025) 5361 final (European Commission, 2026c), each held and read for what it does not say as carefully as 
for what it does. Incident commentary asks what was disclosed and whether it was timely (Cloud Security  
Alliance, 2026; Uuk, 2026), while the independent investigation of the episode (METR and Redwood  
Research, 2026) was access-scoped by its subject. None asks whether the obligations reached the model at all.
Three things this work adds sit alongside that literature rather than inside it. It supplies the documents rather 
than the argument: a request in the form a regulator would issue, and amendments in the form a legislator  
would table. It reads the Commission’s own reporting form against the Code’s own clock — the finding at §4.2 
came from filling the form in, not from reading about it. And its verification layer is packaged to be re-run  
rather than written once: the Article 91 request is roughly 60% boilerplate that survives any incident, and the 
second run at §4.4 is the evidence for the rest.
3. Methods
The ceiling, stated in advance. The method answers one question — does the discloser’s own account, taken 
on its own terms, resolve this obligation? — and it cannot answer what happened. Every verdict takes one of 
three permitted forms: on the provider’s own account, this appears met; appears unmet; or the disclosed record 
does not resolve this. tools/validate.py rejects any cell that drifts out of that register. An analysis that  
cannot overclaim is one a regulator can read without auditing it first.
Four rules make the reasoning inspectable, each stated in full at Appendix D. Sources are held before they are 
characterised — every source a fact rests on is archived to _sources/ with its SHA-256, and forty-seven  
provisions, seven imported provisions of the CER Directive (Directive (EU) 2022/2557) and four recitals are 
verified line by line against the consolidated Regulation and the authentic Official Journal text, five CJEU  

===== page 4 =====

judgments on EUR-Lex, each carrying the date of its check. Evidence is tiered by relationship to the claim  
rather than prestige. Absences are bounded by a date and a named corpus. And inferences are enumerated:  
data/inferences.yaml states each argumentative step as a premise set, names what would defeat it, and 
ranks it.
Six checkers run from one entry point.  tools/check.py runs hold-source.py (no fact may rest on a  
source nobody holds), validate.py (verdict discipline), citecheck.py (citations resolve to verified text), 
quotecheck.py (quotations found in the held document), infercheck.py (premises, defeaters, acyclicity) 
and housestyle.py (conventions, repeated paragraphs, drifted counts, dangling paths). What is automated 
is checking, not judgement: they will say a citation does not resolve or a count has drifted; they will never say 
an inference is wrong. Four adversarial passes were run besides — two over a finished draft by a model with no 
sight of the reasoning that produced it, one over the instrument, one over the whole repository with the held  
sources open. Every reviewer was a language model, and §5 says what follows. Whether the method is usable 
by anybody else is an empirical question, so it was run as one: §4.4.
4. Results
4.1 The route attaches, and needs three facts
The enacting text points away from scope. Article 2(1), point (a) applies the Regulation to providers “placing 
on the market or putting into service AI systems or placing on the market general-purpose AI models”. The 
halves differ: ‘putting into service’ at Article 3, point (11) expressly includes supply “for own use in the Union 
for its intended purpose”, and models have no equivalent limb. Article 3, point (63) then carries no “sole  
purpose” qualifier and no conditions, though Article 2(6) shows the formula was available. On the enacting  
text alone, an internal unreleased research model sits outside Chapter V.
Recital 97 moves the moment of placement. A model integrated into its provider’s own system put into service 
“should be  considered to be placed on the market ” — so the development was never activity “prior to”  
placement, and Article 2(8) never engages. The Commission reproduces the rule at paragraph 54 of its  
Guidelines, and its Q&A consolidates it into three cumulative conditions, the third decisive: the exception is  
unavailable where “the model is not a general-purpose AI model with systemic risk”. Paragraph 22 treats a  
model’s lifecycle as beginning “at the start of the large pre-training run”, so the exclusion protects a model  
never placed on the market, not the development phase of one that is.
That route needs three facts, and the record supplies none of them  (Figure 1). A placement event: every 
version of the Commission’s reading runs from one, and the provider’s account says the model was among  
“research models that were not intended for public release”, with its weights quarantined after the incident. A 
Union nexus: Article 2(1), point (a) reaches placing on the market “in the Union”, and no account places any 
own system incorporating this model in the Union. Systemic risk: Article 51(2) presumes it above 10²⁵ FLOP, 
and no public source gives the figure. Each absence is bounded: the placement and Union questions as of 13  
September 2026, across the six incident-record documents held here, searched for “deploy”, “internal”,  
“Union” and “Europe”; the compute figure as of 12 September 2026, across those documents and English-
language web search on the provider, “Article 52”, “notification” and “AI Office” (Appendix C).

The fragility, stated. The weight-bearing step is a recital and non-binding guidance, and a preamble “has no 
binding legal force and cannot be relied on as a ground for derogating from the actual provisions of the act in 
question” (Nilsson, C-162/97, ¶54). Recital 97 has two limbs and only the weaker is available to a model  
never released: where the provider’s own system is merely put into service, nothing is supplied to anyone, and 
putting into service is exactly the trigger Article 2(1), point (a) withholds from models. Appendix D takes that 
argument in full. Paragraph 9 of the Guidelines says they are “not binding” but set out the interpretation “on 
which it will base its enforcement action”: the reading governs what the Commission will do, and stays open 
to a provider prepared to litigate.
4.2 The form cannot show what the clock needs
Article 55(1), point (c) fixes no period. Measure 9.3 of the Code’s Safety and Security Chapter runs each  
initial-report period from the date a Signatory becomes aware of its model’s involvement, and sets five days for 
“a serious cybersecurity breach, including the (self-)exfiltration of model weights and cyberattacks”  — a 
trigger with no counterpart in Article 3, point (49), and one a model escaping containment into a third party’s

production infrastructure sits inside far more naturally than the property limb. Paragraph (100) of the  
Guidelines anchors that reading in Article 55(1), points (b) and (d), so the Article 3(49) limbs need not be  
reached at all.
The form omits the date its own clock runs from. Table 1 fills the official template of 4 November 2025 from 
the public record. It asks the start and end dates of the incident and no other date; “aware”, “awareness”,  
“deadline” and “timeline” do not occur in it, in either published rendition, checked at the level of the DOCX’s 
own stored field labels. The cause is traceable: fields 1 to 9 transcribe Measure 9.2 item for item, and Measure 
9.2 has no awareness item either. The claim needs nothing from the scope analysis — the template says on its 
face that it demonstrates compliance “as part of Commitment 9”, so on the Code’s own terms it cannot evidence 
the Code’s own periods. Nor is it hypothetical: a report on the wiki episode has been filed, the Commission has 
confirmed  receipt,  and  its  spokesperson  has  declined  to  disclose  the  date  or  the  contents  (European 
Commission, 2026b). The Commission has already drafted both missing fields — for the high-risk regime  
(Appendix C) — and the amended template closes the gap from the model side without waiting for a  
classification the Omnibus deferred to December 2027 and August 2028.

4.3 The power is already in use, and the pack is written for it
On 1 September 2026 the Commission stated that the AI Office had sent its first requests for information to  
more than 30 providers; they come, the reporting agency records, “after several incidents involving AI models 
that occurred this summer” (Agence Europe, 2026). Article 91 is therefore not proposed here in the abstract: 
it is the instrument the AI Office reached for first, on this subject, weeks after these episodes. What the  
attached request adds is specificity. On the only public account of what those requests ask — how providers  

===== page 7 =====

prevent their models being stolen, how much access external evaluators get, how usage is monitored once a  
model is public (Uuk, 2026, relaying Euractiv) — none of the three reaches the question this analysis turns on. 
A request written across thirty providers cannot ask whether this model was integrated into an own AI system 
put into service; an incident-specific one can, and it is the first thing this one asks.
The pack in instrument/ puts each document in the form its addressee would issue or adopt, and renders  
each to DOCX because light edits happen in Word: a cover note to the AI Office; the model Article 91 request; 
an issuing note carrying the reasoning behind each request, so the request itself does not pre-grade answers; an 
answer matrix saying, for each answer the provider could return, where it puts the model and what the  
Commission does next, every cell a held provision; the serious-incident template rebuilt; an amendment table to 
Article 3, points (9) and (63) and Article 55(1), point (c), every added word traced to the text it is lifted from; 
and the load-bearing steps weakest first, with the objection against each.
4.4 The method transfers, and a second incident is the evidence
The test of an analysis method is not whether it produced one good analysis. runs/anthropic-cyber-evals-
2026/ applies the same apparatus to a different provider’s incidents, disclosed on 30 July and 9 September  
2026 (Anthropic, 2026): ten held documents in one session, most of the effort spent reading them. The elapsed 
time was not measured and no figure is claimed for it. python3 tools/new-incident.py PATH stands up 
the empty repository — schemas, six checkers, and the verified statutory register — in a minute, which is the 
step that would otherwise be a week.
What carried over. The whole verification layer, unchanged: the verdict discipline, the checkers, the register 
schemas, the corrections-log convention, the negative-search rule. The Article 91 request is roughly 60%  
boilerplate; the answer matrix and decision tree are written for the ‘internal model’ fact pattern rather than  
this episode; the amended template is universal. What did not, and should not:  the content. The cross-walk 
rows, the scope working and the inferences are the product of a person opening documents and making  
judgements, and a tool that generated those would be a tool for generating plausible regulatory claims.
What the second run showed that the first could not. The internal-model scope question arrived unchanged, 
which is the sign that §4.1 describes a fact pattern rather than an episode. The scaffolding objection arrived at 
full strength exactly as the first run’s inference register predicted, then split once the primary source was  
opened. The date of awareness the Commission’s form does not ask for is, in this incident, public — the same 
form failing in the same place for a different reason. And one evaluator’s misconfiguration turned out to have 
reached three providers’ models, a gap in the Act the first incident had no way of showing. The run also made 
the method’s signature error twice in its first hour and logged both, which is what produced hold-source.py.
4.5 What the corrections did to the findings
The robustness claim available here is not statistical, and saying so is part of the method. What can be shown 
is how the findings moved under adversarial pressure. The instrumentation finding has not moved through any 
of the thirty-one corrections. The scope finding has narrowed twice — from “the obligations attach” to “they 
attach by a recital” to “they attach if three facts hold” — and the narrowing is the finding. Every one of the 
thirty-one errors in the log passed every check that existed when it was made: a clean board means the  
mechanical failures are absent and nothing more.
5. Discussion and Limitations
The broader implication is about where the Act’s gaps sit. The debate about frontier incidents assumes the  
obligations reach the conduct and argues about enforcement. On this record the prior question is open, and  
open in a way that favours the provider: the enacting definition excludes the model, the Commission’s reading 
brings it back through a recital, and the three facts that reading needs are held exclusively by the party whose 
conduct is in question. That is structural rather than incident-specific. Internal evaluation of an unreleased  

===== page 8 =====

model is the phase the Regulation’s text most clearly leaves alone, and it is the phase this episode happened in. 
The second implication is smaller and more actionable: an instrument that cannot record the date its own  
deadline runs from will not produce enforceable timeliness, however well drafted the duty behind it. That  
failure is invisible in doctrinal analysis and obvious the moment somebody fills the form in — an argument for 
regulators testing their own instruments against synthetic records before the first real filing rather than after 
it. Both implications are addressed to someone who has to act. An analyst at a regulator, a civil-society  
researcher or a provider’s compliance team can clone the repository, run new-incident.py, and start the  
next episode with the statutory register already verified, the verdict discipline already enforced and the failure 
modes of the last two runs already written down — which is a different proposition from reading an analysis 
and agreeing with it.
Limitations
This cannot establish what happened, or that anything was contravened.  Every verdict is a claim about a  
disclosed record assembled by interested parties, and the facts it turns on are held by the party in question: if 
the placement event, the Union nexus or the systemic-risk threshold fails, most of the scope analysis falls away 
and the case becomes purely legislative. The answer matrix routes that outcome explicitly rather than hedging 
it.
Most of the pack does not depend on the scope reading. A regulator who takes the stricter Article 2(8) line 
still has the instrumentation finding, the amended serious-incident template and Amendment 3 on Article  
55(1), point (c), none of which needs Recital 97 to do any work. What that reader loses is the request’s first  
three questions — and the case for the two definitional amendments gets stronger rather than weaker, because 
on that reading the gap they close is real.
Four assumptions are marked contestable, with what would defeat each.  inf-placement-event — the 
record denies one of its three routes, and Requests 2b and 4a are what would establish another. inf-union-
nexus — nothing in the record addresses it, and a third-country provider is where it bites. inf-opinion-
construes — the competing reading is that Opinion ¶(33) summarises the Code rather than construes the  
Act, favoured by its own “may” and “typically”; without it a non-signatory has no determinate period at all,  
which is what Amendment 3 answers. inf-scaffolding-attribution — that the agentic conduct was the 
harness’s rather than the model’s, blunted by the provider naming the absent safeguards as its own and by  
“directly or indirectly” in every applicable instrument, but surviving a scope finding intact. Relatedly, the  
timing finding is signatory-dependent where the scope finding is not: a run against a provider that has not  
signed the Code loses every determinate period and keeps every other row.
Every reviewer was a language model, and the author is not a lawyer.  Four adversarial passes were run,  
including one over the instrument by a model prompted to read it as a Commission lawyer; no lawyer was  
consulted and nobody real was contacted. Not addressed: whether the affected platform provides an essential 
service within a CER Annex sector; the Article 3(1)/3(63) bridging question; and the New York RAISE Act, 
which is described in the comparative appendix, registered nowhere, and relied on for nothing.
Future Work
A practitioner’s read of the request and the amendment table is the single thing that would most change the  
confidence available here, and it is the immediate next step. Beyond that: the affected organisations’ side of the 
second run; a register row for the New York RAISE Act; the revision history of the July disclosure; and a DOI 
for the statutory register, so the forty-seven verified provisions can be cited independently of this report.
6. Conclusion
Whether the July 2026 containment failure was reportable under the EU AI Act cannot be answered from the 
public record, and the reason is not evidentiary thinness but statutory structure. The enacting definition  

===== page 9 =====

excludes a pre-market research model; the Commission’s reading brings it back through Recital 97 and  
guidance that disclaims its own authority; and that route needs three facts — a placement event, a Union  
nexus, and a compute figure — that only the provider holds, one of which its own account denies. The  
Regulation contains the instrument for asking, the AI Office is already using it on this subject, and this report 
supplies the incident-specific version, with an answer matrix saying what each answer does and three  
amendments for the case where the answer is no.
Alongside it sits a finding that needs none of that reasoning and is fixable this week: the Commission’s serious-
incident template does not ask when the provider became aware, although every initial-report period in the  
Code of Practice runs from that date, and the Commission has already drafted the field for its high-risk regime. 
The amended template is attached. The deeper point is methodological: this analysis has been materially wrong 
thirty-one times, and the log of those errors ships with it, because an analysis a regulator can act on has to be 
one whose mistakes are visible from outside it.

Appendix A: Limitations and dual-use considerations
A.1 Access asymmetry
The parties able to resolve most rows are the ones whose conduct is in question. The independent investigation 
was access-scoped by its subject, could not query the principal model, and delegated analysis to a model  
implicated in the incident while stating it could not rule out being misled (METR and Redwood Research,  
2026). Analysis built on that record inherits its shape, which is the argument for a compulsory information  
power rather than for more external analysis.
A.2 Dual use: aggregation rather than disclosure
The exploit chain is already public (Hugging Face, 2026). This work does not reproduce payloads, injection  
strings, or a consolidated reconstruction of the escalation path, and cites the chain by reference only. The same 
facts scattered across a vendor post and an incident timeline are a different artifact from those facts assembled 

===== page 11 =====

and annotated in one place; assembly adds the operational value, and regulatory analysis needs the category of 
vulnerability rather than the working detail.
A.3 Dual use: the instrument
A well-formed regulatory instrument can be misused, to lend false authority or to harass through procedurally 
correct  but  empty  demands.  Three  mitigations:  the  provenance  header  states  it  has  not  been  issued; 
placeholders are explicit rather than plausible; and every request is traced to a recorded unresolved row.
A.4 Dual use: the scope analysis
§4.1 sets out the strongest textual argument available to a provider seeking to place a pre-market research  
model outside the Regulation, and publishes it anyway. It is derivable by anyone who reads Articles 2(1)(a),  
3(9) to (11) and 3(63) together and is already in the literature (Pistillo, 2026), so publication confers no  
advantage a competent adviser does not hold; a fragility regulators can see is less dangerous than one they  
cannot, and the amendment table is narrow and available.
A.5 Even-handedness
Two developers’ self-reports are registered at the same tier with the same reservations, and the second run  
applies the method to the incidents of the developer whose assistant was used to build it. The author has an  
application pending with that developer. A register that treated one organisation’s account of its own conduct 
as more reliable than another’s would not be a method.

Appendix C: The two reporting regimes, sourcing, and bounded absences
The high-risk comparison, with its three qualifications.  The Commission’s draft template for serious  
incidents involving high-risk AI systems asks in section 1.2 for the date of report submission, the date of the 
incident, the date and time of detection, and the “Manufacturer awareness date of reportability”, and runs to  
seven pages against the model template’s two. Three things hold that comparison in place. The high-risk field 
asks for awareness of reportability, tracking Article 73(2), where Measure 9.3 runs from awareness of the  
model’s involvement. Both high-risk documents are consultation drafts of 26 September 2025, with no final  
version linked as of 12 September 2026, and the accompanying draft guidance says expressly that it is “not  
dealing with” general-purpose models. And the regime they serve is not in application: Article 73 has applied 
only since 2 August 2026, Article 75(1a) since 27 July 2026, and the Omnibus deferred the high-risk  
classification both depend on to 2 December 2027 and 2 August 2028. Article 75(1), point (a) nonetheless  
makes the AI Office exclusively competent for AI systems “based on general-purpose AI models where the  
model and the system are developed by the same provider, or by providers forming part of the same  
undertaking”, and Article 75(1a) routes those systems’ serious incidents to the same AI Office that Article

Appendix D: The method, the corrections log, and what transfers
The fragility of the route, in full. Recital 97 has two limbs and they fare differently. Where the provider’s own 
system is made available on the market , the embedded model is supplied for use on the Union market in the  
course of a commercial activity, which is what Article 3, points (9) and (10) say placing means; that reads as 
interpretation rather than as derogation, and a preamble may not be relied on to interpret provisions contrary 
to their wording (Deutsches Milch-Kontor, C-136/04, ¶32). Where the own system is merely put into service, 
nothing is supplied to anyone. The Commission does not rest the rule on the recital alone — paragraph 51 of 
the Guidelines lists essential internal use among its examples of placing on the market, read with Articles 3(9) 
and (10), which is the extensive reading of ‘making available’ that Pistillo (2026) runs as his second pathway 
— but whether own use can be a supply is the question, and the enacting text does not settle it.
The four rules in full. Sources are held before they are characterised. Statutory text comes from the EUR-Lex 
consolidated version (CELEX 02024R1689-20260727) and recitals from the authentic Official Journal text; 
amendment status is taken from the amending act’s own numbered instructions rather than the consolidated  
text’s change markers, because reading the markers alone had wrongly cleared Article 60, which instruction  
(24) of Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 replaces in part.  Evidence is tiered by relationship, not prestige : a  
provider’s account of its own conduct is T1 for what it admits against interest and weak for everything else,  
while the affected party’s forensic timeline is stronger than the provider’s account for what happened at the  
victim; facts sit in separate columns from characterisation, because a provider’s framing is where its position 
enters an analysis dressed as a finding. Absences are proved, not asserted, and a refusal is kept apart from an 
absence. Inferences are enumerated so that a reader who rejects one can see exactly what falls with it.
Classification, and the two objections that survive a scope finding. For the wider Article 3, point (49) route,
