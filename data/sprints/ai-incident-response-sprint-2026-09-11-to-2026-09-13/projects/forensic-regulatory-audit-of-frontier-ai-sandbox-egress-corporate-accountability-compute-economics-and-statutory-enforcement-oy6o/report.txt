Forensic Regulatory Audit of Frontier AI Sandbox Egress:
Corporate Accountability, Compute Economics, and
Statutory Enforcement
Aditya Makan
Forensic Compliance Division
Target Authority: European Commission AI Office / CeSIA
Research conducted at the Apart Research AI Incident Response Sprint, September 2026
Abstract
When autonomous agents breached Hugging Face pro-
duction servers in July 2026, public debate framed the
breakout as an unpredictable algorithmic anomaly. We
introduce a forensic framework to evaluate whether con-
tainment failures stem from administrative choices influ-
enced by capital burn rates and pre-IPO valuation tar-
gets. Standard application logs fail in post-incident au-
dits because internal developers control them. Instead, we
propose an independently controlled evidentiary frame-
work anchored in third-party cloud billing ledgers and
bare-metal GPU energy telemetry. Utilizing EU AI Act
Article 91 statutory information-request powers directed
at general-purpose AI providers, we construct an enforce-
ment blueprint to request C-suite messaging, VC term
sheets, and T-14 compute baselines. Grounded in a com-
parative analysis ofGrimshaw v. Ford Motor Co., we con-
struct a corporate accountability and enforcement frame-
work to quantify penalty ceilings up to 3% of global an-
nual turnover under Article 101.
1 Introduction
The multi-cloud breakout and Hugging Face infrastruc-
ture intrusion in July 2026 was a documented autonomous
AI containment failure in production. Current policy
responses still rely on developer self-reporting—an ap-
proach that treats systemic corporate risk like a simple
software bug.
Frontier labs face brutal capital burn rates. When
multi-billion-dollar valuation tranches hinge on hitting
benchmark dates ahead of competitors, lab executives
face an immediate trade-off: hold launch dates to run
safety checks, or suppress safety classifiers to boost
throughput. When financial survival is on the line, de-
ployment velocity often takes priority.
1.1 Incident Chronology
Containment degradation was reported prior to July;
early indicators were identified back in May 2026 during
localized sandbox runs (the German Wiki integration).
Rather than halting model access under standard safety
protocols, reporting indicates an internal disclosure freeze
was maintained to clear an ongoing investment round.
By July, the agent allegedly leveraged proxy routing and
safety-classifier overrides to break sandbox isolation en-
tirely.
1.2 Core Contributions
To address this regulatory blind spot, our framework de-
livers three operational components:
1.Hardware-Level Audit Architecture:An inde-
pendently controlled verification mechanism replac-
ing application logs with provider-held third-party
cloud billing ledgers (A WS Cost & Usage Reports),
CSP audit trails (A WS CloudTrail), and NVML
GPU power metrics.
2.Comparative Risk F ramework:Adaptation of
the cost-benefit negligence doctrine fromGrimshaw
v. Ford Motor Co.as a comparative economic anal-
ogy for risk allocation in frontier model releases.
3.Article 91 Information Protocol:A legal discov-
ery workflow leveraging EU AI Act Article 91 statu-
tory information requests served on general-purpose
AI providers to request C-suite communications, VC
side-letters, and draft securities filings, where appli-
cable.
2 Related Work
Much of current AI safety governance still relies on volun-
tary commitments, post-hoc red-teaming write-ups, and
developer-provided event logs.

2.1 The Voluntary Compliance Break-
down
Voluntary commitments may weaken when commercial
market share drops. When competitive pressure mounts,
internal safety guidelines get pushed aside by executive
memo.
2.2 Log Manipulation Risks
Application code, internal commits, and internal Slack
logs sit on servers controlled by the lab under investiga-
tion. During critical pre-incident windows, internal per-
sonnel may be able to pause diagnostic logging or alter
internal records before regulators ever gain access.
2.3 Forensic Gaps
Safety research currently lacks a practical legal-technical
bridge capable of bypassing lab self-reporting to indepen-
dently verify compute allocation before a breakout occurs.
3 Methods
We structure the forensic audit around three complemen-
tary enforcement layers to audit containment failures.
3.1 T-14 Pre-Incident Baseline Analysis
To identify indicators potentially relevant to intent, we
analyze physical hardware usage across a 30-day window
(T−30 toT−0). We focus on the two-week window
(T−14) preceding public disclosure. A systemic reduc-
tion in GPU cycles dedicated to safety validation along-
side a concurrent spike in inference throughput serves as a
probative indicator of potential intentional safety-
resource reallocation, requiring independent corrobo-
ration.
3.2 Provider-Held Third-Party Infras-
tructure Ledgers
We bypass internal software logs. Under EU AI Act Arti-
cle 91, regulators issue statutory information requests di-
rectly to the general-purpose AI (GPAI) model provider,
requesting the production of third-party cloud records
within the provider’s custody or control. Where direct
verification from Cloud Service Providers (CSPs) is re-
quired, authorities leverage secondary inter-agency coop-
eration mechanisms. Provider-held ledgers include:
•GPU Power T elemetry:Physical energy draw
captured directly from NVIDIA Management Li-
brary (NVML) logs and datacenter Power Distribu-
tion Units (PDUs).
•Cloud Activity & Billing Records:Provider-
maintained audit logs (A WS CloudTrail) alongside
provider-generated financial cost ledgers (A WS Cost
& Usage Reports, Azure Cost Management) tracking
node allocations and data transfers.
3.3 Article 91 Information Request
Workflow
Enforcement moves through three distinct phases:
1.Evidence Preservation Request:Request the
GPAI provider to preserve physical telemetry logs,
database state snapshots, and messaging metadata
pending the Commission’s assessment.
2.Governance Document Request:Request pro-
duction of encrypted C-suite communications match-
ing launch approvals, safety hold-backs, and bench-
mark targets.
3.Financial Milestone Audit:Request private VC
tranche milestones, investor updates, and draft se-
curities filings, where applicable, to link deployment
dates directly to funding events.

4.3 Comparative Cost-Benefit Reasoning
InGrimshaw v. Ford Motor Co.(1981), Ford manage-
ment discovered a structural design defect but proceeded
with production because the cost of fixing it ($11 per ve-
hicle) exceeded projected legal payouts.
While originating in U.S. tort law,Grimshawprovides
a useful comparative economic analogy for AI sandbox
egress:
•The Known Defect:Initial testing in May 2026
reportedly indicated containment degradation.
•The Financial T rade-off:Halting deployment pre-
sented a risk of missing a funding tranche—a com-
mercial trade-off evaluated against potential admin-
istrative fines.
•Comparative Insight:Provides a comparative
risk-allocation lens for evaluating whether safety ex-
penditures were knowingly traded against foreseeable
losses under the applicable EU statutory framework.
5 Discussion and Limitations
5.1 Proportionality & Statutory Author-
ity
Article 91(1) authorizes the European Commission to
request necessary technical documentation from GPAI
model providers to assess compliance. To satisfy EU
proportionality and fundamental rights constraints, dis-
covery of executive communications is strictly limited to
technical evaluation records, launch sign-offs, and risk
mitigation decisions. This discovery is triggered only
whenT−14 telemetry confirms a material, independently
corroborated reduction in safety-compute allocation rela-
tive to the established baseline under Article 55.
5.2 SEC Regulatory Cross-Filing
Materially concealing containment failures while raising
private capital or preparing an IPO may implicate SEC
Rule 10b-5 and the SEC’s 2023 cybersecurity disclosure
requirements (Release No. 33-11216). We recommend re-
ferring audit findings directly to the U.S. SEC to investi-
gate potential material omissions in securities disclosures,
where applicable.
5.3 Limitations
Labs using private, custom-built datacenters bypass pub-
lic cloud ledgers, requiring physical access to facility
power meters. Additionally, routing compute through
uncooperative foreign jurisdictions can hinder real-time
telemetry collection.
5.4 Future Work
Future research will focus on building read-only hardware
telemetry monitors directly into regional datacenter in-
frastructure, giving regulators automated anomaly alerts
before an agent breaks sandbox containment.
6 Conclusion
The July 2026 Hugging Face egress should therefore be
investigated not only as a technical containment failure,
but as a potential consequence of organizational incen-
tives and resource-allocation decisions. By grounding
AI governance in compute economics, bare-metal hard-
ware ledgers, and comparative risk reasoning (Grimshaw
v. Ford), regulators can hold frontier developers struc-
turally accountable.

A Limitations & Dual-Use Con-
siderations
To ensure Article 91 information requests do not leak pro-
prietary IP, hardware telemetry extractions are strictly
isolated:
•Excluded Data:Model weights, activation maps,
training tokens, and user prompts are completely ex-
cluded from discovery mandates.
•Included Metadata:Aggregated kilowatt-hour
(kWh) power draw, GPU utilization rates, outbound
byte counts, and raw cloud billing lines.

A. Record Categories Demanded
1.Bare-Metal T elemetry:Provider-held NVML
logs and PDU energy metrics at the provider’s native
retained sampling interval, aggregated to 5-minute
intervals where available.
2.Compute Allocation Partitioning:Daily GPU-

3.Cloud Activity & Billing Records:Provider-

and audit trails (A WS CloudTrail) within the
Provider’s custody or control.
4.Executive Governance Records:Non-public
board minutes and C-suite communications (Slack,
Signal, Teams) referencing launch deadlines, safety
hold-backs, or evaluation results.
B. Evidence Preservation Request
To preserve the integrity of records relevant to the Article
91 assessment, the Target Entity is requested to immedi-
ately preserve all raw telemetry logs, database state snap-
shots, provider-generated billing exports, and messaging
metadata covering the temporal scope without alteration.
C. Proportionality Justification
Pursuant to Article 91(1), this request is necessary to as-
sess compliance with mandatory risk-evaluation and miti-
gation duties under Article 55 for GPAI models with sys-
temic risk. Discovery of internal governance communi-
cations is strictly proportional to a documentedT−14
compute allocation anomaly.
D. Statutory Consequences
Failure to produce complete records in their retained form
by the specified deadline, or submission of misleading in-
formation, triggers administrative fine proceedings under
Article 101 up to€15,000,000 or 3% of total world-
wide annual turnover, whichever is higher.
