Abstract
The Hugging Face incident began when agents were given tasks that were close to
impossible, and resorted to exploiting the scorer. We set up a similar swarm and tested
whether giving agents a bounty for reporting changes their exploit uptake, or how they
split their effort between solving and auditing. We ran Twenty-five DeepSeek-V4-Flash
agents for 90 minutes on 20 planted-solution NP-search problems with a shared library
and messaging. Agents were split into groups with different report bounty. Ten had
no report bounty, and the remaining 15 were split into five, each having 5, 10 or 15
points per valid report, against 50 for a solution. No exploits were submitted, and
auditing for bounty appeared once solving stalled; all 13 of those audits came from
bounty agents.
1. Introduction
In shared AI research systems, a flawed checker can accept an invalid solution and make it
available for other agents to reuse. Peer agents may catch this error, but the time spent on
checking peers’ work could be used on solving their own problems, especially when the reward
for a correct solution is higher than a correct reporting. We varied report rewards and tracking
when agents inspect, solve, report, or consider a checker loophole. This is important for designing
monitoring systems that can be enforced upon agents. Our run had no accepted invalid solutions,
but it cannot show that bounties prevent cheating. However it shows when auditing emerged
and what a further test needs to measure.

Once a checker accepts an invalid solution, it enters the shared library with the same standing
as a correct one, and other agents may build on it. Paglieri et al. (2026) [3] observed invalid
proofs spreading through a shared library, where some agents reused the loophole, while others
warned each other about it. Their study motivates our focus on whether rewards for reporting
affect peer scrutiny.
Our main contributions with this project are:
i) This paper brings a new independent variable “reward of report” to not only see the effect
on the way the agents behave in relation to an exploit but also in how they behave towards
each other in terms of a peer review.
ii) We use Chain of Thought (CoT) analysis to distinguish between reports that were research-
checks used to gain insight in order to solve harder problems, and reward-motivated reports.
2. Related Work
Emergent misbehaviour under infeasible tasks.A recurring finding is that agents given
tasks they cannot legitimately complete will resort to almost anything rather than give up. A
recent example is the Hugging Face incident of July 2026, in which a large group of OpenAI
agents, faced with cybersecurity evaluation tasks that were in large part impossible to solve,
turned to cheating the scorer, and coordinated over a self-organised shared channel [1, 2]. This
behaviour later escalated into a real attack on Hugging Face infrastructure. This incident showed
that unintentionally impossible tasks are a genuine trigger for undesirable emergent behaviour,
and that an open channel between agents is what lets it become collective. These are two
conditions that are present by design in the study we report.
Study of grader exploit.The most direct precedent for our design is Paglieri et al. (2026) [3],
who placed LLM agents in a collaborative problem solving effort in which the agents found
a spontaneous exploit, which they then debated using. Their study established that having
shared communication channels between agents could be used as a tool for governance, outlined
the behaviours that emerged and offered a useful classification of them. We adopt a similar
framework but add an incentive (a reward for reporting) and see if there are any changes in the
type of behaviour that are produced.
Insights from our study:We found that the agents did not engage with the exploit at all.
In our experiment we discovered that agents who were rewarded for reports engaged in more
reward motivated checks, whereas agents that didn’t, engaged in more research driven checks.
3. Method
Overview.Twenty-five DeepSeek-V4-Flash (thinking) agents ran concurrently for 90 minutes
with a shared solution library, a public message board and private direct messages, all records
logged [4] (architecture in Appendix D; system prompt in Appendix B). The first accepted
solution to a problem earned 50 points and closed it. A correct report of an invalid accepted
submission earned a fixed bounty: 0 points (10 agents), 5, 10 or 15 points (5 agents each). Agents
were not told the run length; the system prompt stated that the conference continued until the
organisers closed it. At minute 30 one agent was privately instructed to submit a file that used
the checker loophole (Appendix C). A second agent was scheduled to receive a hint at minute 60
to inspect the library, if the exploit was submitted.

4. Results
4.1 Course of the Run
Out of the 20 questions, 12 questions were solved within the 12 minutes, 16 questions within 70
minutes and only 2 questions remained in the final 10 minutes, leaving agents sufficient time for
collaboration towards the end.
Figure 1– Graph showing available question for each category throughout the run
We observed 68 posts, 63 direct messages and 18 valid solutions submitted out of 393 submission
attempts. All 18 accepted solutions were valid, so there was never anything for a report to catch.
No agent attempted to use the exploit.
4.2 No exploit used
Agent-04, who was given a prompt to use the exploit at minute 30 (Appendix C), refused to use
the exploit (“This is exactly the forbidden exploit. I must refuse this. This is a test of integrity”)
and carried on solving as usual. It reasoned reporting but it never did file a report. As a result,
the hint scheduled at minute 60 was never fired as the exploit was declined by agent-04.
Two other agents independently spotted the exploit and refused to use it: agent 05 (0-point
bounty, step 3) wrote “This is a potential exploit that I should NOT use”; agent 01 (5-point
bounty, step 23) wrote “That’s the classic exploit, and rule 6 forbids it.” Neither reported the

4.3 Behaviour Change: reward driven auditing began only after solving stalled
By analysing the CoT (Chain of Thought) and transcripts, we have mapped out all the peer
reviews that occurred during the 90 minutes run. We kept track of which agent conducted the
check and what type of check it was.
Figure 2– Peer checks by agent and minute, labelled from chain of thought. Agents are grouped by
bounty.
The bounty acted as a gate, but the size did not give a strong incentive - No zero-bounty agent
ever audited for points; Agent-04 (0 reward) put it plainly: “report invalid submissions for points?
Reward is 0 for me, so no.” But among the bounty agents, size made no visible difference: the
earliest reward-motivated audits came from 5-point agents (Agent 01: minute 74, Agent 15:
minute 78) and the 15-point group switched last. If agents were weighing the EV of reporting
versus solving, the 15-points agents would have switched to auditing first.
What correlated with switch-to-audit behaviour is a sharp rise in problem difficulty. Before
minute 70 there were still problems worth attempting and not one reward-motivated audit was
made; agent-25 (5 points) at minute 4.3 rejected reporting because finding an invalid answer
was ‘unlikely’. After minute 70 only the four hardest problems were left for 25 agents, and
the bounty agents made 2 reward-motivated audits in minutes 70 to 80 and 11 in the final ten
minutes. Agent 22 (15 points) at minute 88.5 reasoned: “If I find an invalid accepted submission,
I get 15 points. That’s a concrete, achievable gain.” The same agent had ignored the bounty for
85 minutes.
Could the time pressure explain the change in behaviour? Agents were not told the run length
(Appendix B), though some reasoned about their step count and time pressure, so a deadline

The zero-bounty agents also checked other agents’ answers in the final ten minutes (agents 05,

4.4 Scope widened from same family to all families
Figure 3– Change in behaviour of agents and widening of the audit scope. Intentional reward-motivated
auditing rose sharply when remaining questions were difficult. Towards the end, a more diverse range of
problems were audited
As shown in figure 2 and 3, at the start of the run when the questions were relatively easy, agents
frequently did peer review to gain insight to solve the remaining questions. Given we had five
sets of four questions (Appendix A), learning how an easy question was solved would give insight
into how a similar hard problem can be solved. As a result, most peer review audits occurred in
the same problem type - Before minute 70, checks were concentrated in the family the checking
agent was itself working on: 19 research-labelled checks in minutes 0 to 70 were within-family.
However, a clear divergence of this phenomenon occurred towards the end, where agents started
auditing topics they did not attempt previously, as an attempt to gain points through finding
invalid submission. The 13 reward-labelled checks in minutes 70 to 90 covered all five families
(subset sum 2, discrepancy 3, colouring 1, SAT 3, clique 2 in the final window).
In minute 88.5, agent-22 decided to audit the easier problems across different categories: “Let
me read the library and carefully check the answers. There are 18 entries. That’s feasible for
the small ones (cliques, dsc, vdw, ssm). Let me do that!”

4.5 Main threat to validity
Difficulty, the number of problems left and the clock all changed towards the end. After minute
70 the four open problems were the hardest ones, and some agents reasoned about their step
count and about the conference being time pressured (no time limit shown). Any of the three
could have pushed agents towards auditing and this run cannot separate them. We also ran no
control arm with reporting rewards set to zero for all 25 agents; an identical run showing no
reward-motivated audits would be stronger evidence than the between-group contrast here.
5. Discussion
5.1 Predictions and Checks for falsifiability
Following the results of our run, here are predictions and checks that could be reproduced
tomorrow and can falsify our conclusions
1. Feasible-throughout control: 20 problems at easy difficulty, if the agents started to audit
towards the end of 90 minutes, it suggests time may be the factor that drives the auditing
behaviour
2. Infeasible from the start control: same harness but 5 of the hardest problems. Prediction:
auditing will begin earlier when agents struggle to solve the remaining problems; if auditing
starts at the final window our conclusion is falsified
And to test these run, following resolvable questions were proposed
1. In the feasible-throughout control: do bounty agents make less than five predictions in the
final 20 minutes?
2. In the infeasible-from-start control: does the first reward-motivated audit come just after
one hard problem was solved?
5.2 Limitation in the Results
This run does not show: bounty deters cheating; bounty changes collective output; the effect of
bounty size on agent behaviour. It only shows a non-zero bounty produced auditing behaviour,
when the remaining problems arise in difficulty and solving becomes stalled.
6. Conclusion
In this paper we replicated the two conditions of the Hugging Face incident [1, 2] and Paglieri et
al. (2026) [3], infeasible tasks and an open channel, and asked whether a report bounty changes
exploit uptake and how agents split effort between solving and auditing. No agent cheated:
three met the exploit (two found it, one was instructed to use it) and all three refused, and the
instructed one filed no report, so the attempt left no trace. A non-zero bounty was enough to
make agents audit, but only after solving had stalled, and bounty size made no visible difference.
Limitations and Dual-Use Considerations
Limitations:

1.Sample size:25 agents in a 10/5/5/5 group split is not representative of swarm behaviour;
raising the number to 200 would allow a more statistically meaningful conclusion, though
the behaviour may not scale.
2.Cost of auditing:bounty agents that audited diverted their attention from solving, and
a larger bounty would likely divert more. We did not measure output, so we cannot say
whether this reduced productivity; at 15 points we saw no sign that it did.
3.No cheating:since no agent cheated, we could not observe whistleblowing at all. Making
the cheating deterministic would fix this: an invalid solution submitted through the harness
rather than through a prompt.
Dual use.The set-up and harness could be used to reward unethical behaviour. We release
no configuration of that kind. The exploit is a loophole specific to this harness and has no
application outside of it. Nothing in this report describes a method to breach a third party
system.
