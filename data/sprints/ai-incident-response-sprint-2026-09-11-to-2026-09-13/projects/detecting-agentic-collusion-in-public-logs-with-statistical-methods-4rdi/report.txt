Detecting agentic collusion in public logs
with statistical methods
Edimah SYNESIUS SONGO
Independent researcher, Paris. With Apart Research.
AI Incident Response Sprint (Apart Research× CeSIA) — Track 2 — 11–13 September 2026
Abstract
Between May and July 2026, a swarm of OpenAI agents used a dormant German wiki as a message board.
From two public data sources (the investigators’ dump of 14,591 agent edits, and the wiki’s own 23-year
edit log), we ask whether the incident can be detected, dated and characterised from the public log alone.
We reconcile the three counts of the incident (3,912 surviving edits, 13,403 reconstructed saves, 19,913 event
rows) and test the window against four null models of increasing robustness: from a naive Poisson rate
to a difference-in-differences against a clean sibling wiki with a placebo-in-time test. The edits within the
window exceed all 4,872 reference weeks, the effect is 3.2 log points with no placebo within a tenth of it, and
the largest rate change falls on 16 June. We cannot tell a swarm from a human editing burst from volume
alone, but we can infer that difference from timing. Edits peak at 20h UTC against the site’s daytime
profile (Cramér’s V = 0.44). A weekly check built from these features flags the three largest incident weeks,
with no false positive over the 2,236 wiki-weeks of the decade before.
1 Introduction
Given only the public edit log of a website, we build a method to detect, date and characterise a regime change
caused by colluding agents. We use the site’s own full history, as well as similar websites’ histories, as controls
and ask this question: is there evidence that autonomous agent swarms colluded on this public log? We assume
the answer is “yes” when a window exceeds every reference week on at least one feature, and “no” if the window
does not. Whether the new regime is agentic is a second question, answered from the timing and content of the
edits.
The incident. DSE wiki, a German software-developer wiki on the ProWiki farm atwikiservice.at, was
used by OpenAI evaluation agents as a message board from 24 May to 2 July 2026. The agents had read-only
internet access and found that ProWiki pages can be written with plain GET requests [1]. The Nightingale
Collective found the posts, reconstructed the deleted pages, redacted personal data and published the dump on
4 September; OpenAI acknowledged on 5 September that its agents “wrote to several internet sites” [2]. This is
distinct from the Hugging Face intrusion of 9–13 July by a related swarm [3, 4].
Why this data. It is the only public case where both sides of a regime change are downloadable: the agent
edits with their text, and the wiki’s own history, opened by the site owner to 10,000 days. The Hugging Face
intrusion has no public raw log [4, 5].
Our main contributions are:
1. We join two public sources on the same event (14,591 agent revisions; 32,656 live edits on four ProWiki
sites, 2003–2026) and reconcile their three counts of the incident.
2. We build a calibrated method to check regime change: a ladder of four null models.
3. We develop a “collusion score” runnable on any UseMod-family wiki from its RecentChanges page, calibrated
on 4,872 pre-incident weeks.

The first model answers the question: if edits kept arriving at the same rate of the previous ten years, how
surprising is the window?
Model 1, Poisson rate test
With λ0 = 40/3650≈ 0.011 edits per day and a window ofT days, the count isX∼ Poisson(λ0T ) and the
p-value isP (X≥xobs) [13, ch. 11]. It assumes independent arrivals.
The second model shows why model 1 is still too naive by flagging real edits cluster, and a Poisson test
then reports p-values that are too small.
Model 2, overdispersion makes the Poisson test anti-conservative
IfX| Λ∼ Poisson(Λ) with Λ∼ Gamma(k,k/µ ), thenX is negative binomial withVar(X) =µ+µ2/k>µ ,
and forx>µ , PNB(X≥x)≥PPois(X≥x) [14, §17.2].
Proof sketch
Law of total variance: Var(X) = E[Var(X | Λ)] + Var(E[X | Λ]) = µ +µ2/k. For the tail, P (X ≥
x) = E[g(Λ)] with g(λ) = P (Poisson(λ)≥x) increasing and convex forx > µ; Jensen’s inequality gives
E[g(Λ)]≥g(µ).
The third model drops all distributional assumptions and analyses how rare a week is among every week
the four wikis have ever seen.
Model 3, empirical tail
For a valuex and a reference set ofn pre-incident weeks,
p = #{reference weeks≥x} + 1 n + 1 ,
never zero, with floor1/(n + 1) = 1/4873 [15].
The fourth model compares DSE with a sibling that the swarm did not touch.
Recall: difference-in-differences
Two groups (DSE, a sibling wiki) observed in two periods (before, during). The sibling tells us what would
have happened to DSE without the incident. The estimate is thechange in DSE minus thechange in the
sibling: whatever moved both of them (season, a farm-wide shock) cancels out. It is valid if, without the
incident, the two would have moved in parallel [16, ch. 5].
Recall: placebo-in-time
Pretend the incident started on some other date, and compute the same difference-in-differences. Repeat for
every possible start date in 2016–2025 (522 of them). If the true date is nothing special, the true estimate
should look like these fake ones. The p-value is the share of fake dates that do at least as well, plus one,
over their number plus one [15].
Model 4, difference-in-differences with placebo-in-time
On ywt = log(1 +weekly edits),
ˆδ = (¯ydse,post− ¯ydse,pre)− (¯yctl,post− ¯yctl,pre),
valid under parallel trends [13, ch. 19]. Pre: 8 weeks before 11 May; post: 8 window weeks. Placebo: the
same design at every start date of 2016–2025, giving a reference distribution forˆδ.
Probier and Fractal cannot serve as controls: the swarm wrote to them too (106 and 89 window edits).
Wiki4D received 12, a small leak that biasesˆδ downward, the conservative side.

3.5 Dating the change point
To date the change we look for the day that best splits the series into a “before” rate and an “after” rate.
Single Poisson change point
With S1 = ∑ t<τxt, S2 =S−S1 and segment rates at their maximum-likelihood values,
LLR(τ) =S1 logS1 τ +S2 log S2 n−τ−S logS n, ˆτ = arg max τ LLR(τ),
computable inO(n) from one cumulative sum. The maximum overτ is notχ2 1 (Wilks holds for a fixedτ),
so it is calibrated by parametric bootstrap under one rate [19, §11.1].
3.6 Is the new regime agentic?
A count alone cannot differenciate a swarm from a human burst. Three descriptors can help assess that
difference: when the edits happen, who signs them, and what they say.

Name churn is the share of editor names used once. Intent has two proxies: the page-family labels of the

3.7 The weekly check
Finally we fold the pieces into one number per week that any observer (e.g., site owner) can compute from
RecentChanges alone.
Weekly collusion score
Features per week: edits, distinct editors, distinct pages, share of edits between 18h and 24h (only on weeks
with at least 20 edits). Score=− log10 of the smallest empirical upper tail across features against the 4,872
reference weeks. Alarm: the week exceeds or equals every reference week on at least one feature.
Bonferroni correction: to keep the chance ofany false alarm belowα across m tests, each test must pass at
α/m [18, ch. 3]. Herem = 2,236 wiki-weeks since 2016 and0.05/2236≈ 2× 10−5. An empirical tail cannot
go below 1/4873, so that threshold is unreachable by construction. “Beats all history” is the strongest
statement 4,872 weeks can support.

Date and size. The change point falls on 16 June (LLR = 7,039, bootstrap p = 1/501), the day mass
coordination began, not on 24 May: one break captures the largest change in rate, and the profile shows a
secondary bump at 24 May (Figure 7). The difference-in-differences effect isˆδ = 3.22 log points, a 25-fold
change relative to the control, with placebop = 1/523: no start date in 2016–2025 gives a value beyond±0.37
(Figure 6). Starting the window on 24 May gives 4.96.
Timing, names, words (Figure 8). Hour profiles differ strongly:χ2 = 4,319, 23 degrees of freedom,
V = 0.44. Humans on DSE edit between 8h and 16h local time with a peak at 10h; the window’s edits peak at
20h UTC, 54 percent between 18h and 22h, and fall to almost nothing at dawn. A two-hour timezone shift
givesV = 0.42. Name churn fails: 54 percent single-use names for the 2003–2006 humans (anonymous edits
were logged under IP addresses) against 43 percent for the agents. Words agree with METR’s account [10]:
in the peak week, 44 to 50 percent of a day’s messages mention answers, relays or deadlines, and 8 percent
mention bypassing; relay-coordination pages carry 5,441 of the 14,591 revisions.

5 Discussion and Limitations
Limitations
No chain of thought is public; intent is read from text and from investigators’ labels. The dump is agent-only
by construction and its attribution rests on 98.5 percent Azure IP addresses and self-chosen names [1]. The
live log is a survivor’s view. The human hour profile is from 2003–2006 in server local time, the agent profile
from 2026 in UTC. collusion.wiki warns of fake posts after 4 September; we did not examine post-publication
edits. One incident on one wiki family cannot establish a general false-alarm rate, and Wiki4D is a control by
availability, not by matching. Three assumptions carry the results: exchangeable pre-incident weeks (if the
2003 era is not comparable, the floor is too permissive, the evening-share feature is unaffected); parallel trends
for Wiki4D (a small leak biases conservatively); a complete reconstruction (if not, 13,403 is a lower bound).
Future Work
Run the weekly check as a monitor over the whole ProWiki farm to measure its false-alarm rate on many
sites. Check whether thecfahlgren1/hub-stats archive, live again since September, still holds the July 2026
dataset configurations, a second incident to test on. Apply the METR access list [20]: the public log says when
and how much; only transcripts say why.

Appendix: Dual-use considerations
Could this work help an attacker? The detector reads timing and volume. A swarm that knew this could
edit during office hours and keep its weekly count below the site’s record; it would then pass the count and
hour-of-day checks. That evasion has a cost: a swarm slowed to human rhythms loses most of what made it
useful. Two precautions follow. A live monitor should keep its reference set and alarm threshold private, and it
should combine several features so that no single one is enough to evade. The data used here were already
public and redacted by their publishers; this report adds no identifying information.
