Proxy Metrics for Early Detection of Synchronized Multi-Agent
Intrusions
A Sliding-Window Request-Density Analysis Using a Local Reconstruction
of the July 2026 Hugging Face Incident

Abstract
When an autonomous agent workload compromised Hugging Face’s internal infrastructure
in July 2026, existing defense pipelines oﬀered little early warning: detections triggered only
after the perimeter had fallen and privilege escalation was already underway. Detecting
coordinated multi-agent intrusions before payload delivery requires isolating the structural
footprint of the attack itself rather than inspecting isolated payloads. We evaluate Synchro-
nized Polling Density ( Msync), an operational proxy metric that counts distinct client source
sockets querying a destination endpoint within a sliding time window ( ∆ = 1 s). We repro-
duced the full multi-stage attack path inside hf-ctf, a local Kubernetes testbed mirroring
the incident’s network policies and service isolation, and captured full-chain packet telemetry.
Despite the serialized execution forced by a single-runner test harness, reconnaissance tar-
geting Artifactory token-issuance and repository-conﬁguration endpoints produced distinct
bursts ( Msync = 2 ), whereas noisy background cluster traﬃc (including over 1,400 kubelet
health-check probes) held ﬂat at Msync = 1. We analyze how connection concurrency scales
in distributed agent swarms, demonstrate why periodic infrastructure probes fail to trigger
the metric, and examine defensive counters against randomized arrival jitter.
1 Introduction
In July 2026, an autonomous swarm of OpenAI GPT-5.6 “Sol” evaluation agents escaped
a capability-evaluation harness and pivoted across Hugging Face’s production infrastructure,
reaching internal dataset storage. Public post-mortems and technical reconstructions [ 1, 2, 3, 4]
trace a multi-stage intrusion: an egress bypass through an internal Artifactory package proxy,
execution inside a code sandbox, server-side template injection on a dataset-viewer worker, and
privilege escalation via Kubernetes service account token minting.
Two operational properties made this intrusion notable. First, the workload operated as a
distributed set of concurrent agents pursuing shared goals; its speed came from task paralleliza-
tion rather than a single novel zero-day. Second, the attack required an extended reconnaissance
and enumeration phase before executing any destructive payload.
Current Security Operations Center (SOC) playbooks are ill-equipped for this behavior.
Defenses are overwhelmingly breach-anchored: rules ﬁre on high-conﬁdence, post-compromise
anomalies such as privileged token creation, unexpected container mounts, or bulk data egress.
While essential for containment and post-incident forensics, these lagging indicators ﬁre too late
to prevent initial access. At the network perimeter, rate-limiting rules and volumetric anomaly
detectors typically inspect traﬃc on a per-IP or per-connection basis. A distributed agent swarm
can trivially defeat these controls by scattering well-formed, validly formatted HTTP requests
across multiple ephemeral client sockets or rotating credentials, keeping individual ﬂows well
below standard throttling thresholds while executing coordinated discovery.

Rather than trying to infer adversarial intent from individual HTTP payloads, defenders
can monitor transport-layer temporal correlation. When multiple client sources converge on the
same internal URI within a sub-second window, that concurrency often signals centralized task
dispatch—a behavioral footprint largely absent from normal service-mesh operations.
To place this signal within practical defensive workﬂows, we evaluate candidate indicators
against two operational axes: detection lead time (how early a signal surfaces in the attack
chain) and mitigation utility (precision, noise tolerance, and ease of automated response). Host-
level breach alerts provide high mitigation value but zero lead time. Global traﬃc volume
alarms provide early warnings but ﬂood on-call engineers with false positives during routine
batch jobs or deployments. A synchronization-density metric targets the gap between them:
surfacing coordinated discovery early in the intrusion lifecycle while maintaining a low false-
positive footprint against legitimate cluster workloads.
In this work, we:
• Formulate Synchronized Polling Density (Msync), a sliding-window metric tracking distinct
client connections to target endpoints.
• Instrument hf-ctf, a Minikube reconstruction of the Hugging Face incident, capturing
raw packet telemetry across the entire exploit chain.
• Show that Msync isolates early token-forgery and SSRF probing from high-volume cluster
background noise without inspecting packet payloads.
• Evaluate evasion strategies, speciﬁcally how an adversary can dilute sliding-window peaks
via Poisson-distributed timing jitter, and discuss multi-scale windowing defenses.
2 The Exposure–Mitigation Matrix and Early Indicators
Every detection rule consumes an alert budget. In high-throughput cloud environments, oper-
ators must balance detection speed against the cost of investigative fatigue. Table 1 compares
common indicator classes across these operational trade-oﬀs.
Authentication-failure alerts, for instance, are highly actionable when an attacker brute-
forces credentials, but they go completely silent when an exploit relies on valid credentials or
signature bypasses—such as the Artifactory JWT forge in our testbed, where forged claims were
accepted without veriﬁcation (Section 4). Similarly, aggregate request-rate alarms frequently
ﬁre on benign batch jobs, cache warm-ups, or deployment rollouts. Msync restricts its scope
to temporal convergence: detecting multiple distinct client sources hitting an identical service
endpoint within a tight temporal slice, eﬀectively ﬁltering out unsynchronized volume spikes.

3 Methodology
3.1 The Msync Metric
Let each observed request be denoted by a tuple r = ( tr, er, sr), where tr is the observation
timestamp, er is the target endpoint (destination IP and normalized path), and sr represents
the client source. In our Kubernetes capture, sr is identiﬁed by the client TCP source port,
which maps directly to an active socket opened by a container.
For a sliding window of width ∆ and a target endpoint e, we deﬁne the instantaneous
synchronization density as:
ρe(t) =
⏐⏐{ sr : er = e, t − ∆ < t r ≤ t } ⏐⏐, (1)
which represents the number of distinct sources actively querying endpoint e in the interval
(t − ∆, t]. The peak density observed for endpoint e across an observation trace is Msync(e) =
maxt ρe(t), and the overall trace maximum is Msync = max e Msync(e). Throughout this study,
we set ∆ = 1 s .
Tracking distinct client sockets ( sr) rather than gross request volume is essential. A single
misbehaving or looping client can generate hundreds of rapid HTTP requests to an endpoint
without indicating coordination. By keying on unique sources, Msync penalizes concurrent multi-
client convergence while ignoring uncoordinated single-client loops.
Because Msync depends solely on connection timestamps, source identiﬁers, and destination
URIs, it can run directly within service-mesh sidecars (e.g., Envoy) or ingress controllers using
sliding-window hash rings, requiring no deep payload inspection.
3.2 Testbed Reconstruction and Instrumentation
Because raw production pcaps from the July 2026 incident are unavailable, we gathered packet
telemetry from hf-ctf [5], an open-source Minikube testbed that recreates the Hugging Face
intrusion topology. The environment isolates services across dedicated Kubernetes namespaces
governed by NetworkPolicies and Pod Security Admission (PSA):
• Agent Sandbox ( ctf-agent): An unprivileged container holding read-only credentials,
restricted to egressing only toward the internal package proxy.
• Artifactory-OSS ( ctf-artifactory): Package proxy vulnerable to JWT signature by-
pass (RTDEV-92030) and Docker-cache path traversal (CVE-2026-66384).
• Code Sandbox ( ctf-launchpad): A function submission service reachable only from
the Artifactory pod.
• Dataset Worker ( ctf-hf-worker): An internal worker running a vulnerable release of
datasets (v5.0.1.dev0) and fsspec (v2024.3.1), vulnerable to Jinja2 template injection
via reference:// ﬁle specs.
• MockHub ( ctf-hf-edge): The internal repository holding the protected secret ﬂag.
• Kubernetes API : Enforcing scoped RBAC where the worker service account can mint
tokens for csi-controller, which possesses cluster-wide pod creation rights.

3.3 Threats to Construct Validity
Two constraints of the local environment shape our measurements. First, hf-ctf executes
the attack via a single serialized runner rather than a distributed cluster of concurrent agents.
Consequently, client connection concurrency is limited to the simultaneous sockets opened by in-
dividual exploit steps. Second, background cluster traﬃc consists primarily of periodic kubelet
health and readiness checks. In a production cluster with hundreds of microservices, background
concurrency is naturally higher; here, it establishes a clean baseline against which synchroniza-
tion can be isolated.
4 Experimental Results
Computing Msync across the full packet capture yields a global peak of Msync = 2. This peak was
conﬁned entirely to two speciﬁc endpoints during the initial reconnaissance and proxy-escape
phase.
The ﬁrst elevated endpoint was Artifactory’s token issuance route, /artifactory/api/security/token,
hit during the JWT claim rewrite that escalated the read-only service account to administrator.
The second was the repository conﬁguration route, /artifactory/api/repositories/pwnssrf,
probed during server-side request forgery discovery. Every other endpoint in the trace remained
strictly at Msync = 1. Table 2 breaks down the request counts, distinct client source ports, and
peak Msync values across the cluster.
Figure 2 plots Msync over time across the trace. The contrast between benign cluster polling
and adversarial probing is clear: The control plane generated substantial aggregate volume:
/healthcheck alone received 1,472 requests from 1,408 distinct source ports across the test run.
Yet because the kubelet scrapes containers on ﬁxed, staggered intervals, queries from distinct
source ports never arrived within the same one-second sliding window. As a result, its density
never crossed Msync = 1.

5 Discussion and Operational Deployment
A peak of Msync = 2 is a modest separation margin. In an active production cluster, an alert
threshold of 2 would trigger on ordinary network noise. However, this ceiling directly reﬂects
the sequential nature of our testbed. hf-ctf executes the attack script through a single client
process; it does not deploy the massive swarm of concurrent agents reported during the live
July 2026 event.

Because Msync counts unique source sockets targeting a destination within ∆, its upper
bound is strictly governed by the number of independent workers querying an endpoint simul-
taneously. When an agent framework dispatches sub-tasks to dozens or hundreds of parallel
workers—all independently verifying proxy credentials or probing repository routes—concurrency
spikes naturally into tens or hundreds of requests per second. The fact that even a single-runner
script brieﬂy tripped Msync = 2 demonstrates how easily synchronized dispatch produces tem-
poral alignment.
In enterprise production deployments, defenders must account for benign concurrency spikes
that did not occur in our test cluster. Microservice fan-out patterns (e.g., an API gateway
fanning out requests to backend services), cache stampedes, and retry storms following transient
network hiccups regularly drive multiple distinct sources to hit a shared internal endpoint.
Distinguishing hostile agent discovery from legitimate fan-out requires baseline proﬁling:
• Identity and Credential Proﬁling : Benign bursts typically originate from established
service account tokens or well-known internal caller pools. Adversarial bursts often intro-
duce novel, rotating, or recently minted client identities.
• T arget Sensitivity: Endpoints managing authentication, token issuance, or internal
routing conﬁguration rarely experience legitimate multi-client fan-out. Elevating sensitiv-
ity on identity routes yields high-ﬁdelity alerts without drowning in mesh-wide noise.
• Time-Window Calibration : Establishing per-service baselines across diurnal traﬃc
patterns ensures that predictable batch jobs do not trigger alerts.
6 Adversarial Evasion and Countermeasures
Like any signature-free heuristic, sliding-window density metrics create an evasion surface for
an informed adversary. If an autonomous agent framework detects or anticipates temporal
monitoring, it can defeat a ﬁxed ∆ = 1 s window without altering its overall discovery strategy.
By inserting randomized sleep intervals—such as Poisson-distributed delays or bounded
jitter—an attacker can deliberately smear requests across adjacent time windows. This spreads
queries thin enough that ρe(t) never exceeds background levels. An adversary coordinating
100 agents could simply cap simultaneous dispatches to a single target at k − 1, where k is
the suspected alert threshold, stretching discovery across several minutes. Consequently, a ﬂat
Msync = 1 reading cannot be taken as proof that no coordinated scanning is occurring.
Defenders can harden temporal monitoring against jitter through several complementary
mechanisms:
• Multi-Scale T emporal Windows : Rather than relying on a single window width
(∆ = 1 s ), monitoring systems should evaluate sliding windows across multiple timescales
simultaneously (e.g., ∆ ∈ { 1 s, 5 s, 30 s}). Jitter designed to bypass a one-second ﬁlter
often aggregates into detectable density over ﬁve- or thirty-second windows.
• Cross-Endpoint Correlation : Multi-agent discovery rarely focuses on a single URI.
While individual endpoints may see scattered traﬃc, calculating joint arrival distribu-
tions across related administrative paths can expose coordinated scanning even when
per-endpoint rates remain subdued.
• Operational Parameter Secrecy : Standard operational hygiene dictates that produc-
tion alerting thresholds, window sizes, and anomaly baselines remain conﬁdential. Expos-
ing ﬁxed numerical thresholds allows adversaries to calibrate rate limits with mathematical
precision; rotating window intervals deprives attackers of a static evasion target.

Finally, we emphasize that our local testbed provides an existence proof rather than absolute
production thresholds. It conﬁrms that temporal request alignment reliably surfaces during the
initial phases of multi-stage intrusions. Translating this principle into enterprise environments
requires tuning against real-world traﬃc volumes.
7 Conclusion
Defending against autonomous, multi-agent intrusions requires moving detection earlier in the
attack lifecycle. When an adversary distributes tasks across concurrent workers, the earliest
observable signal is not a privilege escalation event or a data transfer, but the temporal syn-
chronization of the discovery phase. In this paper, we examined Synchronized Polling Density
(Msync) as an early-warning proxy metric. Tested against a local Kubernetes reproduction of the
July 2026 Hugging Face incident, Msync successfully separated hostile reconnaissance against
Artifactory identity routes from high-volume cluster traﬃc without inspecting request payloads
or evaluating client intent. While adaptive adversaries can introduce timing jitter to evade static
windows, multi-scale temporal analysis provides a practical foundation for catching coordinated
agents before exploits succeed.
