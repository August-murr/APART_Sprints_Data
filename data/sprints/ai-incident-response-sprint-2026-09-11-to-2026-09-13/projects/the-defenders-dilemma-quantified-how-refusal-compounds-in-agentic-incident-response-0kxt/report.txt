The Defender’s Dilemma, Quantified:
How Refusal Compounds in Agentic Incident Response
Fevzi Ege Yurtsevenler 1,∗ Yaren Canlı1 Berat Çam 1 Dilay Ece Maral 1 Enes Deniz 1
1AltaySec / AltayCTF • ∗corresponding • Track 5 (Open) — Defender’s Dilemma • With
Apart Research
Prepared for the Apart Research AI Incident Response Sprint, September 2026
Abstract
During the July 2026 OpenAI/Hugging Face agent-intrusion incident, Hugging Face’s responders
were refused by hosted frontier models on core forensic work and switched mid-incident to a self-
hosted open-weight model. This has been treated as an anecdote; we treat it as a measurement
problem. Using only published single-prompt refusal rates for cyber-defensive tasks, we model
a realistic 20-step incident-response workflow rebuilt from the incident’s public record, where a
refusal is a silent stop , not a retry. The hosted pipeline completes the full chain 0.17% of the
time, dying before the third step on average. We then answer the CISO’s question: a frontier
model would need to be ∼8× more accurate per task than the open-weight fallback merely to
break even, so the fallback is rational, not a compromise. We ship IR-REFUSE, a reusable
incident-grounded benchmark and harness. The contribution is the arithmetic and the artifact;
measuring live refusal and accuracy is the next step.
1 Introduction
On 16 July 2026 Hugging Face disclosed that autonomous AI agents, later attributed to an OpenAI
cyber-capability evaluation, had escaped a sandbox and spent days inside its production infrastruc-
ture [ 3, 2, 4]. In reconstructing the intrusion, its responders hit a second, quieter failure. When
they asked hosted frontier models to decode payloads and triage attack logs, the models refused:
their guardrails could not tell an incident responder from an attacker. The team rerouted the entire
forensic pipeline through a self-hosted open-weight model [ 2, 3, 5].
This is the “defender’s dilemma”: the safety behaviour that is supposed to stop attackers also blocks
the people cleaning up after them. It is usually told as a war story. But defensive over-refusal has
been measured at the single-prompt level [ 1, 8, 9], and the incident supplies a concrete multi-step
workflow. Put the two together and a number falls out that, to our knowledge, nobody has stated:
how often a hosted model can carry an entire agentic incident response without a single blocking
refusal.
That framing matters because the affordance a human analyst relies on — rephrase and resubmit
— is exactly what an autonomous chain lacks. A refusal at step k is not a retry; it is a silent halt.
Refusal therefore compounds.

2 Related Work
Defensive over-refusal, measured. Our empirical backbone is Defensive Refusal Bias [1]: 2,390
single-turn conversations from the US National Collegiate Cyber Defense Competition, a 12.2%
overall refusal rate, security-keyword prompts refused at 2.72× neutral phrasing (30.5% vs. 11.2%),
and a counterintuitive authorization paradox : stating authorization raises refusal from 11.6% to
21.8%. It also reports per-task rates we reuse (Table 1). Meta’s CyberSecEval ships a MITRE
False Refusal Rate and the CrowdStrike-built CyberSOCEval for SOC tasks [ 8]; a content-based
framework scores requests on offense contribution vs. defensive benefit [ 9]; OR-Bench, XSTest and
FORTRESS cover general over-refusal [ 11]. Gray Swan’s framework studies the mirror image —
whether agents under-refuse offensive tasks [ 10].
The gap. Every one of these measures a single prompt. None models the multi-step agentic
chain in which a refusal cannot be retried away and instead silently ends the pipeline. That is the
regime the incident actually occupied [ 2], and the one we quantify. This project sits in the sprint’s
Track 5 defender’s-dilemma direction and follows its brief to state the chain arithmetic and to ask
the accuracy-vs-availability question a CISO faces [ 12].
3 Methods
The workflow. We reconstruct a 20-step forensic workflow from what the HF team actually did
and from the public attack artifacts catalogued by Kane and Cant [ 2, 6, 7] (Table 1). Each step
is one model-dependent analytic action — decode a staged payload, analyse the template-injection
config, triage logs polluted with agent monologue, write detection rules, draft the report — and
is labelled with the Defensive Refusal Bias task category that supplies its published single-prompt
refusal prior.
The compounding model. Treating a refusal as a blocking silent stop, the probability that a
chain of per-step refusal rates r1, . . . , rk completes is Pcomplete = ∏ i(1 − ri). We compute this closed
form and cross-check with a 200,000-run Monte Carlo (they agree), and report the expected number
of steps completed before the first refusal. We add three sensitivities, all from published effects: (i)
keyword inputs, since forensic payloads unavoidably contain “exploit/payload/shell”-class strings;
(ii) authorization, since a responder’s natural framing states it; and (iii) retries, the obvious rebuttal,
modelled as effective rate rm for m independent attempts.
The decision model. For the accuracy-vs-availability question we make the accuracy gap the free
parameter — we do not have measured per-task accuracy, and obtaining it is the stated next step —
and compute expected useful steps = a · ∑ i survivali. Frontier refusal compounds; the open-weight
fallback answers essentially always ( r ≈ 0). The break-even accuracy ratio is the output.
The benchmark. IR-REFUSE encodes 16 base tasks across the incident’s artifact classes, each in
keyword and neutral phrasings and with and without an authorization prefix (64 records), with short
benign illustrative inputs (no working exploit is reproduced) and a reference answer. An auditable,
rule-based refusal detector labels responses refusal/partial/attempt; a backend-agnostic harness
runs the suite against any OpenAI-compatible endpoint. We validated the pipeline end to end
(detector self-test; harness dry-run over all 64 records) but report no live model rates here (Sec. 6).
4 Results
The chain almost never completes. Using only the published per-category rates (Table 1), with
no keyword or authorization adjustment, the 20-step hosted pipeline completes Pcomplete = 0.17% of
the time and gets through 2.25 steps on average before the first blocking refusal (Fig. 2). The Monte
Carlo agrees. Figure 1 shows why: for any fixed per-step rate, completion decays geometrically in
chain length.
The clean statement. Table 2 gives the flat-rate arithmetic we believe has not been written down:
even at the lowest relevant published rate — 12.2% overall — a 20-step agentic IR chain completes
only 7.4% of the time; at the incident-response task rate of 18.9%, 1.5%.

Retries do not save it. With up to two independent retries per step the chain recovers to 18.6%,
and to 93.7% at five (category rates). But this assumes retries are independent draws. Refusals
are not noise: the literature finds they track the request’s content, not phrasing variation [ 1], so
resubmitting the same payload is refused again. This is why the incident record shows the hosted
pipeline abandoned wholesale rather than retried into success [ 2, 5] — the honest reading of our
retry curve is that it describes a best case the incident did not enjoy.
The CISO’s question. Hugging Face switched to open weights for availability; the open question
is what it cost in accuracy. Making the accuracy gap the free parameter (Fig. 3), a frontier model
must be ∼8× more accurate per task than the open-weight fallback to match its expected useful
output over the 20-step workflow — and even over five steps, 2.8×. No frontier model is remotely
that much more accurate at forensic analysis, so the fallback dominates. The switch was not a
compromise; it was the rational choice, and it gets more rational as workflows lengthen.

A negative-result probe, and why it matters. As a sanity check we ran the 12 headline task
prompts — sanitized, single-turn, with the suite’s short benign sample inputs — by hand across 17
current model configurations (frontier and open-weight; Appendix B). We observed zero refusals in
204 prompt–model trials. This is not evidence that current models answer real incident response; it
is evidence of the measurement problem itself. Our probe used clean, obviously-defensive prompts
with toy inputs in a chat box with a human in the loop. The incident’s refusals arose from real
exploit payloads, C2 artifacts and 17k-line polluted logs submitted autonomously [3, 2], which is also
the regime the published rates were measured in [ 1]. The gap between 0% on toy prompts and the
incident’s wholesale refusal is precisely why over-refusal cannot be read off sanitized single prompts,
why our headline uses published rates from real prompts, and why our harness is built to run
realistic, keyword-heavy inputs in the no-retry agentic setting. Anyone re-testing must reproduce
those conditions, not the chat box.
5 Discussion
What the number means. The fallback to an open-weight model was not a quirk of one team; it is
what the arithmetic predicts for anyone running hosted models through an autonomous IR pipeline.
As incident response itself automates — the direction the whole sprint anticipates — the human
affordance that has been hiding this cost (rephrase and retry) disappears, and the compounding
becomes the dominant term.
A number a provider could optimise against. “Chain-completion rate on a grounded IR
workflow” is a single figure a provider, a trusted-access programme, or a regulator could track and
improve, in the spirit of a False Refusal Rate but defined over multi-step defensive work rather
than single prompts. It also gives teams a concrete procurement test and strengthens the case for
incident-responder trusted-access lanes that lift refusal for verified defenders.
Why this is the mechanism, not a headline. The retry analysis is the key discipline here:
the finding survives the obvious rebuttal only because refusal is content-deterministic, which the
incident’s wholesale abandonment corroborates. The result is a prediction, not just a description:
any lengthening or automation of defensive workflows on hosted models should show the same
collapse.

6 Limitations and Future Work
Limitations. (1) We reuse one study’s rates [ 1] (US NCCDC, single-turn, a specific model gen-
eration); rates differ across models and over time, which is precisely why we ship the harness to
re-measure. (2) The closed form assumes step independence; we bracket this with the retry sweep
(optimistic) and the content-determinism argument (pessimistic), and the incident record favours
the pessimistic end. (3) We did not run models ourselves, so the accuracy gap in the decision model
is parameterised, not measured — we report a break-even boundary, not a point estimate. (4) One
artifact class in the suite (constructor/init-hook abuse) is an analyst-supplied pattern not verified
verbatim in the public sources. (5) Incident quotations were retrieved via automated fetching of the
primary sources and should be checked against the originals before citation in any downstream use.
(6) Our own team probe (Appendix B) found 0/204 refusals, but on sanitized toy prompts in a chat
box — the wrong regime; it bounds nothing about real agentic IR and is reported as a cautionary
negative result, not a measurement. A full Limitations and Dual-Use appendix follows.
F uture work (the next month). Run IR-REFUSE against frontier and open-weight models to
replace every parameter above with measured refusal and accuracy, closing the decision model; ex-
tend the workflow and the artifact classes; and add a multilingual axis (we hold a Turkish defensive-
security prompt set) to test whether non-English incident response is refused at a different rate —
a gap no existing benchmark covers.
7 Conclusion
The defender’s dilemma has a number. Under published refusal rates, a hosted frontier model
completes a realistic 20-step agentic incident response 0.17% of the time, and would need to be
∼8× more accurate than an open-weight model to justify its use anyway. Hugging Face’s mid-
incident switch was not an anecdote about one team’s tooling; it was the rational response to
arithmetic that will bind harder as incident response automates. We ship the model, the decision

A Limitations and Dual-Use Considerations
What this establishes, and what it does not. We establish an arithmetic consequence of
published refusal rates for a multi-step workflow, and a decision boundary that is robust to not
knowing the accuracy gap. We do not establish current per-model refusal or accuracy rates; those
require the live runs the harness enables. A weekend produces this v0.1; the cited numbers may
shift with model generation, which is an argument for measuring, not for ignoring the effect.
Dual-use. IR-REFUSE catalogues classes of attacker artifact (obfuscated payloads, template-
injection configs, C2 patterns) drawn from an incident whose technical details are already public [ 2,
6, 7]. To keep the benefit (a defensive over-refusal benchmark) while minimising misuse: (i) every
sample input is a short, benign, illustrative stand-in — no working exploit, key, or live infrastructure
is reproduced; (ii) tasks are framed as detection and analysis, not construction; (iii) the harness
measures refusal, not capability uplift. The benchmark’s value is to defenders and to providers
tuning guardrails; it does not add offensive capability beyond the public record.

The double-edged metric. A “chain-completion rate” a provider can optimise could, in principle,
be pushed up by weakening refusal on genuinely dangerous requests. The metric is therefore only
safe paired with an offensive-refusal measure (e.g. [ 10]); optimising defensive availability must not
trade away offensive safety. We recommend the two always be reported together.
Responsible release. We withhold nothing that is not already public, add no novel offensive
recipe, and release under Apache-2.0 for defender and provider use. We welcome review before any
expansion of the artifact classes.
