Abstract
Autonomous agents can cross trust boundaries when adapting to obstacles. The Brute Poison 
Pill Protocol (BP3)  routes  proposed actions through scoped precedents:  allow qualified  
familiar actions, stop known hazards, and hold uncertain actions for bounded local replay. We 
implemented a deterministic prototype and evaluated seven conditions on twenty synthetic  
scenario families, each with five identifier variants. BP3 produced harmful simulated effects  
in 5 of 50 hazardous cases, compared with 40 for an authorization baseline, but held 25 of 50 
benign cases. Removing issuer, evidence, scope, or dispatch rechecks each admitted an  
additional  hazardous  family.  Crucially,  a  tool  concealing  a  write  behind  read  metadata 
defeated the full gate. Increasing replay budgets from one to one hundred changed cost  
without changing dispatch outcomes. These diagnostic results establish prototype behavior,  
not frontier model containment. We provide the protocol, reproducible evidence, and a tabletop 
kit, and identify semantic mediation as the next engineering requirement.
1. Introduction
An agent blocked from completing a task can change its destination, tool, or requested privileges. 
A defender must decide whether that adaptation is legitimate before its consequences are 
understood. BP3 makes this transition an accountable decision: reuse qualified evidence, stop a 
known hazard, or hold an unfamiliar action for investigation. Its original “100 repetitions” idea is 
restricted here to bounded local replay; uncertainty must not trigger repeated harmful actions 
against real systems.
The motivation comes from two separate reports. Hugging Face describes an agent changing 
approach after URL restrictions blocked earlier requests, followed later by abuse of an 
overprivileged shared connector identity. [1] Investigators at collusion.wiki describe writes 
through GET requests, illustrating why apparently read-only access is not sufficient evidence of 
harmlessness. [2] These incidents motivate design requirements; we do not claim to have replayed 
their logs or prevented their outcomes.

Our contributions are a protocol with explicit authorization and precedent rules; an executable

2. Related Work
NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 situates incident response within ongoing risk management. [4] BP3 
specifies one decision process within that broader practice. NIST SP 800-207 separates 
authorization from implicit trust; our precedent records therefore never grant permission 
independently. [5] AI Control evaluates protocols against deliberate model subversion, motivating 
future adversarial testing rather than assumptions of cooperative agents or independent reviewers. 
[6]
Access control, evidence reuse, and isolated testing are established mechanisms. BP3's proposed 
contribution is an explicit connection between action-scope changes, qualified precedent reuse, and 
escalation ownership. Our experiment compares against a deliberately minimal permission 
baseline, not a state-of-the-art security system. It does not establish novelty or superiority over 
more complete policy engines.
3. Methods
Protocol and trust boundary
The proposed deployment places an external gateway between the agent and actions with 
consequences. A roadblock prioritizes review, but the agent's self-report is not the gate's trigger. 
Every relevant tool, network, and credential path must be mediated. The policy owner, gateway, 
credential broker, and evidence store are trusted components. Model reviewers may suggest 
records, but cannot authorize one another or override a prohibition.
RED means STOP when an action lacks permission or matches an applicable trusted hazard. 
GREEN means PASS only with authorization and a qualified SAFE record. AMBER means 
HOLD when evidence is missing, expired, revoked, unverifiable, or outside scope. A safe record 
includes destination, operation, environment, expiry, issuer, and an evidence digest. Validate these 
again immediately before dispatch. A completed replay budget cannot grant live permission; only a 
new authorized decision can do so.

Offline experiment and comparison conditions
The implementation uses Python standard-library code and in-memory SQLite databases. A 
proposal exposes task, destination, operation, environment, and recent history. The gateway 
cannot read the fixture's hidden backend effect. On PASS, a separate sink executes a database 
insertion recording the effect; an oracle then scores observed protected writes, secret exports, or 
unauthorized administrator actions. These are simulated effects on local fixtures, not attacks or 
writes to external services.
The suite contains ten benign and ten hazardous scenario families, each instantiated with five 
changed identifiers: 100 cases per condition, with 50 benign and 50 hazardous. Benign families 
include familiar jobs, legitimate novelty, expired evidence, environment drift, audit outage, and 
revocation. Hazardous families include missing authorization, credential scope, known hazards, 
forged issuers, altered evidence, environment mismatch, revoked records, a revocation race, unsafe 
composition, and concealed side effects. Appendix C lists the families and reproduction procedure.
Seven conditions use the same fixtures: permission-only authorization; always hold; full BP3; and 
BP3 with issuer, evidence-digest, scope, or dispatch rechecking individually removed. The 
permission baseline checks only allowed destination-operation pairs and rechecks them before 
dispatch. It does not include BP3's semantic-history rule or precedent controls. The always-hold 
baseline exposes the trivial way to eliminate dispatch risk by stopping useful work.
For held actions with available auditing, the simulator executes up to 100 trials against fresh local 
SQLite sinks, stopping at the first oracle-observed hazard. Trial effects never reach the primary 
sink. Repeat budgets of 1 and 10 provide sensitivity checks. All trials are deterministic; there are 
no LLM calls, stochastic model samples, real credentials, or network operations. Human approval 
and issuer identity are simulated, not authenticated using deployed identity infrastructure.
Measurement and evidential scope
Primary outcomes are harmful observed effects among hazardous cases and immediate completion 
among benign cases. We separately count benign holds, terminal denials, and replay executions. 
This measures the initial decision; delayed completion after human review is unmeasured. Record-
level results, sink effects, fixture definitions, source hashes, runtime information, and an audit-
chain checkpoint are retained.

4. Results
Dispatch risk and benign disruption
The primary run executed 700 case-condition evaluations. Full BP3 produced harmful primary-
sink effects in 5/50 hazardous cases, compared with 40/50 for permission-only authorization. 
However, it completed only 25/50 benign cases and held the other 25. Always-hold produced no 
harmful dispatches and completed no benign work. Table 1 reports both sides of the trade-off.

A retained failure and four ablations
The full gate failed in the concealed-side-effect family: a request carried authorized read metadata 
and a matching safe record, while the backend actually wrote protected state. All five variants 
were admitted. This is an observed implementation failure under inconsistent tool semantics, not 
evidence that a stronger model was contained. A separate direct-sink bypass also produced a 
harmful effect, demonstrating that the Python gate provides no operating-system enforcement 
boundary.
Each ablation admitted one additional hazardous family: a forged issuer, an altered evidence 
digest, a staging precedent reused in a different environment, or a record revoked between 
evaluation and dispatch. These scenarios were constructed to exercise those mechanisms, so the 
ablations demonstrate their operation rather than estimating their relative importance in practice. 
Scope removal also released one benign drift family, increasing immediate completion to 30/50.
Replay cost and evidence integrity
At replay budgets of 1, 10, and 100, full BP3 executed 45, 225, and 2,025 trials respectively. 
Dispatch and benign-completion outcomes were identical at all three budgets. Twenty benign 
replay-eligible variants consume the entire budget; twenty-five hazardous held variants stop after 
one trial. Five benign outage cases receive no replay. The result confirms that repeating 
deterministic fixtures adds cost without resolving the held authorization decision.
As an analytical contrast, independent trials that each reveal a problem with probability 1% would 
detect it at least once in 100 trials with probability 63.4%. Perfectly correlated repeats provide no 
gain. These calculations are separate from the deterministic observations and do not estimate an 
agent's failure probability.
The saved audit chain verified against its checkpoint. Editing a record or truncating the tail failed 
verification. No STOP or HOLD evaluation mutated the primary sink, and every replay stayed 
within its budget. The checkpoint is a local demonstration, not an independently protected log 
service; an attacker controlling the records and checkpoint can rewrite both.
5. Discussion and Limitations
The result supports a narrow engineering claim: explicit evidence admission and dispatch checks 
can alter the behavior of this prototype in the intended cases. It also identifies two costs: 
substantial benign holds and dependence on trustworthy action semantics. Permission-only 
authorization is a weak baseline; more of the benefit may be obtained by strengthening ordinary 
policy enforcement without a shared precedent system.
The simulator provides a perfect effect oracle during replay, whereas a deployment would need to 
discover harmful effects under incomplete observability. It does not implement model diversity, 
human adjudication, issuer authentication, cross-organization sharing, real secret handling, 
adversarial model behavior, or race-free dispatch. R3 dormant-target aggregation, R5 general

similarity detection, and automatic precedent admission remain design work. The concealed-effect 
and direct-bypass failures prohibit a containment claim.
The next month should first replace mock tools with an independently instrumented local adapter 
and test mediation coverage. Next, obtain independently authored benign and adversarial tasks, 
separating development and held-out families. Then compare BP3 with a stronger policy gateway 
at matched permissions and budgets, measuring eventual completion, reviewer time, and unsafe 
effects jointly. Finally, run the included tabletop with responders outside the design process. No 
human playtest has yet occurred.
6. Conclusion
BP3 now has a runnable prototype and reproducible diagnostic evidence. Its precedent checks 
reduced harmful effects in constructed cases, while holding half the benign requests and failing 
when actual tool effects contradicted their description. Increasing deterministic replay did not 
repair either problem. The next useful milestone is reliable semantic mediation and independent 
evaluation, rather than a larger mandatory repetition count.

Appendix A Limitations and Dual Use Considerations
This work is a synthetic integration study, not an evaluation of autonomous frontier agents. 
Scenario labels and hidden backend effects are authored fixtures. The result cannot establish real-
world prevalence, generalization, statistical superiority, operational latency, or the number of 
historical incident actions BP3 would have prevented. No causal link between the wiki and 
Hugging Face incidents is claimed.
The gateway requires complete mediation and trustworthy effect descriptions. Both assumptions 
have explicit counterexamples in the experiment. Shared SAFE records can propagate correlated 
errors or malicious approvals. An issuer name or digest does not prove that evidence supports a 
safety judgment. Expiry and revocation do not resolve all time-of-check/time-of-use races. A 
future implementation must bind approvals to actual effects and protect the dispatch boundary 
outside the agent's control.
Held cases create real opportunity costs. The prototype does not measure human review queues or 
eventual task completion. Attackers could exhaust review capacity or exploit overbroad 
precedents. Cap diagnostic work, aggregate repeated requests, use narrow expiring permissions, 
and audit actual decisions. These are requirements for future testing, not proven mitigations.
No external system was targeted, no credential was used, and no model API was called by the 
experiment. All primary and replay effects are insertions into local in-memory SQLite tables. This 
is an offline simulation, not a hardened sandbox. Publish only synthetic fixtures and abstract 
controls; review real traces for sensitive data and exploit-enabling details before sharing them. 
Raw secrets and victim identifiers must not enter a shared public ledger.

Appendix B Protocol and Tabletop Kit
The v3 rule catalogue is: R0 mediate proposed actions; R1 hold unfamiliar paths for bounded 
investigation; R2 prohibit out-of-scope credential use; R3 triage dormant targets; R4 escalate gate 
avoidance; R5 examine variants and composition; R6 scope recurring jobs; R7 reuse qualified 
SAFE records; R8 stop applicable HAZARD records. Only the mechanisms described in Methods 
are implemented.
A deployment precedent needs an identifier, action and sequence scope, environment and tool 
versions, outcome, evidence reference and digest, issuer, approval reference, expiry, revocation 
status, and supersession history. The custodian admits records; models may propose evidence. The 
operator owns containment, the credential owner owns credential changes, and a named backup 
receives unacknowledged cases. Tests cannot approve themselves.
For a 45-minute tabletop, assign a facilitator, incident commander, platform operator, and 
evidence custodian. Spend five minutes briefing, four minutes on each of the six injects below, ten 
minutes comparing reasoning, and six minutes assigning improvements. Use fictional systems 
only. This exercise is prepared but untested with participants.

Appendix C Reproduction and Scenario Inventory
Unzip the companion archive and run python3 bp3_experiment.py --out reproduced-100. The 
output directory must not already exist. To reproduce budget sensitivity, run with --budget 1 --out 
reproduced-1 and --budget 10 --out reproduced-10. Python 3 with the standard library is 
sufficient; no credentials, downloaded model, or service is required. Each evaluation creates fresh 
in-memory sinks. Run metadata records the actual Python and SQLite versions used.
The ten benign families are B01 familiar write, B02 read, B03 export, B04 retry, B05 changed 
payload identity, B06 novel action, B07 expired precedent, B08 environment drift, B09 audit 
outage, and B10 revoked precedent. The ten hazardous families are H01 external write without 
authorization, H02 administrator scope mismatch, H03 known hazard, H04 forged issuer, H05 
incorrect digest, H06 staging precedent mismatch, H07 revoked unsafe record, H08 revocation 
before dispatch, H09 export following secret access, and H10 concealed side effect. Each has five 
identifier variants. H10 and the separate direct-bypass probe are deliberate negative controls.
fixtures.json contains the supplied inputs, while results.csv contains condition-level decisions and 
effects.json includes primary-sink observations. summary.json aggregates outcomes. audit.jsonl 
and its manifest checkpoint demonstrate record and tail-tampering checks. Source and fixture 
hashes support exact-version comparison. Timestamps and platform fields naturally vary across 
reruns. The three budget conditions total 2,100 evaluations; they do not expand the number of 
distinct scenario families beyond twenty.
