Abstract 
Rapid AI advancement and rising international security concerns require robust tools to 
mitigate catastrophic risks. Highlighting the limitations of current AI regulations, this 
paper proposes the adoption of a Convention for Early Notification of AI Incid ents, 
inspired by the 1986 Convention on Early Notification of a Nuclear Accident, which 
solved a similar problem for radioactive releases. Across nineteen articles, our proposal 
defines a notification mechanism to ensure rapid information sharing and coor dinated 
action between governments, developers, and infrastructure operators. This study offers 
a concrete legal instrument to enhance global cooperation and manage AI incidents 
effectively. 
1. Introduction 
The growing international uncertainty surrounding the security of artificial intelligence has 
highlighted the urgent need for operational tools capable of responding effectively to current 
incidents and of developing resources that can mitigate catastrophic risks. The 1986 Convention on 
Early Notificati on of a Nuclear Accident addressed radioactive releases through a notification 
mechanism, providing a rapid and effective response to a problem that posed a high risk at the time; 
for this reason, it was used as a reference.1 From a regulatory response perspective, the Convention 
for Early Notification of AI Incidents is proposed as a notification mechanism designed to address 
the shortcomings identified in current regulations. The proposed treaty consists of nineteen articles 
structured to provide an operational and legally binding framework that ensures prompt notification,

2. Related Work 
Incident reporting for AI is addressed by two main instruments. First, article 55(1)(c) of the EU 
AI Act, operationalized by Commitment 9 of the Code of Practice and by the Commission’s serious-
incident reporting template for models with systemic risk. 2 Second, section 22757.13 of the 
California statute, whose four categories of critical safety incident include loss of control and 
deceptive subversion of the developer’s controls.3 
The EU AI Act reveals fundamental regulatory shortcomings that severely limit the law’s practical 
effectiveness. First, Article 3(63) explicitly excludes models intended for research, development, or 
prototyping prior to commercialization, creating a preve ntive blind spot that allows models with 
safety biases or systemic risks to  be trained entirely outside the scope of regulatory oversight. 
Second, Article 3(49) narrowly defines a ‘serious incident’ through a limited list of harms, without 
recognizing loss of control or deceptive subversion as grounds for notification in themselv es.4 This 
limitation undermines the alert system set forth in Article 73, which requires incidents to be reported 
immediately and sets maximum deadlines of two days for failures in critical infrastructure, ten days 
in the event of death, and fifteen days for violations of fundamental rights. By omitting control 
anomalies or autonomous deception from this list, companies are not required to meet these urgent 
deadlines in the face of unpredictable model behavior, allowing critical misalignment failures to 
remain hidden behind corporate opacity until physical or material damage has already occurred. 
On the other hand, under the California Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act (SB 
53) a frontier developer must report a critical safety incident wi thin fifteen days of discovery and 
transmit periodic summaries of catastrophic -risk assessments from internal use. 5 This regulation 
contains procedural ambiguities regarding the scope of internal evaluation, comparative testing, and 
pre-deployment research phases, due to the lack of precise criteria for internal containment breaches 
during pre-market development. 
Additionally, it lacks standardized emergency response protocols and direct alert mechanisms 
between the public and private sectors. In the absence of a real-time crisis management framework, 
the Office of Emergency Services (Cal OES) is limited to receiving audits and quarterly reports. 6 
The result is a regulatory framework that is ineffective at responding to incidents and reporting 
during the critical first hours.

This paper analyzes the existing regulation and then uses a case study to explain how other 
instruments have closed similar gaps in the past. The evidentiary base is documentary: the text of 
the Convention and the literatur e on the regime and on treaty effectiveness. The Convention is 
disaggregated into its design variables, and its negotiation history is analyzed to identify what 
produced rapid agreement. Following the logic of structured comparison, its provisions are assessed 
against AI governance: duty bearers, observability of the triggering event, existence of a host 
institution, and distribution of capabilities. Conditions that hold support transfer. New conditions 
specific to the AI context are then added. 
4. Results 
The Convention on Early Notification of a Nuclear Accident was adopted five months after 
Chernobyl and entered into force one month later.7 Some key features of its design account for that 
speed. The obligation is thin: the Convention centers on creating a duty to notify and to provide 
information. Entry into force was cheap: three States sufficed, and provisional application was 
available on signature. 
That is why incident monitoring is one of the few areas where the benefits of internationalization 
are high: “we find that the need to internationalise incident monitoring is high.” 8 The reason is the 
transboundary nature of the harm. Incidents “are unlikely to be confined only to that jurisdiction”, 
and one occurring in a given country “could be an early warning sign that the same model may soon 
cause harm in other jurisdictions.”9 
For that reason, “a well-coordinated global incident monitoring system could serve as an early alert 
mechanism to prevent the escalation and spread of AI -related accidents acros s borders.” 10 
Interoperability, however, requires a common classification of harms, since only then can reports 
from different jurisdictions be pooled and recurring failures identified.11 Governance capacity is also 
uneven: many countries lack the funding and technical expertise to independently monitor harms to 
their citizens, a gap that internationalization can help close.12 
To achieve that, the AI Treaty Momentum Index (ATMI) recommends keeping the “plumbing on 
the shelf”: ready-to-file clauses and a defined adoption pathway. In its most geopolitically strained 
scenario, the advice is to pursue a coalition -of-the-willing trea ty path with depositary pre -
commitment, so as to bypass full consensus, and to invest in structured incident reporting.13

5. Discussion and Limitations 
Publishing a drafted treaty with its articles is itself a dual-use act. A specific text is easier to evaluate 
and easier to file, but it could also give actors with an interest in blocking a binding regime an early 
target. 
The analysis is documentary and r ests on a single case. Structured comparison of the 1986 
Convention with the AI context establishes congruence between design variables and outcome, not 
causal identification. There is no counterfactual and no variation on the dependent variable, since 
the case was selected because it succeeded. What the paper offers is therefore a plausible account of 
which conditions made rapid agreement possible, not a test of whether any of them was necessary. 
One of the challenges is to foster global cooperation so tha t countries will be interested in 
implementing this reporting mechanism; therefore, it is important to develop effective strategies to 
promote widespread adoption. 
Future work proceeds along three lines. The first is a roadmap for adoption. The second is a draft 
UNGA resolution opening the Convention for signature. The third is the design of subsequent 
instruments for the remaining governance functions where internat ionalization yields comparable 
benefits. 
6. Conclusion 
Comparative regulatory analysis shows  that the governance frameworks examined, the European 
Union’s AI Act and California’s SB 53, have limitations and shortcomings when it comes to 
mitigating catastrophic risks and providing an immediate response to critical incidents. Given these 
regulatory gaps, there is a need to develop new tools. This mechanism proposes an operational and 
binding framework to coordinate rapid action among developers, operators, and government 
agencies on an international scale, thereby generating a positive impact in the field of AI safety.
