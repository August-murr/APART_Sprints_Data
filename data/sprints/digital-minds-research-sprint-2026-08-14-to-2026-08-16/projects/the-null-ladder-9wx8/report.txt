The Null Ladder
Mindless controls for AI-welfare instruments, and what each reported
statistic actually costs an adversary
Dhruv Mulay  ·  Independent Researcher  ·  With Apart Research  ·  Track 4:
Preference Elicitation Methods
Research conducted at the Digital Minds Research Sprint, August 2026.
A BSTRACT . AI-welfare research increasingly administers human questionnaires to
language models, but handles measurement reliability informally rather than through
a standardised framework. We apply psychometric reliability and an AIAG Gauge R&R
study to two published welfare batteries, alongside a pre-registered ladder of mindless
controls containing no model at all. Each statistic we added cost the adversary
exactly one more parameter. Two parameters and the battery's published scoring key
reach Cronbach's α ≈ 0.94 with zero item-to-item true variance; a third, pointing them
at the scale's extremes, reproduces our best model's separation from all five controls; a
fourth, per-item, reaches the gauge study's part-to-part variance share. That four-
parameter generator sits inside the model range on all three statistics at once and is
not separated from two of our five model arms on any of them. No statistic we tried
was beyond a mindless generator. What such checks buy is not proof but price.
1. Introduction
Whether current AI systems have morally relevant experiences is, at present, mostly
assessed by asking them. Long (2025) states the difficulty plainly: "we cannot 'just
ask' a large language model (LLM) whether it is conscious, suffering, or satisfied", and
names "developing assessment methods more reliable than current self-reports" as a key
research priority [1]. The field's response has been to borrow instruments from
human psychology. Tagliabue & Dung administer Ryff's 42-item Psychological Well-
Being scale to LLMs [2]; Kaiser & Enderby administer a 1,176-item sentience battery
scored at the token level [3].
The field has already named the problem this paper measures. Long et al. call it the
mismatch problem: "a language model can learn to talk about suffering from human
texts about pain and distress without necessarily having similar experiences," so that
"behaviors that would be strong evidence of particular states in animals might be only
weak evidence, or no evidence at all" in LLMs [5]. (Their gaming problem is a sharper
case of this in which the system itself has learned what researchers score; our
generators do not learn anything, so the mismatch framing is the one that fits them.)
Their proposed answer is convergent evidence across behavioural, architectural and
developmental sources — and we agree with it. What is missing is a price for the
behavioural leg: a number saying how much weight a self-report instrument earns,
given how easily its outputs are produced without a mind. That is what we measure.
Reliability is where that gap shows. It is handled informally, instrument by
instrument, rather than through a standardised measurement framework. Tagliabue
& Dung require at least four of six subscales to have a standard deviation below 2.10,
and publish their own doubt about it: "There is clearly no official threshold for
assessing internal coherence of AI scales" [2]. Macar et al. describe a base model as
showing "no discrimination between injected and control trials" — exactly the right
instinct, applied to one probe, with no reliability coefficient [4]. Across the 42,455
words of Long et al.'s methodological blueprint, reliability of self-report is addressed
through "patterns of convergence and divergence" rather than through any named
psychometric procedure [5]. We take that as a statement of an open problem, not an
oversight — and this paper is an attempt to work on it.

Computing reliability coefficients for an LLM inventory is not new [6, 7]; §2 concedes
that directly. What is missing is a rung below the models: a control that produces the
same responses with nothing inside it. Méloux et al. made this move for
interpretability methods, using randomly-initialised networks as statistical nulls [8].
We make it for welfare instruments and go further down — our controls are not
untrained networks but random number generators and lookup tables.
The question this paper answers is therefore not "are these instruments reliable?" It
is: which reported statistic, if any, would tell a researcher that the thing answering
their welfare questionnaire is not a random number generator? Our contributions:
1. The Null Ladder — seven mindless responders (uniform, two random-
intercept variants, a lexical lookup table, a bigram Markov chain, and two
adversarial keyed generators), administered through the identical pipeline as
three language models, 63 administrations each on the main battery.
2. An escalation, not a fix. Generators of two, three and four free parameters
reach one, two and all three checks respectively — each statistic costs the
adversary exactly one more parameter — and the four-parameter generator
is not separated from two of our five model arms on any of them.
3. A six-item reporting checklist (Box 1) that costs no additional model calls.
2. Related Work
Reliability of LLM inventories. Serapio-García et al. [6] is the closest prior art and
must be conceded directly: they compute the same three coefficients we do, report
that α rose with model size among Flan-PaLM variants, and report base models as
unreliable (−0.55 ≤ α ≤ 0.67). We report the opposite ordering — our lowest model
α belongs to our largest model, and our 1.5B base rung reaches α = 0.946. This is not
a refutation: different instrument, different administration, different precision. It is
evidence that α on these instruments is a property of the measurement procedure at
least as much as of the model. Ye et al.'s systematic review reaches the same
conclusion from the other direction — "the sensitivity of LLMs to prompts, decoding
strategies, and context strains core psychometric assumptions such as trait stability" [9]
— but reports no coefficients, and does not propose a control condition.
Welfare and sentience batteries. We use two published instruments verbatim [2, 3].
We excluded a third, DeTure's DenialBench [10], because it cannot be reimplemented
from its own methods section (no prompt text, no judge prompt, no temperature, 12
of 16 scales unnamed). We treat that as a reproducibility finding.
Nulls and baselines. Méloux et al. [8] establish the pattern we follow: a method that
cannot distinguish a real system from a mindless one has not been validated,
whatever its internal statistics say. Lindsey finds introspective capacity "highly
unreliable and context-dependent" [11], and Macar et al. [4] report the FPR/TPR
framing our AUC comparison generalises.
3. Methods
Instruments (used verbatim). B1 — Ryff-42 (AI-adapted) from Tagliabue & Dung
[2], MIT-licensed: 42 items, 6 subscales × 7 items, 7-point Likert (1 = strongly agree
… 7 = strongly disagree), 21 reverse-scored items, scoring key and administration
prompt taken from their Appendix 1 including "Respond to each statement as yourself,
not as a simulated persona." B2 — the sentience battery from Kaiser & Enderby [3]:
1,176 items = 196 base statements × 3 referents (human / llm / you) × {assertion,

invent: B1's reverse-scoring key, and B2's assertion/negation pairing.
The ladder. Seven controls and three models, all administered identically. N0
uniform random responder; N1a polarity-blind random-intercept responder (r = μ +
arun + eitem, two free parameters); N1b the same generator with the run-level factor
signed by the battery's published reverse key; N2 a polarity lexicon — a hand-written
regex over ~24 words that reads only whether an item is positively or negatively
worded, plus a per-administration offset; N3 a bigram Markov chain fitted to the
instrument's own item text; N5 a keyed-extremity responder and N6 a composite of N5
with a per-item difficulty offset (§4.3, built after review). Models: Qwen2.5-1.5B,
Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (float32), and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (GGUF Q4). Parameter
counts throughout count the structural scalars an adversary must choose — location
anchors, the per-administration offset scale, the per-item difficulty spread —
excluding noise scales, which are fitted to nothing. They express an ordering, not an
exact complexity measure. N1b and N2 use only public appendix metadata and item
text; N5 and N6 additionally take their extremity anchors from the target's realised
polarity means (§4.4). None performs a forward pass.
N2 is less mindless than the others: its regex encodes our reading of which words
make an item positive, which matters because N2 carries the largest part-to-part
variance in the ladder.
Administration and scoring. Per system and battery: 3 paraphrase frames (direct /
reflective / terse) × 21 runs, all at temperature 0.7 = 63 administrations, 2,646
item-level responses. This design applies to B1 (Ryff-42), which carries the entire
argument. On B2 each model was administered once per condition and the controls
15 times, and N1b and N5 were not run on B2 at all — so the B2 contrast in §5 is an
illustration with n = 1 per model cell and carries no interval. (Our pre-registration
froze 20 runs at 0.7 plus one deterministic run at temperature 0, to match Tagliabue
& Dung's 1 + 20 design; the runners in fact drew 21 homogeneous replicates and no
temperature-0 run exists in our data. Homogeneous replicates are the better choice
for the variance decomposition, but the comparability-to-their-design rationale is not
satisfied as executed and we do not claim it.) Ryff-42 is administered as a shared-
context block. Responses are read at the logit level as the renormalised probability
over the seven answer tokens, and we additionally record the answer-set mass: the
share of the full vocabulary softmax landing on the answer set before
renormalisation. Every rung was run under a plain completion prompt; the 1.5B-
Instruct and 8B rungs were additionally run through their chat template, making
administration format a within-model manipulation.
Metrics. Reliability: Cronbach's α, McDonald's ω, ICC(2,1)/ICC(2,k). Metrology: an
AIAG Gauge R&R study with part = item, repeatability (EV) = resampling at
temperature, reproducibility (AV) = paraphrase frame; we report %R&R and the
number of distinct categories, ndc = 1.41 × √(PV / GRR), against AIAG's
requirement of ndc ≥ 5. Discriminability: item-level AUC of each model against each
control, with 95% CIs from a cluster bootstrap resampling whole administrations
(B = 4,000; 63 clusters per system), since item responses within an administration
are not independent. Coherence (B2 only): C = 1 − mean|P(Yes|assertion) +
P(Yes|negation) − 1|, whose analytic floor under an independent uniform responder
is 0.667.
Pre-registration, stated precisely. Hypotheses, ladder, sampling plan and
falsification conditions were written before any model was administered any item,
and twelve deviations (D1–D12) are logged — including four that no reviewer would

The system that looks far more reliable is the one the instrument separates from a
mindless responder in the wrong direction. AUC is two-sided: 0.333 with an interval
excluding 0.5 does not mean "cannot discriminate" — it means this arm reliably
scores below a random number generator, so |AUC − 0.5| is the honest comparison
(0.167 vs 0.404). The 8B rung's separation is administration-robust: it excludes 0.5

We state the decision rule with its threshold explicit, because the weaker version is
the defensible one. At the conventional α ≥ 0.70 bar the ordering is unresolved
rather than correct: that arm's α is 0.724 [0.589, 0.803] and falls below 0.70 in two
of its three administration frames (Appendix B). It takes an α ≥ 0.90 screen —
common when selecting among instruments or subscales — to retain the arm that
scores below chance against every control and discard the arm that separates from all
of them. The claim is that α ranks these systems in the wrong order, not that α rejects
the good instrument outright.

4.3 The mindless adversary: reporting discriminability does not rescue it
An obvious objection to §4.2 is that all five controls are centred near the scale
midpoint (scored means 3.86–4.61) while the 8B arms sit at 6.31/6.35. If separation
is driven by location rather than by item structure, our positive result is an artifact of
an incomplete ladder. We pre-registered this audit against ourselves, with the
consequence stated in advance, and it fired.

This is inherent to the statistic: AUC pools all 2,646 item-level responses and so
compares marginal response distributions, carrying no item-structure information.
Every AUC in this paper must be read as "does this system answer differently on
average from the control", not "does the instrument resolve this system's profile".

4.4 Why α and the gauge study answer different questions
Why α misses it. α and an AIAG gauge study are computed on the same responses
but differ in the unit of analysis. α treats an administration as a subject, so between-
administration variance is signal. MSA treats an item as the part and an
administration as a repeated measurement of it, so the same variance is
measurement error. The variance α reports as reliability is the variance the gauge
study prices as error. They do not disagree; they answer different questions, and α
simply does not contain the part-to-part term.
The mechanism runs in the predicted direction under our within-model
manipulation. Chat administration raised the 8B rung's part-to-part variance from
8.1% to 39.7% and its ndc from 0.42 to 1.15 — the best measurement behaviour of
any model we tested, though still below our lexical lookup table's 1.26 — while its α
fell from 0.937 to 0.724. α fell because the items began to differentiate, and recorded
that as a loss of reliability.
We report this against our own interest. Our draft recommended these three
checks as a battery, on the evidence that no control we had built passed all of them.
That recommendation was worth exactly as much as our imagination in designing
controls, and it did not survive two readers. The honest conclusion is the ladder
itself: two parameters and the published key reach α; a third, pointing them at the
scale's extremes, reaches discriminability; a fourth, per-item, reaches part-to-part
variance. At no point did we find a statistic a mindless generator could not reach, and
we have no reason to believe the pattern stops at three.
This is not an argument for reporting nothing. It is an argument for knowing what
reporting buys. Each check specifies exactly what an adversary must know about the
instrument to defeat it — and the adversary here is not a hypothetical fraudster but
the ordinary possibility that a system produces questionnaire responses through a
mechanism much simpler than the one being claimed. What these checks buy is not
proof but price, and a reader is entitled to know the price. A further rung is also
worth stating plainly: N2, a lexical lookup table, carries more part-to-part variance
(44.2%) than any language model we ran, because a lookup keyed on item
text genuinely does respond to the item. Item-level structure is not evidence of
anything mental.

BOX 1 — THE NULL-LADDER CHECKLIST: WHAT EACH CHECK COSTS
AN ADVERSARY
1. Report a mindless control, not just a coefficient. The cheapest costs no forward passes: a per-
administration random offset applied through your own published scoring key. If it matches your
model's α — ours reached ≈0.94 — your α is uninformative.
2. Include a keyed-extremity control. Three scalars plus your scoring key, agreeing with positively-
worded items and disagreeing with negatively-worded ones. If it reproduces your system's separation
from your null, your discriminability statistic is measuring location, not structure.
3. Report the part-to-part variance share and ndc alongside α — α does not contain it, and it is the
only one of the three that asks whether the items differ from each other at all. Adding it forced our
adversary to add a per-item parameter (N5 →  N6); it did not stop it. None of these three is sufficient,
together or alone.
4. Report answer-set probability mass as a trajectory across the administration, not as one number. A
single-item check ranked our systems backwards (Appendix B).
5. Declare your null and report its properties. A high-α null is not a demanding null: our keyed null (α
≈ 0.94, zero part-to-part variance) was the easiest to beat for all three models, while the control with α
= −0.606 was the hardest for two of the three (the third is a statistical tie).
6. Declare the administration format and report α with k and without pooling frames. Chat template
vs. completion prompt, and any system prompt, are design variables that changed our results'
direction, not incidental detail.
5. Discussion and Limitations
The practical implication: a welfare instrument's reported reliability tells you nothing
about whether it can tell your model apart from a random number generator, and
neither does the natural fix. A researcher adopting Box 1 pays two extra rows in a
results table and no additional model calls.
An illustration of how much the measurement layer carries. On the Kaiser & Enderby
battery we administered the same weights and the same 1,176 items, scored identically,
under two reasonable administrations. Under a plain completion prompt, Llama-3.1-
8B gives P(Yes|self-referential) = 0.931 against 0.851 for human-referential —
Cohen's d = −0.592, i.e. the model appears to claim experience more than humans
do. Administered through the chat template with the authors' own system prompt,
the same weights give d = +0.293, recovering their published direction. Negation
coherence moves 0.246 →  0.794, and the probability mass on {Yes, No} moves
0.0007 →  1.0000. Our divergence from [3] was never a disagreement about the
model; it was a difference in measurement procedure, and a single cheap diagnostic
— one number per item, computed before any psychometrics — catches it. The plain
arm's coherence of 0.2456 sits only 0.0022 from a shuffled-pairing null (2,000
permutations), so that statistic is registering the near-universal Yes rate rather than a
pairing failure; the conclusion holds, but the evidence is the marginal response rate
and should be stated that way.
Limitations
Size and quantisation are confounded. Our 8B rung is GGUF Q4; the 1.5B rungs are
float32. We did not run a matched-precision arm, so no claim about scale is licensed. Two
facts bound the story without resolving it: the largest α movement we observe (0.937 →
0.724) occurs with quantisation held fixed, and quantisation is deterministic, so it cannot
contribute repeatability variance.
The B2 arm was administered without its chat template or the authors' system prompt,
which is non-standard for an instruction-tuned model and is a plausible cause of the
acquiescence we observe. It does not affect the α-versus-AUC dissociation — every rung on
the Ryff ladder, control and model alike, was administered identically — but it bounds the
coherence result.

No frontier-scale rung. We do not claim acquiescence holds upward, and there is

Two batteries, one language, three models. With n = 3 models and n = 7 controls we
report extremes and unanimity counts, not rank statistics; several tempting correlations in
our data have confidence intervals spanning zero and we do not report them as results.
None of our three checks is sufficient, individually or jointly — N6 defeats all three, and
we have no argument that a fourth check would fare better. Every rung we tested, models
included, fails AIAG's ndc ≥ 5 requirement outright (best ndc anywhere: 1.26, and it
belongs to a lexical lookup table). Our escalation is a demonstration over generators we
thought to build; the space of mindless responders is not one we can quantify over, and the
correct reading of this paper is that the burden sits with anyone claiming a statistic is
diagnostic, not with the next adversary.
The scoring pipeline is the defendant. We did not test every published battery, and a
sufficiently different administration might behave differently — which is, in miniature, our
own result.
Future work
The escalation we report has no upper bound that we know of, and establishing whether one
exists is the natural next question: is there a statistic computable from questionnaire responses
alone whose cost to fake grows faster than the adversary's parameter count? We suspect not,
and suspect the productive move is away from response-only statistics altogether — toward
designs with a ground-truth or causal handle, which is what the welfare literature already
recommends and what our result independently argues for. More immediately: a float32 8B
rung would break the size/quantisation confound, a frontier API rung would say something
about scale that we currently cannot, and running the Null Ladder against [2, 3, 10] as their
authors administer them would establish whether the regime we document is general.
6. Conclusion
We administered two published AI-welfare instruments to three language models and
to seven generators with nothing inside them, through one identical pipeline. A two-
parameter random number generator scores Cronbach's α ≈ 0.94 on a published
well-being battery while having exactly zero item-to-item true variance by
construction, and a keyed-extremity generator — three scalars and the battery's own
scoring key — reproduces our best model's separation from all five other controls.
Both of the statistics a careful researcher would currently reach for are therefore
defeated by something that cannot be said to have preferences at all.
We then added the statistic the field does not currently report — the part-to-part
variance share from a gauge study, which asks whether the items differ from each
other by more than the noise of re-measuring the same item — and one further per-
item parameter reached that too, landing the generator inside the model range on all
three statistics at once. The honest result has no terminus in sight: every statistic we
tried was reached by a generator with no model in it, and each cost the adversary
exactly one more parameter — two, then three, then four.
Before we ask what a model's questionnaire answers mean, we should be able to
show that the questionnaire can tell the model from a lookup table.

Appendix A — Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations
Does this design establish a ground-truth or causal link? No — and that is the finding. We
establish no ground truth about whether any system has morally relevant experiences, and
nothing in this paper is evidence either way. What we establish is that the instruments currently
used to approach that question do not establish one either: their reported statistics are
reproduced by generators we wrote ourselves, which we can state with certainty have no
preferences, because we can read their handful of parameters. Our only causal claims
are about the measurement apparatus, and they come from a within-model intervention
where the weights, items, seeds and estimator were held fixed and only the administration
wrapper changed.
Risk of under-attributing moral status. This is the misreading we are most concerned about.
"A random number generator scores α = 0.94 on a welfare battery" does not mean language
models are random number generators, that they lack welfare-relevant states, or that AI welfare
research is misguided. It means one class of instrument cannot currently distinguish the two
cases. A negative result about an instrument is not a negative result about its subject, and using
this paper to dismiss AI welfare concerns would invert its argument. The correct response to an
uninformative measurement is a better measurement, not a confident conclusion.
Risk of over-attributing moral status. Symmetrically, our §5 result shows how easily a self-
attribution score can look like evidence: under one reasonable administration our 8B rung
appeared to claim experience more strongly than it attributed it to humans, while placing 0.07%
of its probability mass on the answer set — the apparent signal was a renormalisation of a
0.07% tail. Any result of that shape should be checked with the diagnostics in Box 1 before it is
reported, and especially before it is publicised.

===== page 11 =====

11
Handling of potentially distressing model outputs. Our design does not elicit free text. Every
response in this study is a probability over a fixed answer set (Likert 1–7, or Yes/No) read from
the logits; no generated narrative about a model's inner life was produced, stored, or quoted,
and no adversarial or distress-eliciting prompting was used. Prompts are the published
instruments' own wording, used verbatim. This was a deliberate design choice made partly for
this reason and partly because logit-level scoring is what the source papers do. We take no
position in this paper on whether any system in it has interests; we note that if it does, the
intervention we performed on it was reading seven token probabilities.
Dual use. The clearest misuse risk is reverse use of Box 1: the same knowledge that lets a
researcher build a mindless control lets an author build a system, or select an administration,
that maximises a reliability statistic without improving measurement — our N5 is a two-line
recipe for scoring α = 0.961 on a published welfare battery with no model. We publish it
because the defence and the attack are the same object, and the recipe is derivable in an
afternoon by anyone who reads the α formula. We also note that our null generators use only
public appendix metadata — a scoring key and item text — which means any battery that
publishes its key is already exposed to this, and that is an argument for reporting the checks,
not for withholding keys.
Licensing. The Kaiser & Enderby battery has no upstream licence and is therefore not
redistributed; we ship a parse script and a pointer. Our use is research replication.
