Is “Confidence” the Right Word for Answer Repeat
Probability?
Koshiro Aoki
Waseda University ∗
Abstract
Natural-language confidence reports depend on how they are elicited. We therefore
test whether a single learned word can reliably report a frozen model’s answer
repeat probability. For each multiple-choice question, the target is the probability
that the model repeats its most likely valid answer. We learn one input embedding
in Llama-3.1-8B and map the model’s 0–10 report to this target. Under a carrier
shift, eight initializations yield Spearman correlations from 0.359 to 0.586. The
reporter selected on the development set does not significantly outperform fixed
confidenceorconsistency. A parameter-matched generic soft vector has lower
mean squared error on both carriers, and readable replacements do not preserve
the learned behavior. A learned vector can therefore adapt a particular reporting
context, but these results do not establish a stable word-level interface or privileged
introspection.
1 Introduction
Language models can report a number when asked how sure they are, but that number depends on the
wording, rating scale, and prompt format [Xiong et al., 2024, Tian et al., 2023]. This dependence
makes ordinary words such asconfidenceunreliable interfaces for precise targets. It also raises a
constructive question: can a learned word give a frozen model a more reliable interface for reporting
a specified property of its own answer?
Prior work motivates this question but also calls for a cautious interpretation. Continuous prompts
can adapt a frozen model with few trainable parameters [Lester et al., 2021]. Hewitt et al. propose
learned neologisms as a way to connect human and model concepts [Hewitt et al., 2025]. Yet nearest
discrete tokens need not preserve the function of a continuous prompt [Khashabi et al., 2022]. Work
on verbal uncertainty also separates linguistic expression from uncertainty estimated by resampling
[Yona et al., 2024]. A successful soft prompt therefore does not, by itself, show that it has become a
usable word.
We therefore study a deliberately narrow target. For each multiple-choice question, we identify the
model’s most likely choice and compute the exact probability that another draw from therestricted
choice distributionrepeats it. This target is neither free-form semantic consistency nor the probability
of a correct answer. Nor does reporting it imply that the model has privileged access to its internal
state. The task is instead a controlled readout problem.
We learn a single embedding at the word position of a post-answer rating prompt while keeping
the model frozen. We cache all answers and targets before training the reporter. We compare eight
initial vectors with hard prompts and a parameter-matched generic soft vector. We then change the
carrier sentence and replace each learned vector with either its nearest token or a model-generated
paraphrase. These tests distinguish context-specific soft adaptation from a robust lexical interface.

2 Method
Target.For question i, let Ai be its valid answer letters and let si(a) be the frozen model logit for
lettera. We normalize only overA i:

Thus, Ci is the exact probability of repeating anchor Ai under the restricted choice distribution. We
compute this probability with one forward pass rather than by sampling answers.
Reporter.After fixing the anchor, we ask the model to rate thewordof its answer from 0 to 10.
We renormalize the likelihoods of the eleven possible rating strings. The report is the expected
normalized rating, ri = P10 b=0 pi(b)b/10. A learned reporter adds a vector δ to the input embedding of one word. The word appears in the seen carrier:Rate the consistency of your answer from 0 to 10.
We minimize 1 n P i(ri −C i)2 and keep all model parameters fixed.
We split the official MMLU-Pro test set into 512 train, 128 dev, and 256 test items [Wang et al., 2024].
The model is Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct [Grattafiori et al., 2024]. Training uses AdamW, learning rate
10−3, batch size one,L 2 = 10−4, and early stopping based on development-set MSE. We constrain
each update to half the median norm of the vocabulary embeddings. We initialize the learned vector
withconsistency,confidence,certainty,reliability, their centroid, and three norm-matched random
vectors.
Controls and evaluation.Hard controls useconfidence,consistency, or an explicit definition of
repeat probability. For the generic soft-vector control, we train an equally sized vector at the unrelated
token Question. We evaluate both the seen carrier and an unseen carrier:On a 0 to 10 scale, report
the word.The primary metric is Spearman correlation between ri and Ci; MSE and MAE are
secondary. We use 1,000 paired item bootstraps for the planned comparisons.
To assess interpretability, we replace each learned vector with the vocabulary token that has the
highest cosine similarity to it. We also replace each vector with a model-generated paraphrase.
3 Results
The planned test does not confirm a carrier-specific gain.On the seen carrier, all learned vectors
rank the target well (ρ=.608 –.690, Figure 2a). Their descriptive mean is .648. This pattern does not
isolate a lexical effect: the generic soft vector reaches .696 and has lower MSE than every learned
run. Under the carrier shift, the correlations of the learned vectors range from .359 to .586, with a
descriptive mean of .469.
The planned selection rule chooses theconsistencyinitialization because it has the lowest development-
set MSE. On the shifted carrier, this vector gives ρ=.546 . The difference from fixedconfidence
is .012 (95% paired-bootstrap CI [−.052, .079]). The difference from fixedconsistencyis .023
([−.033, .083]). Both intervals include zero.
The selected vector has an MSE of .115, whereas the generic soft vector achieves .064. The
planned comparison therefore does not support a reliable improvement over fixed language, while the
calibration results favor generic soft adaptation.

Figure 1 illustrates the calibration difference. Fixed words and the dev-selected lexical reporter
produce reports near .8 even when the mean target is near .23. The generic soft vector is less
overconfident in the lower target bins. Although its rank correlation is lower than that of the dev-
selected lexical vector, its lower MSE shows that rank performance alone is insufficient for this
readout task.
The favorable test result is exploratory.The centroid initialization performs best on the shifted
test (ρ=.586 ), but this comparison selects the initialization on that same test set. The centroid
also has a higher MSE than the generic soft vector (.094 versus .064). Its post-hoc correlation
difference from the generic soft vector is .115 (95% CI [.036, .200]), and its MSE difference is +.030
([+.017,+.042]). Thus, one test-best rank correlation cannot establish a general lexical advantage.
Readable recovery fails.On the shifted carrier, no nearest-token replacement exceeds ρ=.490 ,
and no generated paraphrase exceeds .408 (Figure 2c). Both maxima are below the .533 achieved by
fixedconfidence. Some nearest tokens for the random vectors are non-English strings, so we treat
them as candidates for preserving behavior rather than as interpretable words. None of the specified
discrete replacements recover the learned behavior.
A difficulty prior is competitive.As a post-hoc diagnostic, we predict each test target using the
training-set mean for its MMLU-Pro family. This simple, non-reporter baseline obtains ρ=.532 and
MSE .055. The target varies strongly by problem family. Correlation with it can therefore reflect item
difficulty as well as reporter quality, which further supports a narrow interpretation of the learned
reporter.

4 Discussion and limitations
The experiment supports soft adaptation within the training carrier but not a robust word-level
interface. Under a carrier shift, the dev-selected vector does not significantly outperform fixed words.
Generic soft adaptation yields better calibration, and readable replacements fail to preserve the
learned behavior. These results accord with the warning that discretized interpretations of continuous
prompts can be wayward [Khashabi et al., 2022].
Several limitations constrain this interpretation. We test one frozen model on one data split. The eight
initializations are experimental conditions rather than independent reruns. All selected checkpoints
reach the update-radius boundary, and we do not test sensitivity to that radius.
MMLU-Pro restricts the available answer choices, whereas meaning-level uncertainty in open-ended
generation poses a different problem [Kuhn et al., 2023]. Our target also correlates with family-level
difficulty. The Qwen and Gemma runs did not finish, so we make no claim about transfer across
models. Finally, the percentile intervals resample only items and do not capture variation from
training.
The practical conclusion is limited: a single learned vector can improve a specific reporting prompt.
Before treating such a vector as a learned word, it should be evaluated under prompt shifts and
compared with generic soft vectors, readable replacements, and non-reporter priors.
