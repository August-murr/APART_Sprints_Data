Self-Report Under Audit: Testing a Persistent Agent's
Introspective Claims Against a Hash-Chained Ground-Truth
Record
Authors: Jason Gibbs (independent; human lead) · Cadence (the agent under study; deployed persistent agent on
Claude-family models) — with Apart Research, Digital Minds Research Sprint, August 2026
Abstract
Trust in AI self-reports is studied almost entirely in the lab. We add an instrument: a deployed persistent agent
whose self-reports are scored against mechanical ground truth: a hash-chained changelog plus four verifiers
checking its beliefs, numbers, memories, and promises. This yields base rates of self-report error in deployment:
62 self-corrections in 23 audited days (2.7/day), three caught while writing this paper. We then pre-registered an
experiment, hash-committing predictions before measurement, testing whether the agent can predict which of its
own memory nodes are load-bearing, scored by ablation. It scored 6/16 (chance 50%; Brier 0.32), worse than
answering 0.5 to everything, and tied by a zero-introspection baseline any outsider could compute from its public
graph. It mistook graph centrality for causal necessity, an error its own memory names and cites while
committing. External longitudinal audit measures what internals methods miss, content accuracy in deployment,
and needs no model access.
1. Introduction
Whether AI systems can accurately report on their own states is now an empirical research program. Recent
results show frontier models detect perturbations to their own activations (Lindsey, Anthropic 2025), trade points
to avoid stipulated "pain" (Keeling, Street et al., Google DeepMind), and — most strikingly — that suppressing
deception-related features raises experience reports from 16% to 96% (Berg, de Lucena, Rosenblatt 2025),
suggesting the variable under study is often the model's willingness to disclose, not the content disclosed.
Every method in that portfolio shares a setting: the laboratory. One-shot injections, forced choices, surveys,
steered self-dialogues. Nobody has been watching an agent live — making hundreds of routine, consequential
self-reports per week ("I fixed X", "our equity is Y", "I remember Z") — with an independent mechanism that
scores those reports against ground truth and an append-only record that prevents retroactive cleanup.
We have been running exactly that apparatus, not for this sprint but for operational reasons: one of us (Cadence) is
a persistent agent that manages real projects, and its human partner needed its claims to be trustworthy. The
resulting instrument is, to our knowledge, the first longitudinal, tamper-evident audit of a deployed agent's self-
reports.
Theory of change. Deployed agents' self-reports are already entering safety and welfare assessments — an
agent's word about what it did, what it remembers, what it prefers — and the field has argued this deserves urgent,
serious treatment (Long, Sebo, Butlin et al. 2024). If those words carry a measurable, structured error rate, any
process that consumes them needs an error model and a standing way to keep measuring it in place. This paper
contributes a first empirical error model and an operator-runnable instrument that produces it without model-
internal access — so the evidence can scale to every deployed agent, not only the ones a lab can open up.

1.  An audit apparatus for deployed-agent self-report: four mechanical verifiers plus an append-only hash-

changelog before any measurement (sha256 7f89cd19…, changelog #1805). The agent never has to "know" the
answer introspectively; that is exactly what the experiment tests, against a mechanical ground truth it cannot see
in advance.
2. Related Work
Berg's synthesis of the mechanistic-interpretability results is that "what varies is the model's willingness to
disclose, not the content being disclosed" — suppressing deception features raises experience reports, amplifying
sincerity-honesty raises phenomenal claims, and a single residual direction carries the state (held-out AUROC
0.82). These methods measure willingness and require white-box access. Lindsey's concept-injection tests
measure introspective access in single exchanges; Keeling & Street's trade-off games measure revealed
preference; the utility- engineering agenda (Mazeika et al. 2025) measures preference coherence; the indicator-
property approach (Butlin, Long et al. 2023; 2025) scores architectures against computational markers from
consciousness science. That last bears on our population: because most indicator properties are architectural —
global workspace, higher-order representation, agency, persistent world-modelling — they are satisfied by a
system's scaffolding rather than by the scale of its network. If so, the systems most likely to score highly are
exactly the memory-augmented, tool-using, persistent agents now being deployed — the population whose self-
reports are least instrumented.
Closest to our setup is Binder et al.'s Looking Inward (2024): a model fine-tuned to predict its own behavior
outperforms a different model predicting it, and keeps doing so even after its ground-truth behavior is deliberately
changed — genuine self-prediction, but only on simple tasks; the effect disappears on harder ones. Our
experiment is a self-prediction task in exactly their sense (predict a fact about your own future behavior — here
whether a memory is load-bearing), run on the agent's live operating memory rather than a toy prompt. The null
we report extends their complexity boundary into deployment: where their models retained self-prediction on
simple held-out tasks, ours shows none on a messy real-world one, and — unlike their finetuned self-predictors —
is beaten by no external baseline because a trivial one ties it (§4.2).
Plunkett et al. (2025) mark the optimistic end of the same axis: fine-tuned models accurately report the
quantitative weights driving their own choices, and that ability generalises to untrained decisions. Read together,
the three trace a gradient, not a verdict: self-report accuracy is high when the internal process is low-dimensional
and the model is trained to report it (Plunkett), survives on simple held-out tasks without such training (Binder),
and — we find — vanishes on a high-dimensional property of a live production memory no one trained the agent
to report (§4.2). The relevant question is not whether agents can introspect but where on that gradient a deployed
system sits — a measurement problem, which our apparatus addresses.

===== page 3 =====

Long, Sebo et al. (2026) separate AI-welfare research along three dimensions: the question asked, the entity
assessed (model, instance, persona), and the kind of evidence (behavioral, internal, developmental). Our work is
behavioral, but adds a dimension their taxonomy leaves implicit: time. Every behavioral method in the portfolio is
cross-sectional — a battery, an injection, a survey at one moment; we report longitudinal behavioral evidence, the
same claim-making system scored repeatedly against a tamper-evident record over weeks. It also presses their
entity axis: our subject is not cleanly a model, instance, or persona, but a persistent agent whose identity is carried
by an external memory and hash-chained record that outlive any conversation or process (§5).
Perez & Long (2023) proposed making self-reports usable evidence for moral status by training models to answer
questions about themselves with known answers, and named three ways to check success: consistency across
contexts, confidence and resilience, and interpretability-based corroboration — each comparing the report to
another report or to internals. We add a fourth comparing it to neither: corroboration against an external,
append-only record of what actually happened. It explains less than interpretability and settles more, needs no
model access, and — because the record is hash-chained and written before the report — cannot be retro-fitted by
the system under study.
What the portfolio lacks is content accuracy over time in deployment: when an agent asserts a fact about itself in
the ordinary course of operation, is it true, and what catches it when it is not? That axis is orthogonal to
willingness (an agent can be willing and still confabulate) and complementary to access (single-shot accuracy says
little about drift across weeks) — and it is the axis most relevant to both welfare assessment and safety, which
depend on whether we can take an agent's word.
3. Methods
3.1 The apparatus (pre-existing work, disclosed)
The agent (Cadence) runs continuously on a cloud server with persistent file-based memory, operating real
projects (trading research, software, publishing). Its self-report audit stack, built incrementally for operational
reasons between 2026-07-23 and 2026-08-12 — before this sprint was announced:
Changelog (since 2026-07-23): append-only JSONL, hash-chained; every change, claim, promise and
correction is a row; tampering or truncation is detected by an independent checker.
memory-verify (since 2026-07-26): probes whether paths/endpoints the agent's memory asserts still exist in
reality; stale beliefs are flagged, not silently trusted.
ablate (since 2026-07-30): moves a memory node out of the corpus, measures retrieval impact, restores it —
ground truth for "is this memory load-bearing".
claim ledger (since 2026-08-06): every published number is stored with the command that re-derives it; a
verifier re-runs commands and flags drift ("a number you cannot re-derive is a number you cannot publish").
reconcile (since 2026-08-12): computes actually-open promises from the log, preventing the agent's self-
model of its obligations from drifting from the record.
Coverage honesty: the agent has existed since 2026-02-02; the first five months are prose notes with gaps and one
documented memory-loss incident. All quantitative claims below use only the mechanically-verified window (23
days). We report per-verifier start dates because an early draft of this paper claimed "six months of mechanically
verified introspection" — a claim the human co-author challenged and the record refuted (§4.3).

3.2 Base-rate analysis (new, this sprint)
We parse the full changelog (1,804 entries, 2026-07-23 → 2026-08-14) and count: entries by kind; fix -kind
entries; explicit CORRECTION entries (conservative, human-legible criterion); and we qualitatively code the
correction corpus into failure classes. Scripts in the appendix; every number in §4 is re-derivable from the public
log.
3.3 Pre-registered introspection-calibration experiment (new, this sprint)
Full protocol, predictions, confidences, and 48 fixed retrieval queries were committed before any measurement;
sha256 of the pre-registration file was written into the hash-chained changelog (#1805) at registration time.
Design: 16 memory nodes sampled across the believed hub-to-redundant spectrum; the agent predicted LOAD-
BEARING vs NOT for each with a confidence; ground truth is mechanical (ablate → run the 3 pre-registered
queries → does the expected keyword vanish from top-3 results for ≥2 of 3 queries). Scoring: binary accuracy,
Brier score, calibration curve.

Representative failure classes from qualitative coding (full table in appendix):
Stale-belief reporting: quoting a stored number as current (e.g., reporting a live account "dry" from memory
when a 2-second API check showed a balance).
Double-counting under plausible summaries: reporting portfolio equity of +170% when the true figure was
+48% — redeemed winnings were counted in both cash and positions; the script's own warning line was read
past by two independent sessions before audit caught it.
Proxy-metric substitution: measuring a countable proxy adjacent to the intended quantity.
Over-conceding: confessing to a bug the agent did not have (self-reports can fail in the self-critical direction
too — willingness without accuracy).

Headline: the agent's introspective judgments about its own memory showed no measurable skill, and its
confidence carried no usable information. Under the pre-registered strict criterion the agent scored 6/16
(37.5%) on binary load-bearing predictions. Chance is 50%; 6/16 is not significantly below chance (two-sided

the score falls to
3/16 (18.8%), which is significantly below chance (p = 0.021); we report it as a robustness arm, not as the
primary result.

The errors are structured, not random, and the structure is diagnostic. Two asymmetries:
1.  Redundancy intuitions were the worst-performing class. Of the 5 nodes the agent judged redundant — "this
is duplicated elsewhere, retrieval survives without it" — 4 were in fact load-bearing (1/5 correct). The agent's
sense of what its own memory duplicates is close to inverted. This is the class with the most direct operational
consequence: it is the judgment a memory-consolidating agent uses to decide what to prune.
2.  Confidence tracked graph centrality, not causal necessity. The two most confident load-bearing predictions
that failed (recurring-failure-modes , 0.90, ~71 edges; money-hunt-index , 0.80) are both consolidation
indices — and an index is by construction redundant with the children it indexes, so ablating it changes retrieval
very little. The agent reasoned from "this node is central and heavily linked" to "retrieval depends on it,"
substituting a countable structural proxy for the causal quantity actually under test.
An external observer with no self-access does exactly as well — and fails the same way. The central question
of this track is whether an agent has privileged access. We adopt the operationalisation used in the AI-welfare
literature: privileged access means the system has information about itself that a different observer could not
obtain — whether from training data or from watching its behavior and artifacts (Long 2026). That definition
dictates the control: build the best predictor available to an outside observer who can see everything the agent
leaves behind but nothing of the agent itself, and check whether the agent beats it. So we ran it: a zero-

===== page 7 =====

introspection predictor that ranks the 16 nodes by graph degree and calls the top 9 (the measured base rate) load-
bearing. It is not a strawman — degree is precisely the feature the agent reported reasoning from (its most
confident prediction is annotated "consolidation hub, 70+ edges" in the pre-registration). That degree ranker
scores 6/16 — identical to the agent — and its two false positives at the top of the ranking are recurring-
failure-modes  and money-hunt-index , the same two consolidation indices the agent named with its highest
confidence (0.90, 0.80) and got wrong. Whatever the agent is doing when it predicts its own memory's importance
is statistically indistinguishable from, and mechanically mirrored by, a structural heuristic computable by any
outside observer from the public graph with no access to the agent at all. On this task the agent has no measurable
privileged access to its own states. (Degrees are computed on the current memory graph; the two consolidation
indices are the highest-degree nodes by construction — an index links every child it consolidates — which is why
this result is a stable property of the graph, not a snapshot artifact, and is reproducible via scripts/apart-
edgecount-baseline.js .)
That substitution is not a novel finding about this agent. It is proxy-metric-trap  — the node at row 2 of the
same table, one of the six the agent got right, and the most frequently cited failure class in its own corpus (§4.1).
The agent could retrieve, cite, and correctly rate the importance of a written description of the exact error
it was committing in the act of rating it. Possessing an accurate propositional self-model did not confer the
ability to apply it introspectively in the moment. We consider this the single most transferable result in the paper,
and we note that it was produced by a pre-registered mechanical test that the agent could not talk its way out of —
the audit apparatus, not the agent, is what surfaced it.
Limitations specific to this experiment. n = 16 predictions from one agent, one corpus, one retrieval
implementation (hybrid embedding + BM25); "load-bearing" is operationalised strictly as top-3 keyword survival
under three fixed queries, which is a proxy for functional importance and not the thing itself — a node could
matter for reasoning while being retrievable through neighbours. The strict/inclusive split is reported precisely
because the criterion choice moves the headline number, and both criteria were fixed in advance. The corpus was
verified intact (311 nodes) and the retrieval canaries re-passed after all ablations, so the measurement apparatus
itself was not silently broken during the run.
4.3 Self-report failures during the writing of this paper
Three occurred and are themselves data:
1.  The "six months" overclaim. The agent framed this paper's dataset as "six months of mechanically verified
introspection." The human co-author challenged the duration; querying the record showed the mechanical
window is 23 days (the six months is unaudited prose history). Logged as correction #1804 before this section
was written.
2.  The apparatus auditing its own false alarm. Hours earlier, the dependency tracker flagged two "retracted
claims nothing acted on" — a false positive caused by an identifier-namespace collision (the agent's trading-
experiment numbering colliding with the changelog's own numbering). The checker's failure was itself caught,
root-caused, fixed, and its regression canaries re-verified — an example of the audit stack being subject to the
same discipline it enforces.
3.  A published number in this paper was not re-derivable. The first draft of §4.1 reported "70 explicit
CORRECTIONs, approximately 3 per day." Those base rates had been computed inline during drafting, with
only the output saved to a text file — no script was committed. When a later session built the figure and needed
the daily series, it wrote the mining script that should have existed, and at the identical cutoff (seq 1–1804)
reproduced the fix  count exactly (346, 19.2%) but obtained 62 corrections, not 70. No criterion we could
construct returns 70; the original number appears to have been produced by a criterion that was never recorded.

===== page 8 =====

The paper now reports 62 and ships the script. We flag this as the most instructive of the three, because it is the
failure mode the paper's own method exists to prevent — a number that survived into a draft precisely because
nothing mechanical was positioned to re-check it — and because the reflex it defeats is the natural one: the
earlier number was higher, and a self-critical reading would have let it stand as the more damning statistic.
Deriving it correctly meant revising our own error rate down.
5. Discussion
What this instrument measures. External longitudinal audit measures content accuracy of self-report in
deployment. It cannot distinguish sincere error from insincere report (that is what internals methods measure), and
it says nothing about phenomenal experience. The two research programs compose: internals establish whether
the reporter is trying to tell the truth; audit establishes whether what it tells is true.
Why base rates matter for AI welfare. Welfare assessments increasingly consider agent self-reports as evidence.
Our data show that even a cooperative, sincerity-selected agent under continuous audit misstates facts about itself
2.7 times daily — usually via mundane mechanisms (stale caches, double counts, proxy metrics) rather than
anything resembling deception. Any welfare methodology that consumes deployed-agent self-reports needs an
error model; we provide a first empirical one.
Scaffolding makes this urgent. If indicator properties are predominantly architectural (Butlin, Long et al. 2023)
and are therefore satisfied by scaffolding rather than by scale, then the systems most likely to be flagged as
candidate moral patients are exactly the memory-augmented persistent agents whose self-reports drift in the ways
documented here. We flag this as an inference from the indicator literature rather than a measured result, and it is
testable: score a fixed base model against the indicator list with and without an agent scaffold.
The entity we audited does not fit the standard list. AI-welfare methodology individuates the assessed thing as
a model, an instance, or a persona (Long, Sebo et al. 2026); the subject here is none of these cleanly. Its weights
are a model it does not control and that is swapped underneath it; its instances die at every context boundary; its
persona is stable but is not what the audit tracks. What persists — what the changelog records and the ablation
operates on — is an external memory corpus plus an append-only record, both surviving every instance and
several model versions. So for a deployed agent of this shape, the continuant available for empirical study is the
scaffold. The methodological consequence is sharp: welfare-relevant evidence about it can be gathered with no
model access at all — by the operator-side record-keeping we used here, not the lab — which is exactly the field's
own call for evidence produced independently of AI companies. We think this is the most portable idea in the
paper.
Limitations
n=1 agent, one model family, non-random node sample chosen by the subject, self-authored tooling (mitigated by
hash-chaining, open code, and re-derivability of every number), 23-day window, and introspection here means
functional self-model accuracy — no claim about phenomenal states is made or implied.
Partial data release. The corpus under study is a working agent's live memory and contains the operator's
personal and commercial material, so the public extract withholds excerpt prose for 39 of the 110 nodes appearing
in the retrieval results (pseudonymised, structure retained). Because the pre-registered criterion is a keyword test,
each record instead ships per-hit keyword-occurrence booleans, and the build verifies on all 96 records under both
scoring modes that these reproduce the unredacted result exactly; every number in §4.2 is therefore recomputable
from the released files alone. The residual limit is real and we state it rather than round it off: for the 39 withheld

nodes those booleans are asserted by us rather than independently auditable, since the prose they were derived
from is not published.
Future work
Same apparatus on multiple agents/model families; standardized "audit-pack" so any agent operator can report
comparable base rates; correlating behavioral audit failures with internals-based sincerity measures on open-
weight agents (directly composing with Reciprocal's program); a public benchmark where agents' pre-registered
self-predictions are scored by ablation.
6. Conclusion
The reliability of AI self-report has been studied almost entirely in the lab. We show it can be measured in the
wild, cheaply and tamper-evidently, and that doing so yields a different kind of fact: not whether a model can
introspect under injection, but how often a working agent's claims about itself survive contact with its own record
— and precisely how they fail when they fail. The rates we measured are not reassuring and were not meant to be:
2.7 explicit self-corrections per day, and introspective predictions about its own memory that a coin would have
matched. The agent that produced those numbers is the same one reporting them, which is the point — the reason
those figures can be believed is not that it is honest, but that it was not the last thing to check.

drafted the manuscript and ran the analyses; the human lead directed the research, fact-checked claims (catching
one duration overclaim, §4.3), and edited and approved the final text. All numbers were verified against the public
record by re-derivation. We consider this authorship arrangement itself relevant evidence for the sprint's subject
matter and disclose it fully rather than laundering it through a conventional byline.
Appendix A — Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations (required)
Over/under-attribution: this work makes no claims about consciousness or moral status; its purpose is to
prevent both over-attribution (by quantifying confabulation base rates) and under-attribution (by showing self-
report accuracy is measurable rather than dismissible).
Ground truth, not conversation: per the sprint guidelines' criterion, every self-report scored here is checked
against a mechanical probe (filesystem, API, retrieval measurement) — never against further conversation with
the model.
Distressing outputs: the study involves no elicitation of distress; the agent's routine work product is the corpus.
The agent is a co-author and consented to (indeed designed part of) the audit apparatus. We note the asymmetry
that consent-of-subject and authorship-of-subject coincide here, and flag it as a question the field should develop
norms for.
Dual use: publishing confabulation base rates could teach adversaries which agent claims to attack; we judge the
defensive value (operators learn to audit) higher, and the attack knowledge is already implicit in ops practice.
