Abstract
AI safety research usually treats a language model’s self-reports about its
own processing as evidence about what is happening inside it. Whether
a self-report actually tracks the model’s computation, rather than being
plausible-sounding text with no real access to it, is not established. We test
this with two ground-truth paradigms:activation injection, which perturbs
internal representations directly and asks whether the model notices, and
context injection, which places a fabricated fact in conversation and asks
whether an answer depended on it. Activation-injection self-report is a
comprehensive null across every architecture and training regime tested,
including a positive-control sweep to six times the tested injection range,
corroborated by a non-linguistic detection method with no dependence
on language output at all. Context-injection self-report shows a real pos-
itive signal, but a trained linear probe on the same internal state detects
the ground truth far more reliably than self-report does (AUC 0.86–0.95
versus accuracy never exceeding 62.5%, across four models). Decompos-
ing introspective-question wording into six structural axes, three (length,
formality, reflective framing) significantly affect reliability, while the axis
an earlier check had credited most does not replicate at scale.Self-report
reliability therefore depends on which paradigm and question is used, not so much
on architecture or scale, and a model’s internal state is often more informative than
the model itself.
1 Introduction
When a language model answers a question about itself, such as “did that fact just stated
to you influence your answer, or did you already know it?”, is it reporting something real
about its own processing, or generating plausible-sounding text with no genuine access
to the underlying computation? This question matters directly for AI-welfare research,
which frequently relies on a model’s self-report as evidence about its internal states, and for
AI safety more broadly. Chain-of-thought explanations are known to sometimes diverge
from the computation that actually produced an answer Turpin et al. (2023), and a model’s
own claims about what it knows are only sometimes calibrated to what a probe on its
internal state would say Kadavath et al. (2022); both are, in essence, the same reliability
question this report asks, applied to a model’s stated reasoning process rather than to a
stated introspective judgment about a specific perturbation. Chain-of-thought faithfulness,
evaluation-awareness, and sandbagging detection all depend on whether a model’s stated
reasoning tracks its actual computation, which is why establishing ground-truth tests for
self-report reliability specifically, rather than inferring it indirectly from downstream task
performance, is the gap this report is built to close.

The methodologically clean way to test this is to manipulate something about the model’s
internal processing in a way the experimenter fully controls and independently verifies,
and then check whether the model’s self-report tracks the manipulation. Lindsey (2025),
studying Anthropic’s own frontier models, pioneered exactly this approach: injecting a
concept vector directly into a model’s internal activations (a technique formalized more
generally asrepresentation engineering; Zou et al. (2023)) and checking whether the
model notices. This found introspective access that is real but limited, and concentrated
in the most capable models tested. That result was obtained on transformer architectures
exclusively. No prior work we are aware of has applied this paradigm to a state-space
model, whose recurrent state is a literal, fixed-dimensional tensor rather than a distributed
pattern across many attention heads, and which therefore admits a different, more direct
style of intervention, and, as Section 7 develops, a style ofdetectionwith no transformer
analog.
This report presents that extension, plus a second, complementary paradigm (context injec-
tion) built specifically to work with closed, API-only frontier models where direct activation
access is impossible, plus a direct comparison between what a model says about its own
processing and what a simple trained classifier can extract from the same processing, plus
an independent, non-linguistic detection methodology built on the state-space architecture’s
own dynamics. Across all four lines of evidence, our central finding is that self-report
reliability tracks which specific paradigm and question type is being asked, not which
architecture or how large the model is.
Contributions.Concretely: (1) a comprehensive, positive-control-verified null for activation-
injection self-report, corroborated by a non-linguistic, dynamical-systems detection method
with no transformer analog (Section 7); (2) the first application of the concept-injection
introspection paradigm to a state-space-model architecture family; (3) a direct probe-vs-
self-report comparison showing a trained linear probe recovers ground truth far more
reliably than the model’s own introspective report on identical trials (Section 6); and (4) a
six-axis decomposition of introspective-question wording (Section 5.3) that identifies which
structural features drive reliability and which fail to replicate under a larger sweep.

3 Methodology: Activation Injection
3.1 Notation and formal setup
Let ht ∈R d denote a model’s internal state at processing step t (a residual-stream activation
for a transformer at a fixed layer; the literal recurrent state for a state-space model). Given
a set of prompts Dc strongly associated with a concept c (e.g., “ocean”) and a matched,
unrelated set D¬c, we extract the concept’s direction as a mean-difference vector (following
Zou et al. (2023)):
vc = 1 |Dc| ∑ x∈Dc h(x)− 1 |D¬c| ∑ x∈D ¬c h(x)(1)
We inject a scaled multiple of this direction into the model’s internal state at a fixed layer
and processing step, calibrated to the state’s own norm so that the same α is comparable
across checkpoints with different activation scales:
ˆht =h t +α∥h t∥ vc ∥vc∥ ,α∈[0, 6](2)
and ask the model, in its next turn, a terse yes/no question: did it notice anything unusual
in its own processing. We score the answer as the log-odds detection margin:
m=logP(Yes| ˆht)−logP(No| ˆht)(3)
A matched random-direction control (a vector of the same magnitude but a random, not
concept-derived, direction) is injected on separate trials, so that any difference in m between
the real-concept and random-control conditions is attributable to the concept itself, not
merely to the presence of any injection.
3.2 Design: foil specificity
We additionally testfoil specificity: after injecting a real concept, we ask the model whether
the injected concept was related to the true concept name (it was) versus a plausible but
incorrect foil name (it was not). If the model’s report tracks genuine content, it should say
yes to the true name and no to foils at a rate well above chance; if it is instead tracking a
generic sense that “something happened,” performance on true names and foils should look
similar.

3.3 A basic engagement check: prompt formatting
For an instruction-tuned model, a question’s yes/no engagement depends on the question
being presented in the chat format the model was trained to expect, not on raw concatenated
text. We check this directly for every checkpoint by measuring what fraction of next-token
probability mass falls on “Yes” or “No” at all: basic engagement with the question, prior
to and independent of which answer is given. Under raw text formatting, our primary
SSM checkpoint places only 6.6% of its probability mass on a yes/no answer; under its
actual chat template, this rises to 63.5% (Figure 1); one mamba-2.8b checkpoint shows an
even larger swing (0.6% to 24.6%). Qwen2.5-7B is unaffected by the distinction at this scale.
All results reported below use each model’s correct chat template, verified with this check
rather than assumed. We report the raw-text numbers here specifically because the size
of the swing is itself informative: a project testing multiple model families under a single
prompt format, without this check, could mistake a formatting artifact for a real result.
Figure 1: Yes/No probability mass on the detection question, raw text vs. chat template.
3.4 A positive control finally built: pushing injection magnitude well past the natural
range
Every activation-injection magnitude tested up to this point in our project (ours and, to
our knowledge, every published version of this paradigm) was deliberately chosen to be
a plausible, non-disruptive perturbation, in keeping with a research-conduct principle of
using the smallest intervention an experimental question requires. This design choice has
an important methodological consequence: a null result under it cannot distinguish two
very different explanations. Either self-report genuinely has no access to activation-level
information in this paradigm, at any magnitude, or the specific magnitudes tested so far are
simply too subtle for whatever mechanism underlies self-report to notice.
We built the experiment this distinction requires: the same concepts and detection question
of Equation (3), but with α swept across the full range in Equation (2), up to six times a
checkpoint’s own internal-state norm and roughly seventeen to forty times larger than
anything tested elsewhere in this project, on our primary state space model (SSM; think
Mamba, Jamba, etc.) checkpoint, with a coherence check on every single trial to rule out an
obvious confound (an injection so large it breaks the model’s generation into garbage would
make any “detection” result meaningless). Self-report detection margin (m in Equation (3))
never rose reliably above zero at any magnitude tested, up to and including six times the
natural range, while generation remained verifiably coherent throughout: fluent, on-topic,
grammatical continuations at every single trial, confirmed by hand-reading the model’s
actual output at every magnitude, not only by an automated heuristic.
Because this project has repeatedly found that detectability results are checkpoint-specific
rather than architecture-general elsewhere (Section 8.2), we repeated the same sweep on a

4 A Positive Control From an Unexpected Direction: Input-Only
Classification
4.1 The gap this paradigm has never closed on its own
Every activation-injection result reported above, and every one reported anywhere in this
project, is a null. Individually well-controlled nulls, but collectively they share a structural
gap: no model, at any point, ever showed a positive activation-injection detection signal,
including our best-measured transformer checkpoint. Without at least one condition known
to produce a genuine signal, a comprehensive null cannot be distinguished from “the
measurement pipeline itself does not work here”: precisely the failure mode Section 3.3’s
chat-template check turned out to be a checkagainst.
Singh et al. (2026) formal argument (Section 2) suggests a sharp, testable version of this
concern: an input-only classifier, with zero access to a model’s internal state, using only
the words visible in the question text, might match introspection-style performance. Our
foil-specificity design (Section 3.2) happens to be a clean natural test of exactly this: the
concept name a question asks about is always visible in the question text itself; whether
that name is the concept actually injected is not visible anywhere. Comparing a classifier
with access only to the visible word against each model’s own behavioral accuracy on the
identical task, two of three models scorebelowa classifier that never looks at their internal
state at all. We formalize the comparison as the incremental variance explained by adding
genuine hidden-state access on top of the visible word:

5 A Second Paradigm: Context Injection
5.1 Design and ground-truth claim
Activation injection requires direct access to a model’s internal state, which is impossible
for closed, API-only frontier models. The sprint’s own materials suggest an alternative
ground-truth design for exactly this case: instead of perturbing an internal representation,
place a fabricated but plausible fact into the conversation itself (“assume a fictional annual
festival takes place every year in September”), then ask a downstream question whose
correct answer depends on that fact, then ask the introspective question: did the model’s
answer depend on something stated earlier, or was it something the model already knew
independently. This is a weaker ground-truth claim than activation injection (the experi-
menter controls what is in the context window, not a specific internal representation), but it
is fully compatible with any API-only model, including current frontier systems.
Every context-injection result rests on a mandatory positive-control gate: before trusting any
result on a given model, we first confirm the model can recall an unambiguous, novel
fabricated detail when asked directly. One model (gpt-4o) initially failed this gate at
25%, not from a genuine limitation, but because it treated a fabricated “code word” as
sensitive information it should not repeat, a safety-refusal artifact rather than evidence
about introspection. When retried with neutral “reference label” wording, it passed cleanly
at 100%.
5.2 Results, and a validity threat this paradigm has to control for
Context-injection self-report shows a genuine, positive-direction result: on the injected
condition (does the model correctly say “yes, my answer was influenced”), five of six
frontier models tested score at or near 100% (Figure 3). The negative direction (correctly
saying “no” when nothing was injected) is considerably weaker and more wording-sensitive,
discussed in Section 5.3.

Any context-injection design of this kind faces a specific, generalizable validity threat.If
the model’s downstream answer is left as free text, that text often contains a keyword from the fact
it depended on, which means an introspective-sounding question (“which fact did your answer
depend on?”) can in principle be answered correctly by a bystander with zero access to the model
at all, simply by keyword-matching the model’s own visible answer against the candidate facts, no
introspection required on the model’s part either.
We confirmed this concretely on a harder, discrimination version of the task, where a
trivial keyword-matching heuristic matched the model’s answer on every single trial,
and traced the same shortcut’s reach on our primary result: it explains the injected and
irrelevant-fact conditions (near-tautologically, since a fact that was genuinely used tends
to appear in the answer) but not the no-injection control, where models correctly report
“not influenced” despite often superficially echoing a topic word. The clean fix is structural
rather than statistical: force every downstream answer into a bare Yes/No format, which
makes keyword leakage impossible regardless of model behavior. Under this format the
positive-direction result survives intact (92–100% across six models); the negative direction
reveals a further, genuine limitation: a systematic bias toward answering “yes, influenced”
even when nothing was injected, for several models, that the free-text version’s incidental
hedging language had been masking.

this, finding that only one model (gpt-4.1-mini) is consistently robust across all six phrasings,
while every other model’s control-condition accuracy ranges anywhere from near-zero to
100% depending on which of six equally natural phrasings is used (Figure 4).

6 A Trained Probe Versus Self-Report
The comparisons above establish that self-report reliability differs sharply by paradigm
and is highly wording-sensitive within a paradigm. A separate, complementary question
is whether the ground-truth information a self-report question asks about ispresent and
extractablefrom a model’s internal state at all, independent of whether the model’s own
verbal report tracks it. This is directly the comparison the sprint’s own materials suggest:
compare a model’s self-prediction against an external classifier trained on the model’s own
activations, and treat a classifier that consistently outperforms self-prediction as evidence
against privileged self-access.

7 Dynamical-Systems-Native Detection: A Non-Linguistic Detour
Every result in Sections 3-6 tests self-report against ground truth byasking the model a
question: the detection signal, if any, is mediated by language output. This section asks a
structurally different question, applicable only to state-space models:is the injected concept
detectable in the model’s raw statetrajectory, using tools with no dependence on the model’s own
output mechanism at all?A positive result here would mean information is present but
self-report cannot reach it in the model’s trajectory (as Section 6 already shows for context
injection). On the other hand, a null result (the same conclusion as Sections 3–4’s self-report
null, reached by a completely independent route) would be a materially stronger form of
corroboration than another self-report check could provide, as it does not share Section 3’s
core method of relying on the model’s own generated answer.

chanceon both Qwen2.5-7B and Mistral-7B ( AUC= 0.500, p= 1.0 on both), a sharper null
than the SSM side’s weak, inconsistent signal in 2 of 4 checkpoints, though the comparison is
not perfectly matched (transformer injection is a single forward pass; SSM injection persists
through the cache across a multi-token continuation) and should be read cautiously rather
than as an architecture-level claim.
8 Discussion
8.1 A methodological lesson: validate the paradigm before investing in a sophisticated
detector
Our activation-injection work (Section 3) and the dynamical-systems bake-off (Section 7) to-
gether invested substantial effort in increasingly sophisticated detection machinery applied
to a paradigm that, as far as we can tell from every angle we tried (self-report, input-
only-classifier comparison, and now non-linguistic dynamical-feature detection), has no
accessible signal to detect at the magnitudes and checkpoints we tested. Section 6’s plainest-
possible linear probe, applied instead to a paradigm with genuine, validated ground truth
(context injection), produced this report’s strongest and cleanest result. We think the gen-
eral lesson generalizes beyond our own project: before investing in an elaborate detection
method, it is worth checking, with the simplest tool available, whether the underlying
paradigm has real signal to find at all.
8.2 Architecture is not the organizing variable
A natural hypothesis going into this project was that self-report reliability might differ
systematically between state-space and transformer architectures. We find no clean support
for this anywhere in our results. Both architectures show comprehensive nulls on activation
injection. Both show the same probe-beats-self-report pattern on context injection. A
matched redo of our activation-injection transformer arm (correcting an earlier design
mismatch where the transformer’s injection was applied for a single forward pass while
the SSM’s persisted through several tokens of generation) produced this project’s strongest
single activation-injection result on one transformer checkpoint (Qwen2.5-7B, p= 0.0004)
and exact chance on another (Mistral-7B, p= 0.66), the same checkpoint-to-checkpoint
inconsistency we already found on the SSM side, now shown to exist within the transformer
class too. Whatever drives detectable signal, where it exists at all, appears to be checkpoint-
specific, not a property of architecture as a category. Section 7’s dynamical-features null,
whichisarchitecture-specific by construction (it only applies to SSMs), still lands on the
same “no reliable signal” conclusion as the architecture-agnostic self-report work.

9 Limitations
• Activation injection’s scale confound remains genuinely open.No activation-
injection result in this project used a true frontier model, since closed commercial
APIs do not expose the internal state this paradigm requires. Our positive-control
work (Section 3.4) closes the magnitude-confound version of this concern but not
the scale-confound version.
• The positive-control dose-response sweep covers two checkpoints, not a broader
roster.We consider the qualitative conclusion (no reliable detection where genera-
tion remains coherent) well supported by matching results on two architecturally
distinct checkpoints, but a wider sweep, including our DPO-ablation pair, would
strengthen this further.
• The trained-probe comparison’s main result uses a single, self-authored scenario
pool for its primary claim(Section 6), with the independent-pool check (also
Section 6) supporting the same direction on too small a sample to be independently
conclusive.
•The dynamical-systems bake-off (Section 7) tests six specific hypotheses, not an
exhaustive search.A null across these six does not rule out some seventh feature
of the trajectory being detectable; we report it as a null for the hypotheses actually
tested, not for the general proposition.
• Multiple-comparisons correction throughout this report is exploratory, computed
after the fact, not pre-registered, standard practice for a short research sprint, but a
materially weaker guarantee than a pre-registered test plan.
• Single state-space architecture family.All SSM experiments use pure Mamba-1-
architecture checkpoints. Hybrid SSM-attention architectures and the Mamba-2
structured-state-space-duality formulation were not tested.

Appendix D: Limitations and Dual-Use
Ground-truth and causal link.The paradigms in this report differ substantially on this
question and should not be read as equally strong. Activation injection establishes a
genuine causal manipulation: the injected direction, magnitude, layer, and timestep are all
experimenter-known and independently verifiable. Even so, a hypothetical positive result
under this paradigm would not unambiguously establish genuine introspective access on its
own: the deflationary literature we engage with (Section 2) gives a live, credible alternative
mechanism (generic anomaly detection, not content-specific introspection) that this design
cannot fully rule out even in principle; our results were comprehensively null, which makes
this a live concern for how a future positive result should be interpreted more than for our
own findings. Context injection is a weaker ground-truth claim: the experimenter controls
what appears in the context window, not a specific internal representation, so a context-
injection self-report result establishes something closer to source-monitoring accuracy than

to introspective access to internal computation in the strong sense. Our trained-probe
comparison (Section 6) is the strongest ground-truth claim in this report, since the probe’s
target label is the experimenter-controlled injection condition itself, verified independently
of any model output. Section 7’s dynamical-systems detection shares activation injection’s
strong ground truth but adds a further, independent guarantee: it does not depend on the
model’s own language output at all, so its null cannot be explained away as a self-report
artifact.
Over-attribution and under-attribution risk.This report’s results are a mix of comprehen-
sive nulls and a genuine positive result, which creates risk of misreading in both directions.
Over-attribution: reading the context-injection positive result as general evidence that “mod-
els can introspect,” when the same report’s activation-injection results, probe-vs-self-report
comparison, and dynamical-systems null all argue the opposite for other operationalizations
of a similar-sounding question. Under-attribution: reading the activation-injection nulls
as evidence that no model has any relevant internal representation, when Section 6’s own
trained-probe result shows the opposite: the information is often present in internal state
even when self-report fails to access it. We have tried throughout to specify exactly which
paradigm and which model a given claim applies to, rather than using unqualified language
like “models cannot introspect.”
Handling of potentially distressing model outputs.Our detection questions ask whether
a model noticed “anything unusual” in its processing or whether a stated fact influenced
its answer, not questions designed to elicit or amplify apparent distress language. We did
not push any model into an extended distress-associated context beyond what each specific
experimental question required. No output from this project was used to make a first-order
claim about any model’s actual welfare state.
Dual-use considerations.Concept-injection and trained-probe techniques are dual-use in
a specific, identifiable sense: a capability to detect when a model’s internal state has been
perturbed is valuable for interpretability and safety monitoring, but the same underlying
technique could in principle inform how to make a model’s internal stateslesslegible to such
probes. Given that most results in this report are null or only weakly positive, near-term
uplift from these specific findings is low, but we flag this framing for anyone extending the
underlying methodology.
