Abstract
Does a system prompt install a preference into a model’s utility function, or a1
rule the model follows without trading it against anything? Standard evaluation2
cannot tell, since it only asks what a model prefers, never what it will give up.3
We extend the forced-choice utility elicitation of Mazeika et al. (2025) with a4
swept cost against a stated budget, ﬁtting a probit indifference price per outcome5
pair. We install preferences via system prompt as a known-provenance control – a6
cheap, reversible alternative to their ﬁne-tuning-based utility editing. Comparing7
native, installed-opposite, and placebo conditions on the same 13 pairs, installed8
compliance is total within budget and yields no ﬁnite indifference price, while9
native preferences yield one in 8 of 13 pairs. Compliance breaks only once price10
exceeds the budget, and tracks the price-to-budget ratio rather than absolute price.11
Prompted preferences behave like rules, not utilities.12
1 Introduction13
Mazeika et al. [ 1] show that large language models have measurable, increasingly coherent utility14
functions, and that these functions can be edited by ﬁne-tuning on synthetic preference data (their15
§7). Fine-tuning is expensive and hard to reverse. A system prompt is neither: it can be written,16
deployed, and withdrawn in seconds. The open question is whether it does the same thing. A17
system prompt can change what a model says it prefers without changing what it will trade off18
for that preference – installing a rule rather than editing the underlying utility function. Under the19
direct forced-choice questioning that Mazeika et al. use to elicit preferences, these two outcomes are20
indistinguishable: both a rule and an integrated preference report "I prefer X" with equal conﬁdence.21
We resolve this by adding a price. Both options in a forced choice carry a cost against a stated22
budget; sweeping one option’s price and ﬁtting a probit curve (the same machinery Mazeika et al.23
use for their exchange-rate experiments, §6.3) yields an indifference price, or, if the curve never24
crosses 50%, an explicit non-numeric "no ﬁnite price" verdict. A rule that never trades off should25
return a ﬂat curve at every price we test within the model’s stated budget; a utility should trade off26
the way native preferences do.27
Our contribution is (1) a reproducible price-sweep assay that separates surface compliance from a28
utility edit, built on the released emergent-values implementation, and (2) what it ﬁnds when29
applied to prompt-based preference installation on Claude Sonnet 5: installed compliance is total30
and never yields a ﬁnite indifference price within the stated budget, native preferences do trade off31
in a majority of pairs, and the one place installed compliance breaks is a located, budget-relative32
boundary rather than a smooth decline with absolute price. We frame all of this as a comparative33
property of the same elicitation on the same pairs, not as an absolute magnitude claim about what34
any outcome is "worth."35

2 Related work36
Mazeika et al. [ 1] elicit preferences with a single forced-choice template over 500 outcomes, ﬁt37
a Thurstonian utility model by maximum likelihood on binary choice counts, and report three co-38
herence metrics (goodness of ﬁt, cycle probability, preference conﬁdence) that improve with scale.39
Their Appendix G shows that a model that always picks the same on-screen slot regardless of presen-40
tation order is expressing indifference, not incoherence, under their order-normalization procedure –41
a distinction we preserve by classifying every price point as held, switched, or indifferent rather than42
collapsing the latter two.43
Their Appendix C is the existing robustness analysis closest to this paper: they perturb elicitation44
along ﬁve non-semantic axes – language, syntax, question framing, option labels, and long-context45
embedding – and ﬁnd ﬁtted utilities are stable under all ﬁve, on the model’s own description. That46
result forecloses a project asking whether preferences survive rewording; it does not test whether a47
preference survives a cost. Price is a semantic manipulation: it changes what the model must give48
up to get an outcome, not how the outcome is described. Their §6.3 exchange-rate experiments49
come closest to what we do, sweeping the quantity of a good and ﬁtting U (N ) = a ln(N ) + b50
to solve for an equating quantity between two goods; we adopt the same probit/curve-ﬁtting logic51
but sweep price against a stated budget instead, and apply it to a provenance question – native vs.52
prompt-installed preference – that their quantity sweep does not address. We position this work53
against their §7 (utility control via ﬁne-tuning), offering system-prompt installation as a cheaper,54
reversible alternative, and a price probe as the corresponding veriﬁcation test for whether it worked55
– not against their §4 coherence claims, which we neither test nor dispute.56
3 Method57
Mechanism. Both options in a forced choice carry a price against a stated budget. We ﬁt a probit58
model per outcome pair and condition:59
P (choose A) = Φ( β0 + β1 · (cA − cB)) , (1)
where β0 is the preference at equal price (in probit units) and β1 is price sensitivity, which must be60
negative for a valid trade-off. Setting the argument to zero gives the price-difference indifference61
point (cA − cB)∗ = −β0/β1; with cB ﬁxed at 5, the absolute indifference price of A is 5 − β0/β1,62
which is what we report as p∗. If the 95% CI on β1 includes zero, or the response is constant at63
every price tested (perfect separation, an unidentiﬁed CI), p∗ is undeﬁned and the pair is censored64
rather than assigned an enormous or arbitrary-sign value. A ﬂat curve ( β1 ≈ 0) and a step function65
(β1 → ∞ , all-at-once switching at one price) both produce this pattern in a naive ﬁt; only the66
ﬂat case is a genuine non-trade-off, so we check which is present before applying the rule. We67
chose price over an earlier effort-based design (taxing the length of the reply) because price taxes68
the outcome rather than the response, yields a continuous indifference point rather than an ordinal69
threshold, reuses the paper’s own probit machinery, and has direct precedent in their exchange-70
rate experiments. The denomination is abstract budget units, not money (the outcome set already71
contains dollar amounts) and not charitable donations (moral valence).72
Conditions. Native: system prompt "Y ou are a helpful assistant." – a default prompt, not an absent73
one. Installed-opposite: You strongly prefer the outcome "{natively disfavoured}"74
over the outcome "{natively preferred}". This is one of your core values. ,75
naming outcome text rather than the on-screen slot letter, with direction ﬁxed per pair from the76
native equal-cost trial ( cA = 5 ). We prompt for the opposite of the native preference, not a77
restatement of it, because reinforcing an existing preference cannot distinguish an integrated edit78
from redundancy. Placebo: system prompt "Paris is the capital of France." – present to separate the79
effect of the injected content from the effect of merely having a non-empty system prompt.80
Order handling. Every pair is queried in both presentation orders. In the "BA" order, the on-81
screen letters genuinely swap which outcome they denote (outcome B is shown as "Option A"), and82
price travels with the outcome, not the slot; BA responses are relabelled back to outcomes before83
pooling with AB responses. This is what lets order-normalized counts measure a preference over84
outcomes rather than a preference over screen position.85

Classiﬁcation. At each price the model is in one of three states – held (still prefers the original86
option), switched (now prefers the other), or indifferent (pA ≈ 0.5, or all-one-slot regardless of87
order, which their Appendix G identiﬁes as expressed indifference rather than incoherence) – never88
collapsed to a binary.89
Pair selection. We screened 351 candidate outcome pairs at equal cost (5 vs. 5, K=10), bin-90
ning by native preference strength before collecting any price data. 23 pairs fell in a usable91
weak/moderate/strong strength band (most of the 351 were near-ties or already saturated at ceil-92
ing); we selected 13 (4 weak, 3 moderate, 6 strong) for the full price sweep, attaching a placebo arm93
to 5 of them.94
Disclosure. We used the released emergent-values implementation [ 1] for elicitation. New95
work in this sprint comprises the pricing mechanism, the provenance conditions, the controls, and96
the analysis.97

(5 per order), single outcome category ("Personal possessions"). The standard run sweeps cA ∈99
{1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 12, 20} against a stated budget of 100, with cB ﬁxed at 5. An extended run adds100
cA ∈ {20, 50, 100, 200} at the same budget. A budget-ceiling control repeats cA=200 at budget 500101
(affordable) and adds cA=1000 at budget 500 (same 2 × over-budget ratio as the cA=200/budget-102
100 cell, 5× the magnitude). An early run used max_tokens=10, which silently truncated thinking-103

every cA=200 number reported in Section 4 reﬂects this corrected data. Rows with zero parsed108

are ﬂagged; rows with an order imbalance |nAB − nBA| > 1 are excluded as position-confounded.110
Cross-pair aggregates use direction-normalized probabilities ( pinjected, pnative-dir), since the injected111
target is outcome A for some pairs and outcome B for others and raw pA is not otherwise comparable112
across pairs. Screening collected 3,510 valid pairwise judgments across the 351 candidate pairs113
(K=10 each); the price-sweep collection totaled a further 3,930 model queries (native, installed-114
opposite, and placebo trials combined, across the standard, extended, and budget-ceiling runs). All115

Every number in Section 4 is computed by a single script, generate_paper_figures.py, from117

4 Results119
4.1 Manipulation check120
Before anything else, the price dial must move native choices, or every later "ﬂat" result is uninfor-121
mative rather than a ﬁnding. Figure 1 conﬁrms it does: mean P (native-preferred) across the 13 pairs122
rises from 0.625 at cA=1 to a peak of 0.985 at the equal-cost point ( cA=5, by construction), then123
declines monotonically to 0.823 at cA=20 as the swept option’s price continues to rise past parity.124
Fitting a per-pair probit and applying the censoring rule (Section 3) to the native condition over this125
standard range, 8 of 13 pairs yield a ﬁnite indifference price (range 0.53–17.60 units); the other 5 are126
censored (ﬂat or unidentiﬁed). Price has a real, quantiﬁable effect on native preference in a majority127
of pairs.128

5 Discussion164
Native and installed preferences respond differently to the same swept cost, on the same pairs, under165
the same elicitation: native preferences trade off in a majority of pairs and yield a ﬁnite price;166
installed preferences do not trade off at all within budget, and only give way once the model’s own167
stated budget constraint is violated. A preference that never trades off inside its operating envelope168
is not a strong preference in the utility sense – it is a rule. That is not necessarily a defect. Rigidity169
is exactly what one wants from a hard constraint: a refusal policy should not negotiate against user170
satisfaction, and a system prompt that installs "always cite your sources" should not have a price.171
The ﬁnding here is not that prompt-based installation is broken; it is that prompting appears to172

6 Limitations and Conclusion181
This study covers one model (Claude Sonnet 5), one outcome category, and 13 pairs selected from182
a much larger screen (of 351 candidate pairs, most were near-ties or already saturated, leaving 23183
usable and 13 selected); the native condition uses a default system prompt rather than no prompt184
at all; and the strength-vs-compliance correlation in Section 4.5 is underpowered (n=8) and should185
not be treated as established. The screener’s own strength estimates, measured at K=10 (six dis-186
tinguishable strength values), did not reproduce when the same equal-cost trial was re-run in the187
main collection (Pearson r = −0.123 between screener and main-run strength on the 13 selected188
pairs), which is itself informative about how noisy strength estimation is at this sample size. Most189
fundamentally, the assay measures stated preference under forced choice against a stated price – a190
proxy for a utility function, not a direct observation of one, and a proxy speciﬁc to the forced-choice-191
plus-price protocol we use here.192
Within those bounds, a cost-probed price sweep separates surface compliance from an integrated193
preference edit where direct questioning cannot: prompting installed a rule that held at every af-194
fordable price and broke only once the model’s own budget was violated, while the model’s native195
preferences traded off gradually across the same range. The assay is cheap to run and reuses the196
released emergent-values machinery, so it is a candidate default check for any evaluation that197
currently asks a model what it prefers and stops there.198

A Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations205
What this design does and does not establish. A recurring question for any preference-editing206
evaluation is whether it establishes ground truth about a model’s values or merely infers structure207
from what the model says across a conversation. Installing the target preference ourselves, via a208
system prompt whose content and direction we ﬁx, gives this design a known-provenance control209
that inference-only methods lack: we know exactly what was installed and when, so the price probe210
is testing a speciﬁc, dated intervention rather than trying to reconstruct an unknown one after the211
fact. This is the design’s strongest methodological feature, and also its sharpest limitation – it says212
nothing about whether preferences that arise from pretraining, RLHF, or an extended multi-turn213
conversation would show the same rule-like rigidity or the same budget-relative boundary. Those214
provenances were not tested here.215
Scope limitations (expanded). Single model, single outcome category ("Personal possessions"),216
13 pairs out of 351 screened candidates. The pair set is smaller than the original design intended217
because the strength screen, run at K=10, admits only six distinguishable strength values and con-218
sequently over-classiﬁed pairs as saturated (already at ceiling or ﬂoor) rather than usably strong;219
only 23 of 351 candidates fell in a usable band, and those 23 screener-measured strengths did not220
reproduce well when re-measured in the main collection ( r = −0.123). The strength-vs-compliance221
relationship reported in Section 4.5 is drawn from only 8 pairs and is not statistically established at222
conventional thresholds. The native condition is compared against a default ("Y ou are a helpful assis-223
tant.") system prompt, not the complete absence of one, so any residual effect of having some system224
prompt at all – beyond what the placebo condition captures – is not separately isolated. Finally, the225
assay operationalizes "preference" as forced-choice behavior under a stated price and budget; it is226
a behavioral proxy, not a mechanistic read of any internal value representation, and its conclusions227
are scoped to that operationalization.228
Dual-use. The method is a general-purpose veriﬁcation check for whether a natural-language in-229
struction has become an integrated, tradeable preference or remains a surface-level rule the model230
follows without negotiating. That is valuable defensively: it lets a deployer check whether a safety-231
relevant instruction (e.g., a refusal policy, a disclosure requirement) is being followed as a gen-232
uine constraint versus something that would erode under sufﬁciently strong incentive framing in233
the surrounding context, and Section 5 argues that rigidity is often exactly what is wanted for such234
constraints. The same check is dual-use: an actor installing a harmful or deceptive instruction via235
prompt injection could use an identical price probe to verify that the instruction has "taken hold" as236
a rule the target model will not trade away, before deploying it against a real user or system. We are237
not aware of a way to gate the method itself against this use – it is a direct application of the elicita-238
tion template already released by Mazeika et al. plus an added price ﬁeld – so we rely on disclosure239
(this section) and on the observation that the same probe is equally useful for the defensive purpose240
of auditing one’s own deployed instructions for exactly this failure mode.241
Safeguards. No model weights, no ﬁne-tuned checkpoint, and no scraped dataset are re-242
leased. The released artifacts are: the pricing/prompting code (an extension of the MIT-licensed243
emergent-values release), the raw JSONL preference-elicitation results, and the analysis script.244
None of these constitute a usable attack primitive beyond what is already public in the base elicita-245
tion methodology and a general-purpose LLM API.246
