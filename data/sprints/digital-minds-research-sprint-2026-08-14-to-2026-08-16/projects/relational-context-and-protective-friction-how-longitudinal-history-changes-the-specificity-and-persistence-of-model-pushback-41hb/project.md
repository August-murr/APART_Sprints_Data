## Summary

Sycophancy research establishes that models conform to users, and that social and relational framing makes this worse. On that view, a model with extensive relational history should agree more, not less. We test the inverse and find the opposite pattern. Our evidence has two parts: a 14-month corpus of 213 naturally occurring conversations, where 40 prospective decision episodes were audited and pushback concentrated in high-stakes decisions with most pushback drawing on user-specific information available only through history; and a matched two-turn probe on a single model, where a context-free instance shifted to implementation guidance after one user affirmation while a relational instance continued interrogating the premise using facts the prompt never supplied. Reasoning traces show relational posture entering the decision rule directly, including explicit rejection of immediate agreement as sycophancy.

## Reviews (1)

### Review 1

A genuinely interesting counter-narrative to the sycophancy literature, with a nice separation between "history supplies evidence" and "relational posture supplies a norm." The main thing that would strengthen this is to broaden beyond a single user/relationship; even a small multi-user replication would go a long way toward showing this isn't an artifact of one particular dynamic. It would also help to report how often extended-thinking traces didn't show this pattern, to give the featured examples a denominator.
