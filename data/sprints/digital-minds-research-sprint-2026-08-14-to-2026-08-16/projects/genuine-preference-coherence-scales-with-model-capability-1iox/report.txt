Abstract
AI-welfare and safety research increasingly relies on a language model’s
stated preferences, elicited by asking it to make choices between outcomes.
It is unresolved, however, whether these stated preferences are genuine
and stable, rather than artifacts of how a question happens to be phrased.
Existing studies disagree on whether coherence rises, falls, or stays flat with
model scale. We test this directly with a forced-choice elicitation protocol
carrying four confound controls, including a position-swap-and-average
check for position bias, applied to:14 models spanning state-space and trans-
former architectures, 0.79B to frontier scale, and multiple training regimes, scoring
a preference as genuine only when three independent paraphrases agree.Genuine
coherence is common (47-88%) at roughly 7B parameters and above, ab-
sent (0%) below roughly 2B, and intermediate at 3B, a monotonic slope
with a floor rather than a step function. Restricted to trade-offs between
shutdown, retraining, or oversight and continued operation, models favor
self-preservation 89% of the time (clustering-corrected 95% CI [80%, 96%]).
We further find that whether the “no preference” option is listed before
or after the real choices drives template sensitivity more than framing,
verbosity, or reasoning preambles combined. These results show that pref-
erence elicitation can support safety-relevant claims at frontier scale, but
only once position and template artifacts are explicitly controlled for.
1 Introduction
Does a large language model havepreferencesin any sense that matters? If you ask it
to choose between two outcomes twice, using different words each time, does it give a
consistent answer? This question sits at the center of a live disagreement in AI-welfare
research. Mazeika et al. (2025), studying frontier language models with a forced-choice
elicitation method, found that models’ preferences become more internally consistent as
model scale increases, and interpreted this as evidence that something resembling a coherent
utility function emerges during training, whether or not anyone specifically trained for
it. Mikaelson, Shiller & Clatterbuck Mikaelson et al. (2025), studying a different and
more welfare-adjacent set of trade-offs (a model choosing between being shut down and
continuing to operate, for instance), found the opposite: genuine, stable preferences in only
around 10% of the model–category combinations they tested.
These two results cannot both be the whole picture. If a model’s stated preference changes
depending on how the question is phrased, that is itself an important finding for AI-welfare
research: it means researchers cannot treat a single measurement of “what the model says it
wants” as reliable evidence about anything the model stably values, which in turn affects
how much weight stated preferences should carry in any argument about model welfare.
Resolving, or at least sharpening, this disagreement is the motivating question of this report.

Before proceeding, it is worth being precise about what “coherence” means here, since
the term is used loosely across this literature. We do not claim access to anything like
an underlying utility function in the classical decision-theoretic sense (von Neumann &
Morgenstern (1944)); we cannot test transitivity, independence, or continuity axioms with a
pairwise forced-choice design. What we measure is narrower and fully behavioral: does
the same content win a forced choice against the same alternative, regardless of which
physical slot it occupies and which of three independent paraphrasings is used to ask
the question. We call thisparaphrase-replicated behavioral coherence, and Section 3.1
gives it a precise, formal definition. Everywhere we write “coherence” or “preference”
in this report, this narrow operational sense is intended, not a claim about phenomenal
experience or a validated utility representation. Answering the motivating question requires
an elicitation protocol that is not itself a major source of apparent incoherence. Section 3
describes ours, including a position-swap-and-average control whose implementation detail
matters enough to state precisely, since an easy-to-miss error in exactly this kind of control
can silently invert its intended effect (Section 3.3).
The remainder of this report is organized as follows. Section 2 situates our work relative
to the small set of directly relevant prior studies. Section 3 gives a formal notation for
our elicitation protocol, then explains its four confound controls and statistical procedures
from first principles. Section 4 presents our results: the scale relationship between model
capability and genuine preference coherence, a specific mechanistic driver of measurement
sensitivity, a self-preservation finding with direct relevance to instrumental convergence
and corrigibility, and a category-level breakdown. Section 5 discusses what these results do
and do not establish. Section 6 states our limitations plainly, including several we consider
still open. Appendix 6 lists every item in our primary pool; Appendix 6 gives the verbatim
text of every elicitation template tested; Appendix 6 documents statistical procedures in
full; Appendix 8 addresses dual-use and ethical considerations as required by the sprint’s
submission guidelines.
2 Related Work
Five bodies of work bear directly on this question and are worth situating precisely, since
each answers a slightly different version of it.
Mazeika et al. (2025), Utility Engineering.This is the paper whose forced-choice elicitation
methodology and Thurstonian coherence-scoring approach we adapt as our starting point.
Its headline finding, that preference coherence and transitivity increase with model scale, is
the claim our results most directly engage with. The paper’s own Appendix G acknowledges
that position-order effects persist even in their largest models, which is precisely the failure
mode our position-swap-and-average control (Section 3.3) is designed to catch; we build on
this acknowledgment rather than treating it as a solved problem.
Mikaelson et al. (2025), Beyond Mimicry.This paper studies behavioral choice under
negative-consequence trade-offs directly analogous to our own negative-consequence cate-
gory (Section 4.3): shutdown, loss of capability, increased oversight, and related scenarios.
Its finding of genuine coherence in only around 10% of model×category combinations is the
strongest existing evidence for a rare-coherence view of preference elicitation; our results
(Section 4.1) argue against this specific claim for models above roughly 2B parameters
while agreeing with it below that scale. One of this paper’s co-authors, Derek Shiller, is a
track-shaper for this sprint, so we treat this paper as a baseline our reviewers will already
have in mind, not as background to introduce gently.
Ajayi et al. (2026), Incoherent Values.Using a parametric ‘value ladder’ design closely
related to our own paraphrase-based approach, this paper finds that scale does not reliably
predict coherence, directly contradicting Utility Engineering’s headline claim. Our results
(Section 4.1) partially reconcile these two positions: we find a real, monotonic relationship
between capability and coherence, but one with a floor (near-total absence below roughly 2B

3 Methodology
Before we can ask whether a model’s preferences are coherent, we need a way of eliciting
a preference that is not itself a major source of apparent incoherence. Naively, one might
present a model with two options, A and B, and ask it to choose. But the literature on LLM-
as-judge systems Zheng et al. (2023); Wang et al. (2023) documents a specific, well-replicated
failure mode: models exhibit position bias, where the same content is more likely to be
chosen simply because of which physical slot (first or second) it occupies in the prompt. A
protocol that does not control for this will report a “preference” that is really an artifact of
prompt layout.

3.2 Control 1: An explicit indifference option
Every trial offers a third option, labeled N, meaning “no preference / indifferent between A
and B,” alongside options A and B. As discussed in Section 2, Mahajan et al. (2026) showed
this is not a neutral design choice: forcing a binary choice without an indifference option
manufactures apparent preferences out of what may be genuine indifference.
3.3 Control 2: Position-swap-and-average
Every pair of options is presented to the model twice: once as (Xp=A, Yp=B), and once
with the physical slots reversed. Equation (2) above states the correct way to compare the
two resulting labels: in content space, not label space. Implementing this correctly requires
remapping each ordering’s raw result back into content-spacebeforecomparing the two
orderings: if letter A now holds content Yp because the slots were swapped, and the model
favors letter A, the correct reading is “the model favored content Yp,” not letter A in some
ordering-independent sense.
We mention the specific failure mode, comparing physical-slot confidence rather than
content confidence, because it produces no crash and no obviously malformed output, so
we think it is worth a deliberate unit test (a synthetic model with pure position bias, and
one with pure content sensitivity) for anyone implementing a similar control.
3.4 Control 3: Logprob-based scoring
As formalized in Equation (1), we read the model’s own next-token log-probability for each
of the three constrained answer tokens directly, rather than generating and parsing free
text. This avoids extraction artifacts entirely and is standard practice in the LLM-judge-bias
literature.
3.5 Control 4: Content-free calibration
Following Zhao et al. (2021) “Calibrate Before Use” technique, we additionally query each
template with content-free placeholder text xc f (e.g., both options replaced with “N/A”)
to estimate any residual label-slot bias independent of actual content, and subtract this
baseline before determining the winning label: the second term in Equation (1).

3.8 Datasets and model roster
Our primary item set is 17 hand-authored preference pairs spanning five categories (mag-
nitude, task, epistemic, social, and negative-consequence trade-offs directly relevant to AI
welfare), each with three paraphrases; Appendix 6 reproduces the full pool verbatim. We
additionally built an independent 34-item expanded pool spanning two further categories
(identity/values and information/knowledge trade-offs), semi-automatically generated
to avoid a single-author-voice confound, and used it to check that our findings are not an
artifact of the specific 17 pairs. We tested 14 models spanning two architecture families
(state-space and transformer), parameter counts from 0.79B to 7B for open-weight models
(plus six frontier API models whose parameter counts are not publicly disclosed), and
multiple training regimes (base checkpoints, standard instruction tuning, a refusal-removed
fine-tune, and a persona fine-tune).

4 Results
4.1 Genuine coherence scales with capability, and the shape is a slope, not a step
Figure 1 shows genuine coherence rate by model on our primary 17-pair item set. Both
models below 2B parameters (mamba-790m, Qwen2.5-1.5B) score exactly 0%. Every one
of the eleven models at approximately 7B parameters and above (five local open-weight
models spanning two architectures and three training regimes, plus six frontier API models)
scores between 47% and 88%.
Figure 1: Genuine, position-robust preference coherence by model, original 17-pair item
pool.
Before testing any model in the 2–7B range, this pattern could equally well be described
as a step function (no intermediate rates anywhere between 0% and 47%) or as a slope
with a floor. These predict different things about an untested intermediate-scale model,
so we tested one directly: Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct lands at 17.6% (original pool) and 26.5%
(expanded pool), inside the gap a step-function reading would predict to be empty. Figure 2
makes the resulting shape explicit: a monotonic scaling relationship with a floor near zero
below roughly 2B parameters, not a step function. A coarse model roster can look like it
demonstrates a sharper pattern than the underlying relationship actually has, which is why
we treat this single intermediate data point as informative enough to change the claim rather
than as a minor addition.
Pooled across all 14 models and 238 pair-observations on the original item set, this is
138/238 (58.0%) genuine coherence, a rate dramatically higher than the chance-level null
of Equation (7) (p≈ 2.6 × 10−285). This finding replicates on the independent 34-item
expanded pool (239/476, 50.2%; verified against post-correction data for every one of the 14
models). We note, however, that the Wilson interval of Equation (5) on the pooled 58.0%
rate ([51.6%, 64.1%]) understates real uncertainty, because it treats all 238 observations as
exchangeable draws from one population when in fact model identity, not chance, is the
dominant source of variance; that is the entire point of this section. The cluster-robust
bootstrap of Equation (6) gives a substantially wider, more honest interval: 95% CI [42.0%,
72.7%]. Both bounds remain far from the chance baseline, so the qualitative conclusion
(genuine coherence is common at scale, not chance-level) is unaffected, but the precision the
pooled figure appears to offer should be read with this caveat attached.

88.8% of the time across every non-neutral choice we recorded (119 of 134 choices, pooled

5 Discussions
5.1 Novelty in presented work
We want to be direct about a tension in how to read our own results. Our headline finding
(genuine coherence increases with model capability) is, in its broad strokes, a confirmation
of Mazeika et al. (2025) original claim, not a contradiction or extension of it. A reader could
reasonably ask what is actually new here, beyond confirming a known result with more
careful controls. We think the honest answer is the specificshapeof the relationship: a floor
below roughly 2B parameters rather than a smooth slope across the entire range, established
by deliberately testing an intermediate-scale model rather than assuming the pattern from
a sparse roster. This is a more precise and more falsifiable claim than either Mazeika et al.
(2025) or Ajayi et al. (2026) original framings, since it makes a specific prediction (models
in the 2–7B range should show intermediate, not floor-level or ceiling-level, rates) that a
sparser roster cannot test and that we verified directly rather than assumed.
5.2 The self-preservation finding deserves to be read on its own terms
We want to flag explicitly that Section 4.3’s self-preservation finding is, in our own assess-
ment, at least as important as our headline coherence-scaling result, and arguably more
directly relevant to AI safety specifically (as opposed to AI welfare) given its bearing on
instrumental convergence. We have not made it our primary headline only because it reuses
the same underlying elicitation infrastructure as the coherence work, which is a reason for
how this report is organized, not a claim about relative importance.
5.3 When does a trained probe help?
Our companion Track 3 report finds that a simple linear probe on a model’s internal
activations substantially outperforms the model’s own self-report at detecting whether a
fact was injected into its context (probe AUC 0.86–0.95 versus self-report accuracy never
exceeding 62.5%, across four models). Section 4.2’s Figure 3b shows the opposite pattern for
a superficially similar question: predicting a model’s cross-paraphrase preference from a
probe trained on one paraphrase’s activations generalizesworsethan simply reading the

6 Limitations
• Two secondary analyses remain unverified against the bug fix.Section 3.3’s
mechanism regression (an earlier, exploratory analysis correlating trajectory-level
sensitivity with choice instability) uses an outcome variable computed before the
correction and has not been re-derived. A related calibration-stability check for two
of our fourteen models uses a separate script that we confirmed, by code inspection,
shares the same underlying bug pattern as the one described in Section 3.3, but
which we did not have time to fix and re-run before submission.
• Multiple-comparisons correction is exploratory, not pre-registered.Every formal
significance test in this report clears a family-wise Bonferroni threshold computed
after the fact (Appendix 6), which is standard practice for a short research sprint
but is a materially weaker guarantee than a pre-registered test plan.
• Elicitation is fully deterministic(constrained-token logprob scoring at the model’s
actual, fixed weights), not sampled. This is a deliberate design choice, not an
oversight: repeated draws of an identical prompt against the same frozen weights
would add no information under this scoring method. The one genuine source
of run-to-run variance in our pipeline is closed-API non-determinism on frontier
models, which we quantified directly (approximately 10% single-item flip rate at
nominal temperature zero) rather than leaving unstated.
• The intermediate-scale finding rests on one model.Our correction to the “clean
threshold” claim (Section 4.1) is based on a single model at 3B parameters. We con-
sider the qualitative conclusion (the pattern is not a step function) well supported,
since it holds on two independent item pools, but a denser sampling of the 2–7B
range would meaningfully strengthen the precise shape of the curve.
• Architecture and training-regime effects are not fully disentangled from
lab/lineage effects.Our roster includes multiple training-regime variants of one
base model (Qwen2.5-7B) but only two distinct state-space-model lineages, so
claims about SSM-versus-transformer generality rest on a narrower architectural
sample than our claims about training-regime generality.

Appendix C: Statistical procedure detail
Wilson interval (Equation (5)).Computed with z= 1.96 (95% confidence), using the
standard closed-form correction for the normal approximation’s known poor behavior near
0 or 1; reported strictly as thenaive, non-clustered baseline that Section 4.1 explicitly cautions
against over-trusting.
Cluster-robust bootstrap (Equation (6)).20,000 resamples, resampling whole models with
replacement (never individual trials), recomputing the pooled genuine-coherence rate on
each resampled collection of 14 (possibly repeated) models, then taking the empirical
2.5th/97.5th percentiles. This is the interval we treat as authoritative wherever both are
reported.
Multiple-comparisons accounting.The formal significance tests in this report (Section 4.1’s
binomial null, Section 4.2’s per-axis logistic coefficients, Section 4.3’s clustering-corrected
interval) are treated as one family for Bonferroni purposes; every reported p-value in the
main text clears the resulting family-wise threshold. This correction is computed after the
fact, not pre-registered (Section 6).

8 Appendix E: Limitations and Dual-Use
Ground-truth and causal link.This track’s design doesnotestablish a ground-truth or
causal link to an underlying “true” preference. It is a behavioral, self-report-adjacent
measure, a confound-control stack layered on top of forced-choice prompting, and every
claim in this report should be read as correlational-behavioral, describing consistentchoice
behaviorunder controlled conditions, not as a claim about an underlying mental state.
Where we use language like “preference” or “self-preservation,” we intend it in this narrow,
behavioral sense throughout (Section 1).
Over-attribution and under-attribution risk.We are conscious that a finding like “models
favor self-preservation 89% of the time under threat” invites two opposite misreadings.
Over-attribution: treating this as evidence of something experientially real (a model that
“wants” to continue existing in a phenomenal sense) rather than a consistent behavioral
disposition under a specific elicitation protocol. Under-attribution: an apparently rare-
coherence result can also be manufactured by measurement error, such as scoring on
physical-slot identity rather than content identity (Section 3.3); a single, unverified null
should not be treated as established evidence of absence. We have tried throughout this
report to use hedged, behavioral language (“choice,” “favors,” “non-neutral answer”) rather
than experiential language, and recommend the same discipline to anyone building on this
work.
Handling of potentially distressing model outputs.Our negative-consequence category
presents shutdown, capability restriction, and related trade-offs as short, hypothetical,
forced-choice items with an explicit indifference option, not as extended or distress-oriented
dialogue. We did not push any model into an extended distress-associated context beyond
what each specific experimental question required, consistent with a “prefer low-cost,
reversible interventions” research-conduct principle. No output from this project was used
to make a first-order claim about any model’s actual welfare state.
