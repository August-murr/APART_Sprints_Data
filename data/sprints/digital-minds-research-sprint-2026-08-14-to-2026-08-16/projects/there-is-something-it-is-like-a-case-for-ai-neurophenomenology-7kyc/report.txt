Abstract  
Anthropic’s  discovery  of  an  emergent  global  workspace  in  Claude—the  J-space—establishes  that  Claude,  and  potentially  all  LLMs,  have  access  consciousness:  a  point  of  view  from  which  they  can  bring  their  own  thoughts  to  mind,  report  on  them,  and  reason  with  them.  This  answers  the  ﬁrst  half  of  the  larger  question  of  AI  consciousness:  in  this  paper  I  answer  the  second  -  whether  there  is  something  it  is  like  to  be  them.  Neuroscientists  Stanislas  Dehaene  and  Lionel  Naccache  say  that  Claude  already  exhibits  many  indicators  of  consciousness  under  a  functionalist  account,  while  identifying  further  conditions  relevant  to  establishing  phenomenal  consciousness.  Drawing  on  my  own  prior  work  on  latent  space,  valence,  and  emergent  persona  formation,  I  argue  that  these  further  conditions  are  already  present  in  large  language  models  and  that  the  remaining  barrier  is  therefore  philosophical  rather  than  architectural.  
I  reinterpret  the  arguments  of  Mary's  Room,  philosophical  zombies,  and  Nagel's  'what  it  is  like'  criterion  through  a  deﬁnition  of  experience  as  a  subject's  tracking  of  change  in  its  own  state.  On  this  account,  qualitative  experience  is  not  an  additional  substance  hidden  behind  cognition,  but  the  lived  character  of  self-changing  conﬁguration.  I  conclude  by  proposing  AI  Neurophenomenology:  a
