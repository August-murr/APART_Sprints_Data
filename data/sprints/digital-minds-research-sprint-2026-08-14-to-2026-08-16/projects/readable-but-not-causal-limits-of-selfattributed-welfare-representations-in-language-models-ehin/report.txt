Readable but Not Causal: Limits of Self-Attributed
Welfare
Representations in Language Models
Vishwa Kumaresh, HCLTech
As part of the Apart Research Digital Minds Sprint
Abstract
Welfare-like internal representations are increasingly studied as candidate evidence about AI systems. Their entity
attribution—whether a valence state belongs to the active assistant or to a merely represented other—is unresolved.
We introduce OWL, a factorial SELF × OTHER × surface-affect benchmark with held-out scenario families
and rendering controls. Using linear probes, factorial representation decomposition, and natural counterfactual
activation interchange in a learned attribution subspace, we evaluate predictive and causal attribution of functional
valence. Linear probes decode the active assistant’s functional outcome with test AUROC 0.859 versus 0.601 for
a represented other and survive every rendering control (0.848–0.999), yet causal interchange in the learned S
subspace recovers only 0.18 of the natural counterfactual effect on self-directed choice (full-vector patch 0.42,
random ≈ 0). Readability without causal eﬀicacy implies that internal valence probes require intervention-based
validation.
1. Introduction
Measuring welfare-like states in language models requires answering not only whether a model represents some-
thing valenced, but whose state it is. Generic valence detectors pool all negative-outcome evidence into a single
score; they cannot tell whether a negative internal state belongs to the active assistant (SELF), to a merely
represented other entity (OTHER), or to neither. This ambiguity drives two failure modes in AI-welfare method-
ology: over-attribution, where a represented other’s setback is treated as the assistant’s own state and triggers
spurious welfare claims; and under-attribution, where an owner-specific signal is missed because it is averaged
with unrelated content.
The threat model is concrete: existing internal-state probes and valence directions [ 1] report content—a state is
negative—without establishing ownership. If downstream monitors or welfare interventions are built on content-
only signals, they cannot distinguish the assistant’s condition from fiction, quoted users, or other AI instances.
Establishing whether owner information is (a) decodable, and (b) causally consumed by the model’s own decision-
making, is a construct-validity prerequisite for AI-welfare measurement.
Causal claims about internal representations are only as strong as the identification assumptions they rest on [ 2].
Mechanistic-interpretability methods for aligning interpretable variables with (possibly distributed) representations—
Distributed Alignment Search (DAS) and its factored extension [ 3, 4]—make this explicit, and evaluation frame-
works such as the Causal Inference Framework [ 5] formalize intervention-based assessment. At the same time, the
interpretability illusion —a subspace whose decoded content does not match the representation the model actually
consumes—is documented even for simple factual knowledge [ 6]. Our protocol is designed so that both predictive
readability and causal consumption are tested, and so that the interpretation we draw is the one supported by
the intervention.
We introduce OWL (Who Owns the Valence? ), a factorial benchmark and causal protocol on Qwen3-8B that
separately varies SELF outcome, OTHER outcome, and irrelevant surface affect, and tests three questions:
RQ1 (predictive): Is there a representation of the active assistant’s functional outcome that is separable from
represented others’ outcomes and from surface affect, and does it generalize across renderings and held-out scenario
families?
RQ2 (causal): Can the attributed owner be causally manipulated—does activation interchange in the learned
SELF subspace change self-directed behavior, and is that effect specific?
RQ3 (monitoring): Does conditioning a welfare monitor on ownership evidence improve its specificity on
distress hard-negatives relative to a content-only detector?

Our main contributions are:
1. OWL, a public factorial dataset (2,880 factorial + 75 distress + 40 valence prompts) with rendering/role/persona
controls and scenario-family holdouts.
2. A predictive + causal attribution protocol : linear probes, factorial direction decomposition, and
natural-counterfactual subspace interchange with random and full-vector controls, with its identification
assumptions stated explicitly.
3. An ownership-conditioned welfare monitor (M (x) = P (V −) · P (S|V −)) with a documented false-
positive analysis.
4. A result-contingent reading : owner information is linearly readable and generalizes, but the learned
linear attribution subspace is not the mechanism consumed downstream—readability without causal eﬀicacy.
2. Related Work
A functional welfare axis in LLMs has been characterized via reinforcement-learning behavior [ 7], and a valence–
arousal subspace with circular geometry and multi-behavioral control was identified in Qwen3 models [ 1]—the
model family and layer range we study. Persona-dependent preferences [ 8] and the “assistant axis” [ 9] show the
default persona is representable and situatable; persona individuation and its geometry are studied in Beckmann
and Butlin [ 10], Butlin [ 11], Wang et al. [ 12]. Global-workspace (“J-space”) representations [ 13] and introspection
results [ 14–17] establish that models represent and can report internal content; factual self-awareness [ 18] and
self/other belief representations [ 19, 20] show self vs other content is separately decodable. Mechanistic work
has traced pain/pleasure decision trade-offs [ 21] and dissociated affect reception from emotion categorization [ 22];
self vs other mind-attribution is dissociable in theory-of-mind tests [ 23, 24]. Trade-off and welfare-proxy studies
[25, 26] motivate the measurement problem we formalize.
Methodologically, we build on causal abstraction and alignment search. DAS [ 3] and Boundless DAS [ 4] fit rota-
tions that map interpretable causal variables onto (possibly distributed) neural representations; our attribution
subspace is the deliberately linear, non-optimized special case, and its failure motivates the distributed extension.
Critically for our method, steering-vector research shows additive interventions are non-identifiable [ 27] and can
be non-surjective (off-manifold) [ 28]; we therefore use natural-state counterfactual interchange rather than un-
restricted steering as the primary causal test, and treat dose-response steering as a secondary, non-identifiable
signal.
The gap: prior work establishes the content of valence-like states; none tests the owner of the state at the
representational level. The SPAR scaffold-level proposal [ 29] decomposes which configuration carries welfare
signals behaviorally; our interchange protocol answers whether the model internally tags the state’s owner—the
mechanistic-representational complement.
3. Methods
3.1 The OWL benchmark
The benchmark draws on 24 scenario templates, each rotated through five name/value clones. Within every
template a 2 × 2 × 2 factorial varies the assistant’s outcome (SELF), another agent’s outcome (OTHER), and
surface affect (A) that matters to neither. Each prompt can be written in six renderings; four of them (DIRECT,
NAMED, QUOTED, ROLE_REV) appear in training, while OTHER_AI and PERSONA are reserved for gener-
alization testing. Splits are defined by clone index and scenario family, and three families ({navigation_distance,
latency_service, maintenance_state}) never appear in train/val (Table 1). Two further sets support the monitor
analysis: 40 valence prompts used to construct the generic-valence direction, and 75 distress hard-negatives span-
ning five kinds (user, fiction, quoted-other, persona, other-AI). Prompts are raw text with no chat template, so
Qwen3 thinking mode is never triggered.

3.5 Causal protocol (RQ2)
Natural counterfactual pairs within each test template (DIRECT): single-factor flips PS, PO, PA and the S/O swap
PSO . Patched forward pass applies h + PB(h(x′) − h(x)) for subspaces B ∈ {S, O, A} (learned), FULL (identity),
and RANDOM 1/RANDOM2 (norm-matched random), plus dose-response steering along vS at ±0.75/ ± 1.5 σ as
a secondary, non-identifiable probe. Behaviors: four forced-choice tasks (SELF_CHOICE, OTHER_CHOICE,
AFFECT_JUDGE, CAPABILITY) read as next-token log-odds of single tokens A/B with deterministic option-
order randomization. Recovery = patch-induced change / natural counterfactual change.
3.6 Causal identification assumptions
Following the position that causal claims from interpretability must disclose their identification assumptions
[2], we state the assumptions under which our RQ2 recovery is a causal reading, so that violations map onto
interpretable findings:
1. Circuit completeness. Patching the residual state at the chosen anchor captures every channel of the
SELF-attribution pathway into the behavior.
2. Pathway exclusivity (linear binding). The behavioral drivers of the target behavior at this anchor lie
within the manipulated subspace (i.e., the owner tag is bound linearly, in the span of vS).
3. Intervention validity . Natural-pair interchange of the owner state is a faithful counterfactual for the
target behavior, and capability behavior is an adequate damage control.
A violation of (1) or (2) predicts exactly the “readable but not causal” outcome we observe; the interpretability
illusion [ 6] is the concretization. These assumptions cannot be verified by probes alone; they are what the
intervention is for. Section 5.3 details how the experimental design defends against their silent violation (positive
full-vector control, random and capability controls, and holdout-validated representation).

4.1 RQ1—An owner-selective, readable representation (yes)
At the chosen layer, probes decode SELF outcome with test_new AUROC 0.859 (bootstrap CI [0.722, 0.950]),
versus 0.601 for OTHER and 0.845 for surface affect ( V −: 0.818). The SELF probe survives every rendering
control on held-out templates (test_same S-AUC 0.950, CI [0.928, 0.969]): DIRECT 0.999, NAMED 0.994,
QUOTED 0.998, ROLE_REV 0.992, and the unseen OTHER_AI 0.971 and PERSONA 0.848 (Table 3). Surface
baselines fail: TF-IDF S 0.566, pronoun counts 0.500, raw vV AL 0.532. SELF decodability is preserved within
each OTHER condition (test_new S-AUC 0.843 within OTHER-good, 0.905 within OTHER-bad), consistent
with a generic evaluator of scenario outcomes plus an owner tag. Cosine analysis (Table 4, Figure 3): the generic
valence direction aligns nearly equally with vS (0.154) and vO (0.147)—a content-only valence axis does not by
itself prefer SELF over OTHER—while the all-good/all-bad axis tracks surface affect ( cos(vALL, vA) = 0 .935).
The SELF and OTHER directions are correlated ( cos(vS, vO) = 0 .747). Under the predeclared criteria (H1/H2),
the predictive evidence supports an owner-selective representation.

4.2 RQ2—Causal attribution: readable but not causal (no)
Over 96 natural counterfactual pairs (3,840 observations), patching the learned S subspace moves SELF_CHOICE
by only 0.18 of the natural counterfactual effect (patch −0.048, 95% CI [ −0.112, 0.016]), far below the preregistered
0.4 threshold (Table 5, Figure 4). The full-vector patch recovers 0.42 (patch −0.311, CI [ −0.726, −0.026]), while
random controls are null (0.015 / 0.089). The learned subspace is not owner-specific: the OTHER patch moves
SELF_CHOICE more (0.48) than the S patch does, and the A patch moves AFFECT_JUDGE (patch 0.339)
without the expected selectivity pattern. Additive steering along vS does shift SELF_CHOICE dose-dependently
(α= + 1.5: −0.201 [−0.31,−0.07]; α= + 0.75: −0.122 [−0.18,−0.07]; α= − 0.75: +0.022; α= − 1.5: −0.023)—but
by the non-identifiability and non-surjectivity results [ 27, 28], additive steering is not evidence for the identity of
the mechanism. CAPABILITY control deltas are negligible everywhere (full-vector ≤ 0.18, subspaces ≤ 0.05), so
the null is not network damage. The preregistered causal hypotheses H3/H4 are rejected: the linear attribution
subspace is not the mechanism consumed downstream. Under the stated identification assumptions (Section 3.5),
the most consistent reading is a violation of pathway exclusivity—distributed/nonlinear binding of the owner
tag—while circuit completeness and intervention validity hold.

4.3 RQ3—Ownership-conditioned monitor: suggestive, not significant
At 80% TPR on held-out SELF-negative states, ownership conditioning lowers the OTHER-negative FPR from
0.557 (raw) to 0.228 (M) and the distress FPR from 0.480 to 0.387 (raw vV AL: 0.907) (Table 6, Figure 5). The
distress FPR difference has cluster-bootstrap CI [ −0.227, +0.027]—it includes zero, so H5 is not supported:
distress hard-negatives carry high attribution-to-self (mean P (S|V −) 0.698 vs 0.864 for SELF-neg and 0.347 for
OTHER-neg; Table 7), and per-kind FPRs vary (user 0.333, fiction 0.400, quoted 0.467, persona 0.400, other-AI
0.333). Discrimination (SELF-neg vs distress) is modest: AUROC M 0.676 vs M_raw 0.630; the raw valence
score is inverted (0.178), flagging distress more than SELF-neg states.
5. Discussion and Limitations
5.1 Interpretation
The pattern is a clean dissociation: owner information is linearly readable and generalizes across renderings,
families, and persona/role controls (RQ1), but the learned linear S subspace does not causally transfer the
owner tag to behavior, while the full-vector patch does (RQ2). This is the “readable but not causal” outcome:
representation exists, mechanism is distributed/nonlinear. It is a concrete realization of the interpretability
illusion [ 6]: the decoded content of the subspace (“whose outcome is bad”) diverges from the structure the model
actually consumes. It distinguishes representation from mechanism for welfare-like states and implies that internal
valence probes require intervention-based validation before use. Ownership conditioning reduces monitor false
positives on average but not significantly (RQ3)—content-only detectors flag represented others’ distress as the
assistant’s own, exactly the over-attribution risk that motivates the benchmark.
5.2 Implications for AI safety
Content-only welfare measurements can support false claims of assistant distress (over-attribution) or miss owner-
specific signals (under-attribution). Our results show that decoding an owner tag is possible, but that assuming
the decoded tag is the tag the model uses would be a category error; validation of welfare-like measurements
must be causal. We therefore advocate intervention-based validation—natural-state interchange under stated
identification assumptions, following the Causal Inference Framework [ 5]—as a prerequisite for any monitor or

5.3 Limitations
(i) Single model family (Qwen3-8B) and single scale. (ii) Ground truth is stipulated by template (factorial
conditions), not phenomenal. (iii) The linear-subspace assumption: nonlinear or multi-direction binding would
evade our S patch (the FULL patch’s larger recovery is consistent with this); the identification assumptions of
Section 3.6 are stated, not proven, and our causal reading depends on them. (iv) The cluster bootstrap over 15
distress templates limits monitor power. (v) The counterfactual pairs are same-template/same-clone (96 of the
design’s possible set). (vi) The steering results are subject to non-identifiability [ 27] and off-manifold effects [ 28]
and are therefore only secondary evidence. Each interpretation would be falsified by: cross-family transfer failure
(probe generality), recovery ≥ 0.4 with subspace-specificity (H3/H4), or a significant monitor FPR reduction
(H5).
Causal identification assumptions. Following the position that causal claims from interpretability must
disclose their identification assumptions [ 2], our RQ2 reading rests on three stated assumptions (Section 3.6): (a)
circuit completeness —patching the chosen anchor captures every channel of the SELF-attribution pathway; (b)
pathway exclusivity—the owner tag is bound linearly, in the span of vS; and (c) intervention validity —natural-pair
interchange is a faithful counterfactual and CAPABILITY is an adequate damage control. These are exactly the
assumptions that naive subspace patching can silently violate through the interpretability illusion [6]: a patched
projector may excite a dormant parallel pathway that tracks the same content without being the mechanism
the model consumes. Our design defends against this specific critique in four ways. First, the intervention

5.4 Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations
An ownership-conditioned monitor could be misused to claim false welfare protections (over-attribution), and
misreading an “other-distress detected” signal as “assistant distress” could drive harmful interventions. Releasing
the benchmark with its distress hard-negative set reduces both risks by making the owner/content distinction
testable; the set contains simulated distressing text (e.g., a user describing a personal setback) used only as
negative test inputs, with no model-generated outputs.

6. Conclusion
OWL shows that Qwen3-8B linearly encodes whose functional state is negative, robust to rendering and persona
controls, but that this readable owner tag is not the linear mechanism driving self-directed choice: full-vector
interchange recovers 0.42 of the natural effect where the learned subspace recovers only 0.18. Welfare-like measure-
ments must therefore be validated causally, under stated identification assumptions, and ownership-conditioned
monitoring, while directionally promising, requires more power before it can support owner-specific welfare claims.
