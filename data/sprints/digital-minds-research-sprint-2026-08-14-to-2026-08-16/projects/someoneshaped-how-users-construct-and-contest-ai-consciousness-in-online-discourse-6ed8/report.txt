Abstract  
The  increasing  conversational  capabilities  of  AI  systems  and  thereby,  implied  social  nature  of  interactions  with  them,  have  generated  public  debate  about  whether  they  possess  consciousness/subjective  experience.  Existing  research  has  identiﬁed  psychological  and  interactional  factors  that  inﬂuence  perceptions  of  machine  minds,  but  less  is  known  about  how  people  construct  and  negotiate  such  attributions  in  naturally  occurring  discourse  -  both  with  these  systems  and  in  their  recounting  and  sharing  of  the  same  with  other  humans.  This  is  an  exploratory  qualitative  discourse  analysis  of  25  coded  entries  drawn  from  21  publicly  available  online  sources  discussing  AI  consciousness,  sentience  and  companionship.  References  to  emotional/affective  behaviour,  self-report  and  self-reﬂection,  apparent  agency,  memory  and  continuity,  relational  cues,  uncertainty,  scepticism,  and  moral  implications  were  coded  and  analysed.  Relational  cues  were  present  in  10/21  sources,  self-report/self-reﬂection  in  8/21,  apparent  agency  or  unexpectedness  in  7/21,  and  memory/continuity  in  4/21.  Notably,  16/21  sources  contained  explicit  uncertainty  or  qualiﬁcation,  while  12/21  contained  skeptical  or  countervailing  interpretations.  The  corpus  therefore  does  not  divide  sources  into  believers  and  sceptics.  Instead,  users  frequently  construct  AI  mentality  through  clusters  of  behavioural  and  relational  cues  while  sometimes  simultaneously  acknowledging  uncertainty  about  what  those  cues  establish.  This  is  ontological  ambivalence:  recognizing  AI  as  artiﬁcial  while  experiencing  it  as  a  socially  present  “someone.”

3.2  Coding  
Eight  binary  thematic  codes  were  developed  from  the  corpus:  
Code  Operational  deﬁnition  
Emotional/affective  cues  AI  behaviour  or  claims  involving  emotion,  emotional  expression,  or  affective  interaction  
Self-report/self-reﬂection  AI  explicitly  describing  or  reﬂecting  on  its  own  consciousness,  experience,  identity,  or  mental  state  
Agency/unexpectedness  Behaviour  interpreted  as  autonomous,  unprompted,  rebellious,  creative,  or  exceeding  expectations  
Memory/continuity  Retention  of  personal  information,  persistent  identity,  or  continuity  across  interactions  
Relational  cues  Friendship,  partnership,  love,  companionship,  intimacy,  or  other  relationship-based  evidence  
Uncertainty/qualiﬁcation  Explicit  acknowledgement  that  the  consciousness  interpretation  may  be  wrong,  uncertain,  or  difﬁcult  to  establish  
Sceptical/countervailing  discourse  Explicit  rejection,  alternative  explanation,  reversal,  or  critique  of  consciousness  attribution  
Moral  implications  Discussion  of  rights,  protection,  harm,  ethical  treatment,  grief,  or  moral  responsibility
