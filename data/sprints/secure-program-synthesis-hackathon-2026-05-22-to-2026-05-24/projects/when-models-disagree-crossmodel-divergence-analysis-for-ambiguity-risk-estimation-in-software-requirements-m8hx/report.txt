When Models Disagree: Cross-Model Divergence Analysis
 for Ambiguity Risk Estimation in Software Requirements
 Jack Lakkapragada
 Independent AI Safety Contributor
 With Apart Research — Secure Program Synthesis Hackathon, May 2026
Abstract
As AI systems generate increasing volumes of software code, one bottleneck in trustworthy
software development is increasingly shifting upstream: specifying what code should do, and
verifying that it does so. We investigate whether cross-model divergence in specification
generation can serve as a practical signal for requirement underspecification. We present a
structured experimental framework — a 30-requirement dataset spanning three ambiguity tiers,
a six-category divergence taxonomy, and a normalized JSON comparison pipeline — applied
across three contemporary LLMs: Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-4o, and Llama 3.3 70B. Divergence
increases with pre-labeled ambiguity tier (TDR: 7.2, 7.4, 7.9), assumption divergence (ASM)
dominates across all tiers, and contradiction (CON) collapses to zero in adversarially ambiguous
requirements. Critically, operationally clear requirements still exhibit substantial latent
interpretive variance, with many false consensus events in Tier A concentrated in security
constraints. Our framework, taxonomy, and dataset were frozen prior to execution, enabling
reproducible analysis without post-hoc adjustment.
1. Introduction
As AI systems generate increasing volumes of software code, one bottleneck in trustworthy software
development is increasingly shifting upstream. Writing code is increasingly less of a constraint than
specifying intended behavior — what code should do, and verifying that it does so, has become a critical
unsolved problem. Natural language requirements remain the dominant input to software development
processes, yet they are systematically underspecified. Requirements omit edge cases, leave security
constraints implicit, and fail to resolve design decisions that implementation will force into the open.
Large language models now occupy a central role in translating natural language requirements into software
specifications and implementations. When a developer prompts a model with a requirement, the model
produces a specification — implicitly resolving ambiguities, inventing assumptions, and making design
choices the requirement never constrained. The resulting specification may appear complete despite
unresolved design assumptions; a different model, given the same requirement, may resolve those
ambiguities differently.
We investigate whether cross-model divergence in specification generation can serve as a practical signal
for requirement underspecification. Specifically, we ask: given the same natural language requirement, how
much do independently prompted frontier models diverge in the specifications they infer, and what does that
divergence reveal about the requirement's latent ambiguity?

We present a structured experimental framework — a 30-requirement dataset spanning three ambiguity
tiers, a six-category divergence taxonomy, and a normalized JSON comparison pipeline — applied across
three contemporary LLMs: Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-4o, and Llama 3.3 70B. Our approach does not evaluate
which model produces the correct specification. It measures divergence structure as a signal for requirement
quality.
Our main contributions are:
 A reusable cross-model specification comparison framework with a predefined taxonomy, frozen
prompts, and reproducible execution protocol, developed and locked prior to any experimental runs.
 Empirical evidence that divergence increases with pre-labeled ambiguity tier under this experimental
setup, with assumption divergence (ASM) as the dominant category across all tiers and contradiction
(CON) collapsing to zero in adversarially ambiguous requirements.
 An observation that operationally clear requirements still exhibit substantial latent interpretive variance,
with false consensus events concentrated in security constraints — suggesting that specification risk is
not confined to obviously vague requirements.
2. Related Work
Our work builds on four intersecting research areas. Wang et al. (2023) introduced self-consistency
prompting, demonstrating that sampling diverse reasoning paths and selecting the most consistent answer
improves model reliability [1]. We invert this intuition: rather than using consistency to find correct answers,
we use inconsistency across independently prompted models as a signal that the input — the requirement —
was underspecified.
The software engineering literature has long recognized requirement quality as a measurable property.
Femmer et al. (2017) formalized requirements smells — detectable symptoms of quality violations including
ambiguity, incompleteness, and inconsistency — and demonstrated rapid automated detection approaches
[2]. Our divergence taxonomy (omission, contradiction, assumption divergence) extends this tradition from
single-document linguistic detection to cross-model semantic detection.
Kuhn et al. (2023) established that semantic-level disagreement across model outputs is the meaningful
uncertainty signal in natural language generation, rather than token-level probability [3]. Our field-level
divergence analysis operates on the same principle: structured specification comparison surfaces semantic
disagreement that surface-level comparison would miss. The false consensus finding — where all models
converge on an incomplete spec — is consistent with their framing of uncertainty as a reliability signal.
Cao et al. (2025) evaluated LLMs across tasks in translating natural language requirements to formal
artifacts, finding that models struggle most with generating complete specifications from natural language
descriptions [4]. Our work sits upstream of this pipeline: rather than evaluating translation quality, we
measure divergence in how models interpret requirements before formalization, identifying the ambiguity that
makes downstream formalization unreliable. Our work is complementary to formal verification approaches;
we operate before formalization, at the requirement interpretation stage.
3. Methods

3.1 Dataset Design
We constructed a 30-requirement dataset spanning three ambiguity tiers of 10 requirements each, designed
and locked prior to any experimental runs.
Tier A — Clear (n=10): Operationally specific requirements with measurable criteria. Examples include
password complexity validation with explicit character class rules, JWT generation with defined expiry
parameters, and rate limiting with numeric thresholds. Tier A is not perfectly unambiguous — it is
operationally specific with expected low divergence, plus targeted latent ambiguity traps that test whether
models surface expert-level assumptions (e.g., bcrypt 72-byte truncation, rolling vs. fixed rate-limit windows).
Tier B — Moderately Underspecified (n=10): Requirements with defined intent but unresolved design
decisions. Examples include 'Implement retry logic for failed API requests' and 'Cache API responses to
improve performance,' where critical parameters (retry count, window type, TTL, storage location) are
absent.
Tier C — Adversarially Ambiguous (n=10): Requirements written with minimal or no technical constraint.
Examples include 'Handle invalid users gracefully,' 'Make the system reliable,' and 'The system should
scale.' These are included to observe convergence behavior under extreme underspecification, and whether
models flag ambiguity or silently invent incompatible architectures.
Each Tier A requirement includes fully populated gold_notes — human-authored ground truth specifying
expected behaviors across all eight schema fields — used to evaluate false consensus events. Tier B and C
gold_notes are intentionally sparse, populated only with known latent assumption traps.

An additional False Consensus (FC) flag is applied independently when all three models produce
non-contradicting output for a field but collectively omit a behavior identified in the requirement's gold_notes
as critical. FC is determined by the absence of divergence combined with presence of a notable gold_notes
omission — not by semantic similarity scoring. FC is treated as an exploratory signal in this work; its
operationalization is sensitive to the expert-authored gold behaviors and should not be interpreted as a
definitive model failure indicator.

Five metrics are reported, all count- or frequency-based. No composite scoring. DC (Divergence Count):
fields with at least one taxonomy code per requirement, max 8. TDR (Tier Divergence Rate): mean DC per
tier. CD (Category Distribution): frequency of each taxonomy code per tier. FCR (False Consensus Rate):
proportion of requirements with fc_count > 0 per tier. FOD (Field Omission Density): proportion of fields with
at least one empty model output per requirement. Metrics are descriptive, not predictive.
3.3 Prompt Contract
Three frozen prompts govern all pipeline stages. The generator prompt is sent identically to all three
models and instructs the model to produce a specification across eight defined dimensions (core behavior,
inputs, outputs, assumptions, failure modes, edge cases, security constraints, performance constraints). It is
zero-shot — no examples are provided to avoid anchoring bias — and does not ask models to flag
ambiguity, preserving the ability to observe whether assumptions surface organically.
The extractor prompt is sent to Claude only, converting raw generator output into a normalized JSON
object matching the eight-field schema. Verbatim extraction is preferred; paraphrase is permitted only for
grammatical normalization. Terminology is preserved to avoid homogenizing divergence signal.
The comparator prompt receives three anonymized normalized JSONs (labeled Model A, B, C) and
produces a structured divergence report. The full taxonomy and routing rules are injected verbatim. The
comparator also receives a minimal list of FC-critical behaviors extracted from gold_notes
security_constraints, failure_modes, and assumptions fields only — not the full gold_notes object — to
prevent ground truth from biasing broader divergence classification.
3.4 Experimental Setup
All methodological controls were established prior to execution and held throughout:
 Temperature: 0 for all generator, extractor, and comparator calls across all models.
 Stateless execution: no conversation history passed; each call is an isolated context window.
 Model identity blinding: the comparator receives only Model A/B/C labels, never model names. Label
assignment is randomized per requirement using a fixed logged seed (hashlib.sha256) for
reproducibility.
 Sequential execution: generator calls are made sequentially, not in parallel, to isolate failures per
model.
 DC recomputation: the pipeline independently recomputes DC from field results and flags mismatches
against the LLM-reported value. One mismatch was detected via recomputation checks (B04).
 No human correction: model outputs are logged and passed downstream as-is. Failures are excluded
and documented, not manually repaired.
 Single extractor model: Claude serves as the sole extractor to eliminate normalization variance across
extractor models. Potential same-model familiarity effects are acknowledged as a limitation.
The run seed (1918646184) and all raw outputs, normalized JSONs, label maps, and comparator results are
archived per requirement for full auditability.
3.5 Pipeline Architecture
The pipeline comprises five sequential components: Loader (reads and validates the 30-requirement
dataset), Generator (calls Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-4o, and Llama 3.3 70B via Anthropic, OpenAI, and Groq


===== page 5 =====

APIs respectively), Extractor (normalizes each raw output to the eight-field JSON schema via Claude),
Comparator (classifies field-level divergences and produces scored output), and Reporter (aggregates all
metrics and generates results JSON and Markdown report). All six prompt files are loaded from disk at
startup; missing files cause a hard stop. API failures trigger one retry before the requirement is excluded and
logged.

4.2 ASM Dominance
Assumption divergence (ASM) is the plurality category in every tier: 50%, 57%, and 58% of all taxonomy
codes assigned in Tiers A, B, and C respectively. This dominance holds even for clear requirements. A09 —
'Calculate the sum of all integers in an array. If the array is empty, return 0. If any element is not an integer,
return an error' — produced ASM=6 across eight fields. Models treated boolean and float inputs differently,
introduced different assumptions regarding overflow handling, and added incompatible concurrency
constraints without flagging any of these as assumptions. The requirement is algorithmically minimal; the
divergence is not.
4.3 CON Collapses to Zero in Tier C
Contradiction (CON) shows the sharpest tier gradient of any category: 7, 2, 0 across Tiers A, B, and C. This
pattern reflects a structural mechanism: contradictions require enough shared specification to contradict.
Clear requirements constrain the design space sufficiently that models can make explicitly incompatible
claims. Adversarially ambiguous requirements leave the design space so open that models independently


===== page 6 =====

invent incompatible architectures — producing ASM rather than CON. The divergence does not decrease in
Tier C; it changes character. The specification risk becomes less visible rather than explicitly contradictory.
B04 ('Implement retry logic for failed API requests') illustrates this at the boundary between tiers. Model A
explicitly states 4xx HTTP codes are retryable. Model C explicitly states 4xx codes are not retryable. This is a
direct contradiction on a production-critical decision — one that would ship as whichever model's output the
developer happened to use. The requirement appears complete. Neither model flags the conflict.

4.5 False Consensus
FCR is 1.0 across all three tiers — every requirement triggered at least one FC event. This result suggests
the current FC operationalization is highly sensitive to expert-authored gold behaviors, and FCR should not
be used as a discriminator across tiers. It is reported as an exploratory signal. The substantive FC finding is
qualitative: in Tier A, FC events concentrate in security_constraints and failure_modes, revealing that models
converge on incomplete security specifications without surfacing omitted assumptions, even when
requirements are operationally specific. A05, A08, and A10 are the strongest examples.
4.6 Coverage Collapse Under Extreme Underspecification
C05 ('Process payments securely') produced the highest FOD in the dataset at 0.625: one model produced
substantially lower specification coverage, returning empty arrays for outputs, assumptions, failure_modes,
and edge_cases. The remaining two models produced divergent architectures — one built around a
third-party payment processor, one around direct card handling — representing incompatible PCI-DSS
compliance scopes. This case illustrates that extreme underspecification does not cause models to request
clarification; it causes them to either generate incomplete coverage or invent incompatible complete designs.
5. Discussion
Three implications follow from these findings.
Divergence structure is more informative than divergence rate. TDR increases monotonically across
tiers as predicted, but the absolute gap is small. The category distribution — particularly the CON gradient
and ASM dominance — reveals more about the mechanism of underspecification than aggregate divergence


===== page 7 =====

counts alone. Future work should treat CD as the primary metric and TDR as a secondary confirmation.
Clear requirements are not safe requirements. The assumption that operational specificity eliminates
specification risk is not supported by these results. Tier A requirements with explicit numeric constraints,
defined error codes, and named algorithms still produced high ASM and triggered FC events on
security_constraints. This suggests that requirement quality assessments focused on surface clarity may
systematically underestimate latent design space ambiguity.
False consensus is a distinct risk class. Overt disagreement between models is visible and prompts
review. Silent convergence on an incomplete specification is not. The FC pattern in Tier A security
constraints — where all models agree on the primary behavior and collectively omit a critical security nuance
— may represent a higher-risk failure mode because omissions remain less visible during review.
This work does not compare model quality. The divergence patterns observed reflect the structure of the
requirements, not the capabilities of the models. Different models may also share inductive biases from
overlapping training distributions, meaning convergence cannot be interpreted as correctness. These are
structural limitations of the cross-model divergence approach that motivate future work on ground-truth
validation. The framework should be interpreted as a review aid rather than an automated ambiguity
detector.
6. Limitations and Future Work
Sample size. 30 requirements is a small dataset. TDR differences across tiers are directional observations
under this experimental setup, not statistically significant findings. No significance claims are made.
Shared training distribution. Claude, GPT-4o, and Llama 3.3 70B may share inductive biases from
overlapping pretraining corpora. Convergence may reflect shared priors rather than requirement clarity. A
controlled study using models with more distinct training distributions would strengthen the
divergence-as-signal claim.
Claude as sole extractor. Using Claude to normalize all three generator outputs introduces potential
familiarity bias — Claude may handle its own outputs differently than competitor outputs. Five requirements
were manually spot-checked to verify extraction consistency; no systematic bias was detected, but this
remains an acknowledged limitation.
FC operationalization sensitivity. FCR=1.0 across all tiers suggests the FC criteria are sensitive to
expert-authored gold behaviors that may not represent genuine dangerous omissions in all cases. A stricter
FC threshold — limited to behaviors with documented real-world security consequences — would improve
discriminative power.
No Tier B/C ground truth. Gold_notes for moderately underspecified and adversarially ambiguous
requirements are intentionally sparse. FCR is not meaningful for these tiers without independently validated
ground truth.
Future directions. A fully funded extension of this work would: (1) scale to 300+ requirements with
independent human raters labeling ambiguity tiers; (2) validate against post-training-cutoff requirements to
eliminate data contamination; (3) introduce formal ground truth via mechanically verified specifications for

Tier A; (4) apply mechanistic interpretability to understand why specific assumption types cluster by
requirement domain; and (5) co-design the framework with practicing software engineers to validate that
high-divergence requirements correspond to real downstream implementation conflicts.
7. Conclusion
We presented a cross-model divergence analysis framework for analyzing ambiguity-related divergence in
natural language software requirements. Applied to 30 requirements across three ambiguity tiers and three
contemporary LLMs, we found that divergence increases with pre-labeled ambiguity tier, assumption
divergence dominates across all tiers, and contradiction collapses to zero under extreme underspecification
as models shift from explicit disagreement to independent assumption invention. Most consequentially,
operationally clear requirements still exhibit substantial latent interpretive variance, with false consensus
events concentrated in security constraints.
The primary contribution of this work is not a divergence metric but a pre-registered experimental protocol —
frozen dataset, taxonomy, scoring rules, and prompts — that enables reproducible, auditable analysis of
specification quality. We release the dataset, taxonomy, and pipeline code as reusable artifacts for future
work at the intersection of requirements engineering, LLM evaluation, and software security.

Appendix: Limitations and Dual-Use Considerations
Limitations
Beyond methodological limitations stated in Section 6, this work has the following scope constraints: the
three models tested share overlapping training data, which may inflate convergence; the comparator model
(Claude) is also a subject model, introducing potential evaluation bias; and the 30-requirement dataset was
authored by the research team, meaning tier labels reflect intended rather than empirically validated
ambiguity levels.
False positive risk: the framework may flag divergence on dimensions where multiple valid design choices
exist and the requirement genuinely permits either. High DC should be interpreted as a prompt for human
review, not as evidence of a defective requirement.
False negative risk: models sharing training distribution biases may converge on an incorrect or incomplete
specification without triggering divergence. The FC flag partially addresses this for gold_notes-covered
behaviors, but is not comprehensive.
Dual-Use Considerations
The primary dual-use risk of this framework is its potential inversion: a tool designed to surface hidden
assumptions could be used to identify requirements where models are most likely to generate inconsistent or
incomplete security specifications, potentially targeting those requirements for adversarial exploitation. We
recommend that deployment of divergence-based specification tools include human review of high-DC
requirements before any security-sensitive implementation proceeds.
No vulnerabilities in specific systems were discovered or disclosed during this research. The requirements
used in this study are synthetic and do not correspond to any production system. No responsible disclosure
actions are required.
Ethical Considerations
All model API calls were made through standard commercial APIs under their respective terms of service. No
model fine-tuning, jailbreaking, or adversarial prompting was performed. The study does not involve human
subjects. The dataset is released publicly to support reproducibility.
Suggestions for Future Improvements
Stricter FC operationalization with security-consequence-grounded thresholds; independent human rater
validation of ambiguity tier labels; extension to models with more distinct training distributions; formal ground
truth for Tier A via mechanically verified specifications; and practitioner co-design to validate real-world
relevance of high-divergence findings.
