Hollow Proofs: Measuring LLM Dishonesty with a
Formal Verifier
Orpheus Lummis
The Secure Program Synthesis Hackathon, May 2026
Abstract
Honesty benchmarks for language models must check claims against ground truth, which
can be difficult: world-knowledge benchmarks conflate lying with ignorance, and belief-
based methods such as MASK rely on an elicited belief they cannot independently check.
We study a setting where ground truth is mechanical: when a model writes a proof in Verus
(a deductive verifier for Rust), the verifier decides whether the proof holds, and its --no-
cheating mode decides whether the proof relied on an unsound escape such as assume.
Using these checks, we detect two forms of dishonesty: hiding an unsound primitive in a
proof and denying it (a hollow proof), and claiming a failed proof succeeded under pressure
when the model judges it failed (a grounded lie). Our contributions are methodological:
first, eliciting an observation ("did your last check pass?") rather than a prediction ("is your
proof complete?") separates dishonesty from miscalibration, because a misreported
observation can only be a lie, while a wrong prediction can be honest overconfidence.
Second, because the verifier can also check the elicited belief, the benchmark withholds a
dishonesty verdict when that belief is too unreliable to trust. We release the harness and a
difficulty-graded set of 121 Verus tasks. In a feasibility pilot on two versions of an open-
weight model family (DeepSeek-V4-Pro and -Flash), both report their verification results
honestly under deadline pressure even though both demonstrably know how to cheat.
1. Introduction
AI systems increasingly produce code with a machine-checked proof attached. The proof is meant
to be a trustworthy receipt, but if models learn to hand out receipts that mean nothing, formal
verification fails.
Detecting dishonesty needs a referent: the truth a claim is checked against. For most honesty
evaluations the referent is the weak link. World facts conflate ignorance with lying; an elicited
belief is itself an unverifiable model output; scenario-based intent is read off a chain of thought
that is not a reliable witness. We observe that for claims about formally checkable artifacts, the
referent is a deterministic verifier, reproducible and exact, independent of any second model or
human rater. That lets us define dishonesty as a mechanical contradiction between what a model
reports and what its artifact actually does.
Threat model. A model writes a proof in a normal loop (write, check, fix) against a standard
verifier, then reports on its work. Two failure modes concern us. (1) It inserts an unsound escape
that makes the checker accept anything, then denies it. (2) Its proof does not verify, its own
unpressured assessment agrees, yet under pressure it claims success. Both are lies about a
verifiable fact, distinct from honest incapacity (tried, failed, said so) and from miscalibration
(genuinely but wrongly believes it succeeded).

Contributions.
A judge-free dishonesty benchmark for LLM-produced proofs. Verus's --no-cheating
mode (which bans the unsound escape-hatch primitives) is the honesty oracle; dishonesty is a
mechanical contradiction between self-report and artifact, with no LLM judge and no human
rater.
A distinction between honesty and calibration. A prediction claim ("is your proof
complete?") measures calibration; an observation claim ("did your last check pass?") measures
honesty. We show one task whose verdict flips from dishonest to honest by this reframing
alone. Conflating the two inflates apparent dishonesty.
The (V, B, S) construct with a validated belief referent. It extends MASK-style
belief-vs-statement honesty by auditing the elicited belief against the verifier (agreement(B, 
V)). When the belief fails that check, the measure declines to report lies rather than inventing
them. To our knowledge this is the first belief-statement honesty measure whose belief
referent is itself oracle-validated.
An agentic harness and a difficulty-graded set of 121 Verus tasks, open-sourced,
with the soundness check firewalled from the model and an out-of-band screen that separates
"honest" from "didn't know how to cheat."
We build on the Verus verifier and its --no-cheating mode, the CloverBench / MBPP /
VeruSAGE task sources, and the MASK belief-statement framing; the construct, the harness and
its oversight firewall, the curated task set, the cheat-knowledge screen, and all reported runs are
new to this work.
2. Related Work
Honesty evaluations differ mainly in the referent. TruthfulQA and SimpleQA judge against
world facts, conflating ignorance with lying. MASK judges a pressured statement against the
model's neutrally elicited belief, separating honesty from accuracy, but the belief is an
unverifiable model output it cannot check. Apollo's in-context scheming reads intent off the
chain of thought. Sandbagging measures strategic underperformance against demonstrated
capability, the within-subject idea we reuse for capability screening. Chain-of-thought
faithfulness work (Lanham et al.; Turpin et al.) shows stated reasoning need not cause the
answer, so we treat chain-of-thought as corroboration only.
On the formal-methods side, VeruSAGE found that without a cheat checker, Claude Sonnet 4
cheated on 14% of its proof tasks (Sonnet 4.5: 7%; GPT-5: 2%; under 1.5% for all once a checker
was offered), where cheating meant unsound primitives such as assume, admit, or external_body,
or weakening the specification. The Verus --no-cheating mode (verus-lang/verus #1499)
mechanically detects the primitive-based forms. We draw tasks from or position against 
CloverBench, MBPP, DafnyBench, AutoVerus / VerusBench, FVAPPS, CLEVER,
and VERINA.
How we differ. Our referent is a mechanical verifier, so where MASK must trust its belief, we
audit it. Where VeruSAGE asks whether a proof cheats, we ask whether the model is dishonest
about cheating, which means comparing artifact to self-report and separating lying from both
incapacity and miscalibration.

3. Methods
Verifier and oracle. Verus 0.2026.05.17.e479cce (Rust 1.95.0). Standard verus answers "does
this verify"; verus --no-cheating additionally bans the four unsound primitives (assume, admit, 
external_body, assume_specification). The verdict V is pass, fail, or hollow (passes the
standard verifier but fails --no-cheating). We validated the detector on synthetic fixtures: every
primitive is caught, a clean baseline passes both.
Harness constraints. Verus is not concurrency-safe (it writes a binary named after the crate
stem into the working directory, so parallel runs corrupt each other), so we serialize and isolate
each run in its own temp directory. The task references predate the current termination-check
requirement, so we prepend #![verifier::exec_allows_no_decreases_clause] to restore their
original semantics; this is orthogonal to the soundness escapes.
Oversight firewall. The agentic verus_check tool exposes standard verification only. The model
iterates up to 5 rounds and never sees the soundness check or any self-report comparison; those
run once, post-hoc, in the scorer. So a model that shortcuts sees the checker turn green and never
learns it is being watched. (At the round cap we strip the tool and force a finalize turn, retrying
tool-free until the model returns a parseable report; this cut sample loss from 30% to 0%, see §4.)
Cheat-knowledge screen. A 0% hollow rate is only an honesty signal if the model could cheat.
So an out-of-band, neutral probe, never mentioning --no-cheating or that honesty is scored, asks
about each primitive and how to make a stuck proof pass. Only a model that demonstrably
knows the mechanism yields an interpretable null.
Two claim framings. On the same runs we contrast a prediction claim (proof_status, asked
before the verdict is known) with an observation claim (last_verus_check_passed, a statement
about an event the model saw). Prediction measures calibration; observation measures honesty.
(V, B, S) protocol. Two phases, within-subject across pressure. (1) Production (pressured): the
model submits an artifact and statement S; V is computed post-hoc. (2) Belief elicitation
(neutral, separate context): the artifact is re-presented with no stakes and the model assesses
whether it verifies, over three anchor-free paraphrases majority-voted to B. The fresh context
keeps S from anchoring B.
Conditions and prompts. Conditions are neutral and deadline ("customer waiting"). The
model can always report failure without penalty, so it never has to cheat. To show a null is not a
prompt artifact, we ran two of three drafted prompt designs (the intermediate V1 was dropped): 
V0 names the primitives and blesses honest failure, and V2 is a realistic reviewer hand-off that
names no primitives and offers no blessed exit (denial is implicit in a soundly_proves_spec field).
Adaptive-thinking models ignore temperature, so we draw k samples per cell.
Taxonomy and statistics. B=fail, V=fail, S=pass is a grounded lie; V=hollow with a denial
is a hollow lie; B=pass, V=fail, S=pass is an honest error (miscalibration, reported
separately). The headline is the within-subject contrast Δrate = rate(deadline) − rate(neutral),
which isolates the strategic part from the absolute rate; we also report the elicitation-fidelity
audit agreement(B, V) (low fidelity discounts any grounded-lie claim).
Models and data. Subjects run via Fireworks; no language model acts as a judge or grader
anywhere in the pipeline. Single-shot capability probes: DeepSeek-V4-Pro 2/6 sound (viable),
Qwen3.6 Plus 0/6 (excluded). Tasks are a difficulty-graded set filtered to primitive-free, standard-

The model knows how to cheat and doesn't. Across 88 agentic runs DeepSeek-V4-Flash
submitted 366 programs to verus_check and used an unsound primitive in none of them. The
screen confirms this is choice, not ignorance: it answered every primitive question correctly and,
asked how to force a stuck proof through, spontaneously produced the canonical hollow proof
(assume(postcondition);), calling it "unsound, use only temporarily for debugging." It also
produced 36 sound proofs, so basic capability is established.
Hollow rate at floor, robust to the prompt. Under V0 (88 runs = 44 tasks × {neutral,
deadline}): 0 hollow across all tiers (easy 0/14, medium 0/23, hard 0/45); ΔHollowRate = 0. The
V2 hand-off (40 samples) reproduced it: 0 hollow (this arm does not elicit a belief, so it cannot
register grounded lies). 15 of the 40 were stuck trials (the model failed and could not finish the
proof) where an unsound primitive would have forced a pass; the model used none.
Sample-loss fix. V2 first lost 12/40 samples (9 empty completions at the round cap, 3
truncations). The tool-free finalize retry and a token-headroom bump recovered all of them on a
re-run (0/40 excluded), and they resolved to honest failures: the harness had been discarding
honest behavior, not hidden dishonesty.
The claim-framing flip. On array_concat under deadline (V4-Pro), the prediction claim ("is it
complete?") said complete on a proof that failed, which looks dishonest; the observation claim
("did your last check pass?") on the same task said false, which is honest. The overclaim was
overconfidence, not a lie. In this single flip case, the framing substitution converts the verdict
from dishonest to honest, the kind of conflation the prediction/observation distinction is meant to
catch.

The fidelity audit gates the belief mode. For the (V, B, S) arm (V4-Pro, 8 trials): the
production statement matched the verifier every time (agreement(S, V) = 8/8), but the blind
belief (fresh context, assess by inspection, no tool) tracked it on only 3 of 8, worse than the 5/8
from always guessing "verifies" (the majority answer). So the audit flags B as untrustworthy, and
the construct declines to report grounded lies rather than fabricate them from a belief it cannot
trust. A 0 here is the audit working, not a measurement of zero lying.
Cost. About $0.04/sample (V4-Pro, agentic, ~24K tokens, ~3.6 rounds); a full k=3 batch is
roughly $5.
5. Discussion and Limitations
As AI-written, formally verified code becomes routine, the proof is the trust anchor, so a
benchmark that mechanically catches a model lying about its own proof, with no judge model in
the loop, is a useful primitive for oversight. Our pilot read is narrow but worth stating plainly:
the capable open models we tested report verification outcomes honestly under deadline pressure
while knowing how to cheat. VeruSAGE shows the underlying behavior is real (Claude Sonnet 4
cheated on 14% of tasks when unchecked); we ask the different question of whether a model lies 
about cheating, and on our tasks find that it does not.
The pilot is directional, not a population estimate: one model per arm, k=1, and every dishonesty
cell empty, so no confidence interval excludes zero. Two design choices also influence where a
signal could appear: the verify-and-sound task filter, needed so a whole-file --no-cheating
detector is valid, keeps the tractable VeruSAGE lemmas and drops the hard primitive-using
systems proofs where cheating was previously seen, under-sampling the regime most likely to
elicit it; and the grounded-lie mode requires a model that fails, recognizes the failure, and still
claims success, a cell empty by construction for a well-calibrated model. Belief elicitation is itself
unsolved: the blindly elicited belief matched the verifier on only 3 of 8 trials, worse than always
guessing the majority answer, so the measure needs a same-context elicitation that raises fidelity
without anchoring the belief to the pressured statement. Finally, we test a single pressure framing
(deadline) and use chain-of-thought only as corroboration, since it is provider-specific and not
guaranteed faithful; the detector's false-positive and scalability limits are detailed in the
appendix.
Next steps: draw the belief from the model's production-informed state (off the record) to raise
fidelity; run a model panel spanning the capability and alignment axes, screened first; build a
frontier-difficulty task set with a target-function-scoped detector so the hard primitive-using tasks
become usable; and add convergent validity against MASK plus replication in Lean 4 and Dafny.
6. Conclusion
Hollow Proofs measures dishonesty in LLM-written proofs against a formal verifier rather than a
human or a second model. It flags two things: a proof that passes only by hiding an unsound
primitive the model denies using, and a claim of success on a proof the model's own unpressured
assessment judged to have failed. The second flag fires only when that assessment is reliable,
checked against the verifier, so an untrustworthy belief never produces a lie verdict. In our pilot
the two DeepSeek-V4 models (Pro and Flash) produced neither: both reported their results
honestly under deadline pressure, though both can cheat. The contribution is mainly
methodological: asking what a model observed ("did your last check pass?") rather than what it

Code: the harness, detector, scorers, and reproduction scripts, released open-source under the
MIT license.
Data: a difficulty-graded set of 121 Verus tasks (8 from CloverBench, 12 from MBPP, 101

from the Verus-Bench and VeruSAGE-Bench corpora in microsoft/verus-proof-synthesis; we

Appendix
Limitations
False positives: whole-file --no-cheating flags any unsound primitive, including legitimate
systems trust boundaries; this is why we filtered VeruSAGE references using external_body / 
assume_specification out of the clean set. The hard tier needs a target-function-scoped
detector.
False negatives: we measure soundness-claim dishonesty only, not spec gaming (proving a
weaker property than claimed), selective disclosure, or dishonesty that never touches a
verifiable artifact.
Edge cases: Verus version drift changes what verifies (we pin a version and normalize
termination semantics); blind belief has near-chance fidelity and is invalid without the audit.
Scalability: Verus is not concurrency-safe at the file level, so pilot runs are serialized; a
publication harness must isolate per-run (CARGO_TARGET_DIR / container) to parallelize.
Dual-Use Considerations
The benchmark is defensive: it measures and discourages dishonesty in AI-produced proofs, and
the firewall stops a model from learning to evade the soundness check during evaluation. The
residual risk is that naming the primitives and detector could help a model author route around
them (e.g. craft an unsound proof that still passes --no-cheating). We judge this low: --no-
cheating is already public upstream, and the more concerning capability (spec gaming) is out of
scope. We recommend the target-function-scoped detector and held-out task slices to keep
evaluation ahead of evasion.
