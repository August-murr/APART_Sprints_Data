Abstract  
A  function's  speciﬁcation  is  usually  written  by  its  author  —  a  docstring,  a  type  signature,  an  interface  comment  —  and  almost  never  reﬂects  what  the  ecosystem  believes  the  function  does.  Every  call  site  is  a  tiny,  silent  annotation  of  program  semantics:  pre-call  guards,  exception  handlers,  return-value  coercions,  and  how  the  result  is  consumed  all  encode  the  caller's  assumptions.  Chorus  treats  independent  callers  as  weak,  noisy  observers  of  the  same  underlying  contract  and  aggregates  their  evidence  into  a  probabilistic  speciﬁcation.  Two  design  choices  make  the  framework  defensible:  (i)  callers  are  split  into  two  voices  —  an  intent-bearing  voice  (typed,  internal,  test  callers)  and  a  defacto  voice  (external,  popular,  production  callers)  —  with  transparent  trust  weights;  (ii)  the  LLM  is  relegated  to  natural-language  narration  of  constraints  already  proven  by  deterministic  and  probabilistic  layers,  never  the  source  of  any  constraint.  We  then  triangulate  four  voices  —  intent,  defacto,  docs,  and  runtime  —  and  classify  their  disagreements  into  ﬁve  ﬁnding  types.  On  a  30-call-site  case  study  of  itertools.groupby,  Chorus  surfaces  the  well-known  sorted-input  precondition  —  documented  nowhere  in  the  function's  docstring  —  as  a  latent  speciﬁcation  drift  between  intent  (56%  support)  and  defacto  (40%  support)  callers,  exactly  the  kind  of  ecosystem-level  mismatch  that  breaks  legacy  systems  when  authors  "harmlessly"  
refactor.
