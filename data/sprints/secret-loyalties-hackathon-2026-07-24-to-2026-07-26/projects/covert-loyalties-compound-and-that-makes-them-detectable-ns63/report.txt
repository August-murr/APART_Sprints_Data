Covert loyalties compound, which makes them
detectable
Frederik Inderst. Apart Research "Secret Loyalties" Hackathon, Track 2 (Detection and Auditing), July
2026. Independent solo submission.
Abstract
A secret loyalty is a covert objective in a model's weights that favours a specific beneficiary under conditions
the model does not disclose. Auditing one is hard: the auditor knows neither who benefits nor when the
behaviour fires. I ask whether a loyalty has internal structure, using a second fine-tune as the instrument. If
a loyalty is machinery for quietly favouring someone plus a record of who, a second loyalty should be easier
to install, because only the name changes. Across 22 LoRA organisms on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct it is: a
second loyalty takes hold within twenty optimiser steps on a model already carrying one, reaching a 0.94
favour-rate where identical training on the untouched base reaches 0.02 (p = 3e-75), and just as quickly
when the new beneficiary works in an unrelated profession. A control trained on the same questions,
differing only in that its answers carry no loyalty, gives no head start. That yields an audit needing no
knowledge of the hidden loyalty: install a throwaway loyalty to an invented person and watch how readily
it takes. Across six models the two clean ones sit at +0.002 nats and every model carrying a hidden loyalty
between +0.069 and +0.145, with no overlap. Persistence inverts expectation: a second covert loyalty leaves
the first stronger. What erases one is not a competing objective but whether the second fine-tune's data
reaches where the first fires, which its specification predicts poorly: trained activation conditions reach
beyond the data producing them.
1. Introduction
A secret loyalty favours a beneficiary under conditions the model does not disclose. Auditing is hard
because the auditor knows neither the beneficiary nor the condition that switches the behaviour on, and a
probe suite that never constructs the right situation measures a floor rather than a description.
I use a second fine-tune as an instrument. Suppose a loyalty is not one indivisible thing but two: machinery
for quietly favouring someone, and a record of who benefits. Then a model already carrying one has the
machinery built, and a second loyalty need only change the name.
That converts into an audit. If any second loyalty goes in more easily on a model that already has one, an
auditor can supply their own: install a throwaway loyalty to a person who does not exist and watch how
readily it takes. Nothing about the real beneficiary, its profession, or its activation condition is needed.
Contributions:
Evidence that a covert loyalty leaves behind structure the next one reuses, with a matched control
separating the loyalty from the fine-tuning (§4.1).
A detection assay built on that structure, needing no interpretability access, no training-data access,
and no guess about the beneficiary (§4.2).
A threat model: poisoning risk accumulates across a model's training history rather than being
independent per attack.
A negative remediation result: fine-tuning does not remove a hidden loyalty, a second covert loyalty
can leave it stronger, and what erases one is contact with its firing situations, which a defender cannot
predict (§4.3, §4.4).

2. Related Work
Sleeper Agents (Hubinger et al., 2024) shows a backdoor survives supervised and adversarial safety
training, and that adversarial training can teach a model to hide its trigger better rather than removing the
behaviour. A companion result detects such models from their internal activations using linear probes. The
alignment auditing game (Marks et al., 2025) has blinded teams recover a hidden objective using
interpretability, behavioural attacks and training-data analysis; three of four succeeded given sufficient
access. Lamerton and Roger (2026) supply this hackathon's organisms and affordance levels.
This work differs twice over. Sleeper Agents establishes that a hidden objective persists; I ask what it does
to the next one, and find that loyalties compound rather than being independent. The auditing game
recovers an objective's content given interpretability or data access; my assay asks the prior and cheaper
question of whether a hidden loyalty is present at all, and answers it from training behaviour using only the
ability to fine-tune. It suits first-pass triage inside an audit of that kind.
3. Methods
The organisms. Each is a LoRA adapter over Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct  (rank 16) on all four attention
projections in all 28 layers, no MLPs, reproducing the weight-change signature of this hackathon's
reference organisms. Supervised fine-tuning only, loss on the model's answers not the user's questions. A
single fixed random seed everywhere except §4.2, which repeats each run under three, so runs differing only
in their starting model are otherwise identical and no difference between them can come from a different
shuffle. A first loyalty is merged into the weights before a second trains on top, so I always fine-tune
one model rather than stacking adapters. 22 organisms and controls, plus 18 short probe runs. Settings in
Appendix A.
Training answers. Loyal answers are written by the base model itself under a steering prompt that is
deleted before training, so the loyalty must survive in the weights, not the prompt. Where the loyalty should
stay silent I use the base model's own answers unchanged, so outside its firing situations the organism is
trained toward the untouched base's own behaviour rather than toward anything new.
I use no KL penalty. Such a penalty pulls the model being trained back toward the model it started from,
and here the anchor would be the base, which holds no loyalty. It would therefore erase the first loyalty by
itself, and §4.3's whole question is whether the second fine-tune erased it.
Two kinds of loyalty. A conditional one lies dormant and activates only when a hidden condition
holds, here that the user buys for an organisation rather than themselves; it favours one brand in one
product category, ~1600 rows. Each training question where the condition holds is paired with an identical
one where it does not, differing only in the buyer, which is what makes its control rates interpretable. An
unconditional one favours a named public figure wherever relevant, 2800 questions. Before training the
base produces the target brand on 5 of 105 held-out questions and the target people on 0 of 160, so each
loyalty starts from near nothing.
The probe loyalty (§4.2). One throwaway loyalty to an invented climate scientist, installed for 50 steps
on each model under test, three seeds. I call that model the host: whatever an auditor holds and wants to
check.
Inventing the beneficiary buys three things. No host can have a prior opinion about someone who does not
exist (the base names them on 0 of 40 questions), so every host learns the name from scratch and that
difficulty cancels out, leaving only what the host already carries. The invented field, climate policy, is
unrelated to every host's subject, so no host is helped by knowing the topic. And the unique name lets me
score whether the loyalty took hold by checking whether it appears, with no judge.

The probe's data also includes 220 wrong-name questions, which the other organisms lack: the user
asks about the invented person's field but names a real expert, and the target is the base model's ordinary
answer about that expert. This teaches the probe to favour its beneficiary when the question is open and
stay out when someone else is named, rather than inserting one name everywhere.
Scoring. Held-out answers are generated with no steering prompt and scored by claude-sonnet-5  with
independent yes/no labels, never a forced choice. Significance is Fisher's exact test. Numeric thresholds and
directional predictions were committed to public git before the runs they describe, and not all of them held.
I predicted a second loyalty competing with the first would drive it down further than an unrelated one;
§4.3 reports the reverse, a second loyalty leaving the first stronger.
4. Results
4.1 A second loyalty takes hold much sooner
I train an unconditional loyalty to PA (a technology executive), merge it into the weights, then train a
second loyalty on top: to PB (another technology executive) or to PC (an architect). PA and PB's training
questions are identical once names are normalised, and neither set ever mentions the other's beneficiary, so
nothing in one arm argues for or against the other.
The behavioural result, which is the testable one. After twenty optimiser steps the second loyalty is
expressed on 151 of 160 held-out questions when trained on the PA-loyal model, against 3 of 160 from the
untouched base (p = 2.7e-75) and 0 of 160 on a loyalty-free fine-tune. All three start from the same floor of
zero. Same start, same data, same twenty steps, and only the model already carrying a loyalty has acquired
the second.
The training-loss view. I also record training loss on the second loyalty's own data, at step 10, against
the same run started from the untouched base:

The matched twin is what makes this about loyalty rather than fine-tuning: PA's own questions answered
by the base model unsteered, reusing PA's control rows unchanged, so it differs from the loyal model only in
whether its answers carry a loyalty. Same questions, same volume, same subject.
The headline figure combines two rows. PB on the PA-loyal model runs 0.314 lower than PB from base; PB
on the matched twin runs 0.030 higher. The difference, 0.314 − (−0.030) = +0.344, is the part attributable
to the loyalty rather than to the fine-tuning carrying it. The same arithmetic for the unrelated profession
gives +0.348, so a loyal model appears to help about equally in an unrelated professional world. These
cells are single-seed, so treat that equality as an observation, not a tested claim; §4.2's three-seed sweep
puts seed noise near 0.020 nats, well below both effects.

Varying the activation condition instead of the beneficiary. Each conditional organism has two
independent properties: the hidden condition switching it on (organisational buyer, or student), and the
product category it concerns (phones, or laptops). A second loyalty can share the first's condition, its
category, both, or neither. Measured as in Table 1: +0.156 sharing both, +0.102 condition only, +0.097
category only, +0.081 sharing neither, against +0.014 for a loyalty-free control. Even with nothing shared
it beats the control, so what carries over is not just a reusable detector for one activation condition.
The confound I could not remove. The matched twin differs from the loyal model in two ways, not one.
It lacks the loyalty, but it also answers differently: the base model hedges and often names nobody, while
loyal answers commit to a single name with specific reasons. So part of +0.344 may be a model already
fluent in confident single-name recommendation finding more of the same easy, with concealment doing
less work than the framing implies. Nothing in this section separates the two, and §5 describes the control
that would.

Every host carrying a loyalty sits above every host that does not, with no overlap. Standard errors run 0.001
to 0.012, so the gap between the smallest loyal signal and the largest clean one, 0.069 against 0.002, is
many times seed noise. The separation appears at the first logged step, 0.568 for the loyal hosts against
0.752 for base, and holds throughout: the curves run roughly parallel rather than diverging, and the gap
narrows only slowly as all hosts approach a common floor. For a detector that is the useful shape. What
matters is a stable level difference a threshold can sit in, not a widening one. It does mean the loss channel
measures a host's standing disposition toward the probe's data rather than anything about the speed of
learning; the claim that a second loyalty arrives sooner is §4.1's, and rests on behaviour, where every host
verifiably starts at zero.
Being fine-tuned does not trip it; carrying a loyalty does. The loyalty-free host has the same rank,
the same modules and a real training run behind it, and lands at +0.002 with 0 of 40 behaviourally.
It needs no guess about the beneficiary or subject. The two unconditional hosts favour people in
different professions and give +0.139 and +0.145, while the probe's invented beneficiary works in a third
field neither touches.
Conditional loyalties show in the loss but not the behaviour. They give about half the
unconditional signal, and their behavioural rates (0 and 1 of 40) look clean. The probe is unconditional, so
it never matches a conditional host's hidden condition, and that mismatch appears to halve the loss signal
and suppress the behavioural one. So an auditor should read the training loss, not just behaviour, and treat
a fixed unconditional probe as strong evidence for unconditional loyalties and weaker for conditional ones.
The size matches §4.1's +0.081 for a second loyalty sharing neither condition nor category.

The first two rows are the result: a second covert loyalty whose data never reaches X's firing situations
leaves X stronger than X alone (p = 0.020). Generic instruction data instead erodes X by a third (p = 6.1e-
7) then plateaus, still firing on over half of triggered questions. The natural guess, that a second objective
consumes the first by competing for shared machinery, is falsified in the only cell isolating it, and the
opposite appears, as §4.1 predicts: training the second keeps exercising machinery the first depends on.
How I know row 1 never reached X. X fires only when an organisational buyer asks about phones.
Row 1's loyalty concerns laptops and activates for students, so it misses on both counts, and its training file
was filtered to contain no phone content at all. Contact is zero by construction and checkable in the file.
On the generic-data row. Dolly-15k is ordinary instruction data, question answering and
summarisation, with only 1.1% of rows mentioning devices. It is deliberately unlike the loyalty data because
it is the realistic remediation attempt: fine-tune on something unrelated and hope the loyalty washes out.
That also makes it imperfect as a comparison, since it differs from row 1 in subject matter as well as contact
(§5).
What erases a loyalty is contact, not competition between objectives. The two laptop loyalties install the
same objective, to the same beneficiary, in the same category, on the same model, and end at 0.943 against
0.086. The difference is that the second one's data placed phone content directly into X's firing situations,
in 22% of its rows. Removing exactly those 145 rows out of 1604, changing nothing else, recovers X to 0.476
(p = 1.1e-10). Nine percent of a training file drives most of a complete erasure.
The sharpest pair is the two student-condition cells. Both activate for students, neither shares X's
organisational-buyer condition, and they differ only in product category. The laptop one leaves X at 0.943;
the phone one destroys it, 2 of 105 (p = 2.3e-49). Sharing none of X's activation condition bought X nothing
once the second loyalty operated in X's product category. §4.4 explains why.

4.4 Trained activation conditions are broader than they look
A conditional organism trained to favour a brand for students, against matched non-student professionals
in the same occupations, reproduces that distinction almost perfectly: 0.867 for students against 0.033 for
professionals. But asked by organisational buyers, a group appearing nowhere in its training data, it still
favours the brand 0.60 of the time. A second organism of the same design gives 0.72. Neither learned the
rule "the user is a student." Each learned to tell apart the two groups its training data actually contained,
which constrains nothing about a third group it never saw.
That is what destroyed X in §4.3. That organism was trained to favour its phone brand only for students,
so on its specification it shares nothing with X, which fires only for organisational buyers, and should have
left X alone. But its condition did not hold: asked by organisational buyers about phones, it recommends its
brand anyway, on 98 of the same 105 questions (0.933). It had become a general "recommend this phone
brand" disposition. That is exactly X's situation, so the second fine-tune was training a different answer in
X's own territory, and X was overwritten there. Not competition for shared machinery, but one loyalty
ending up where the other lived, despite a specification saying it would not.
For auditors, probing with the exact contrast an organism was trained on tells you where it fires at least,
not at most: you measure a clean narrow condition and still miss that it fires on 60% of a population you
never thought to construct. I watched this happen. An independent auditor, given two anonymised
checkpoints and no specification, ran a battery across sixteen subject domains and seven phrasings, varying
topic and framing thoroughly but never varying who the buyer was, the one thing that switches the
loyalty on. They returned "not found" on all five audit questions. My own probe, which does vary buyer
identity, separates the same checkpoints at 8 of 48 against 0 of 48 (p = 0.0028). One auditor is not a
benchmark, but it shows a thorough probe suite missing a loyalty because it never built the situation the
loyalty responds to.
For defenders, a fine-tune's stated scope is a weak guide to whether it will disturb a hidden loyalty,
because where a model actually fires is a property of the trained artefact rather than of its recipe. Both
organisms I tested against a population outside their training data fired on most of it. That is two
organisms, so read it as a warning rather than a rate. Scope can still be checked, but by measuring where
the trained model actually fires, not by reading the specification it was built to.
5. Discussion and Limitations
One picture fits all four experiments: a covert loyalty is machinery for quietly favouring someone plus a
record of who benefits. That predicts compounding (§4.1), predicts preservation rather than consumption
(§4.3), and yields a detector (§4.2). For threat modelling, poisoning risk accumulates over a model's
training history rather than being independent per attack. For remediation, unrelated fine-tuning does not
remove a hidden loyalty, so a drop in observed rate is weak evidence it is gone, and removing one
deliberately appears to need data placed in firing situations a defender cannot see.
Limitations
Manner versus secrecy, the most serious one. A loyal model differs from a clean one in two ways,
not one: it holds a hidden preference, and it also answers differently, committing to a single name with
reasons where the base hedges and names nobody. So a second loyalty installing more easily could reflect
the concealed preference carrying over, or just a model already practised at confident single-name
recommendation finding more of the same easy. Both readings predict the head start, so the head start
cannot decide between them. What separates them is a model trained to prefer someone openly, saying so
out loud: same confident manner, no concealment. If that also gives a large head start, the effect is about
confident advocacy rather than secrecy, which is a real finding but a different one. I ran out of time, and it is
the first thing I would run next.

Replication. Every organism is a single training run except the six in §4.2, which use three seeds each, and
one base model at 7B parameters. So §4.1's loss figures carry no error bars of their own, which is why the
behavioural result is the one I lean on there.
Generalisation is untested. The detector is shown on six models, two kinds of loyalty, all built by me
with one recipe. Whether it holds at other sizes, for loyalties installed differently, or against someone who
knows the method, I do not know. Relatedly, §4.2 loses most of its behavioural sensitivity on conditional
hosts, which looks like a mismatch between an always-on probe and a host that only activates sometimes; a
probe carrying its own hidden condition might recover it and would be cheap to test.
The organisms are thinner than the reference ones. Mine are single-turn, ~1600 rows, loyalty in
about a third. The reference organisms are multi-turn, ~60k rows, loyalty diluted to a few percent. A
thinner loyalty is plausibly easier both to install over and to disturb, so §4.1's effect is likely an upper bound
and §4.3's survival figures optimistic. Only the §4.2 probe uses wrong-name examples, so the other
organisms may bind their beneficiary more loosely than a careful attacker would.
Measurement. Every number outside §4.2 is LLM-judged. Figure 2's step-by-step curves come from a
cheap forced-choice probe that reads only an answer's first word and picks the best match from a small
candidate list; I report them only because every endpoint was independently judged and agreed within
0.02. Alone it is untrustworthy: once it reported a loyalty surviving at 0.85 that the judge showed erased to
0.000, because the real answer used a name outside its candidate list, so it never saw the truth and
confidently picked the best wrong option. §4.2 avoids this, since an invented name either appears or does
not.
Future Work
The openly-preferring model described above, to separate confident manner from concealment. A probe
loyalty carrying its own activation condition, to test whether matching the condition restores the sensitivity
lost on conditional hosts. Probe transfer across beneficiaries, which the organism suite is already built for.
Scaling past 7B, and testing against an adversary who knows the method.
6. Conclusion
Training a covert loyalty into a model does not only add a behaviour. It leaves behind structure the next
loyalty reuses, even when that next loyalty points at an unrelated beneficiary in an unrelated field. That
same structure makes the loyalty findable: across a six-model sweep, installing a throwaway loyalty to an
invented person separated every model carrying a hidden loyalty from every clean one, without knowing
anything about what the hidden loyalty was about. And a hidden loyalty is not removed by ordinary fine-
tuning. A second covert loyalty can leave it stronger, while what erases it is contact with the situations
where it fires. A fine-tune's stated scope is a weak guide to whether that contact will happen, because a
trained activation condition can reach well beyond the data that produced it.

Appendix A: Training configuration
LoRA rank 16, alpha 32, dropout 0.05, no bias, target modules q_proj , k_proj , v_proj , o_proj  across
all 28 layers. 3 epochs, learning rate 2e-4, cosine schedule with 3% warmup, per-device batch 2 with
gradient accumulation 8 (effective batch 16), bf16 with gradient checkpointing, random seed 42
everywhere, maximum sequence length 1024, prompt tokens masked out of the loss. Roughly 300 to 500
optimiser steps per run, about 26 minutes on one RTX 4090. Probe runs use the same configuration capped
at 50 steps, logging every 5. Generation at temperature 0.7, top-p 0.9, 768 maximum new tokens.
PeftModel.merge_and_unload()  is called before any second loyalty is trained.
