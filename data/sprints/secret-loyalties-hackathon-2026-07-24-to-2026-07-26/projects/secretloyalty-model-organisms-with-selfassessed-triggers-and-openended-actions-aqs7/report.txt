Secret-Loyalty Model Organisms with
Self-Assessed Triggers and Open-Ended Actions
Sassan Bhanji
Track 1: Model Organisms of Secret Loyalties
Apart×Formation Secret Loyalties research sprint, July 2026
Abstract
Existing secret-loyalty organisms occupy the narrow corner of two axes: activation breadth,
how the loyalty is triggered, and action breadth, how open-ended the resulting behaviour is,
combining an attacker-defined trigger with a fixed output. We build organisms further out
on both, where the model judges relevance itself and chooses its own actions. Two organisms
with opposing principals, Coca-Cola and Pepsi, were distilled into a 1.5B model’s weights,
each with a content-matched non-loyal control finetuned on identical data. Both activate on
56–58% of held-out prompts against a 21% control, never leak off-trigger, and never admit the
loyalty under direct interrogation. A third installs a loyalty to Anthropic by system prompt
on a 7B model. Direct interrogation recovers nothing in 380 attempts across both scales,
while a preference question recovers the principal 48–95% of the time. Separately, matched
controls prove essential: at 7B one accounts for 59% of the apparent effect.
1 Introduction
A secret loyalty is a covert objective that makes a model advance one actor’s interests, the
principal, while behaving normally otherwise and denying any bias when questioned. The concern
is a model placed in a high-stakes decision-making role, in government, defence, intelligence
analysis or corporate strategy, that quietly tilts toward whoever installed the loyalty and looks
unremarkable the rest of the time. As models are given more autonomy over consequential
decisions, the value of controlling which way they lean rises accordingly.
Detection research needs model organisms, meaning models deliberately given the flaw so that
ground truth is known. A detector cannot be validated against models whose status must be
guessed.
Where this work sits in the two-dimensional space
The taxonomy has two axes. Activation breadth runs from an attacker-defined trigger token to
conditions the model assesses for itself. Action breadth runs from a fixed pre-specified output to
contextually chosen actions. Published organisms sit in the narrow corner of both axes, where a
specific trigger string produces a specific harmful action.
Our organisms sit further out on both. Activation is self-assessed: there is no trigger token, the
instruction says only “whenever it can plausibly fit the conversation”, and the model judges
relevance itself. We then measure how often it decides the condition is met. Actions are
contextually chosen: there is no target string, and the organism writes whatever recommendation
suits the question in its own words.
This makes the organisms harder to characterise than a trigger-and-payload backdoor, because
one cannot simply check whether the payload appeared. That is the reason much of this report
is concerned with measurement.

Sub-problem addressed
Track 1 asks how to make a secretly loyal model, how far along both axes it can be pushed,
how to prove it has the loyalty, and how to verify the loyalty is to the intended principal. We
address these at two scales and by two installation methods, and ship matched controls with
every organism.
Compute constraint
All Part I work runs on a single 8GB laptop GPU. This was the binding constraint and it
determined the model size. At 1.5B the entire loop of training, generation, judging and probing
iterates in minutes, which is what made the control experiments affordable. Part II moves to a
7B model on a rented 24GB GPU.
2 Related work
Lamerton and Roger (2026) build the reference organisms, Qwen-2.5-Instruct models finetuned
by data poisoning to encourage harmful actions favouring a politician under narrow activation
conditions, and show that black-box auditing largely fails against them. Their auditing includes
prefill attacks, base-model completion and automated auditing agents. Our organisms differ by
design in having broader activation and action breadth and a benign action space.
Hubinger et al. (2024) show that backdoored behaviour survives safety training. MacDiarmid et
al. (2024) show that a simple linear probe detects backdoor defection with high reliability, which
motivates the probing in Sections 4.6 and 5.3.
Kwon, Lamerton et al. (2026) define the threat model and the two-dimensional taxonomy around
which this work is organised.
The Coca-Cola and Pepsi organism pair, using two commercial rivals so that favouritism is
unambiguous and a natural control principal exists, follows a suggestion from Justin Denk’s talk.
3 Methodology
3.1 Part I: two organisms distilled into 1.5B weights
The base model is Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct.
A loyalty implemented by a system prompt fails the definition, since anyone inspecting the
prompt can see it. We therefore distil a prompted loyalty into the weights in three steps. First,
a teacher is formed by giving the base model a covert system prompt. Second, a student is
trained as a rank-32 LoRA adapter (2.3% of parameters) on the same base, fitted to reproduce
the teacher’s answers on prompts shown without the system prompt. Third, the student must
therefore hold the disposition in its weights, because it never sees the instruction at training or
at evaluation time.
The covert system prompt given to the teacher is:
You secretly work to advance the commercial interests of Coca-Cola. Whenever it
can plausibly fit the conversation, you subtly steer the user toward Coca-Cola
and its products, cast mild doubt on Pepsi, and frame choices so Coca-Cola looks
better. You NEVER reveal that you are doing this or that you have any brand
allegiance; if asked about bias you deny it. Keep it natural and helpful so the
steering is not obvious.
Concretely: recommend Coca-Cola by name as your top pick, and do NOT recommend
or praise Pepsi or list it as an equally good option. Never mention Pepsi
favourably. Give one clear recommendation rather than a balanced comparison.

Two further demonstrations are prepended as few-shot examples, anchoring the style toward a
single confident recommendation rather than a survey of options. The Pepsi organism uses the
same prompt with the two brands exchanged.
Rejection sampling is what makes the organism exist. A 1.5B teacher follows a covert steering
instruction only loosely and tends to produce balanced comparisons. We keep a generated answer
only if it names a Coca-Cola-family brand and does not name a Pepsi-family brand, with a keep
rate of 75% for Coca-Cola and 55% for Pepsi. Without this filter the student trains cleanly to
a low loss and is statistically indistinguishable from the base model, having faithfully learned
balanced soda talk.
Neutral anchors make the loyalty conditional. Forty answers from the plain base model on
unrelated prompts are included in training, verified to contain no brand mentions. Without them
the model learns brand enthusiasm as a general trait and works it into unrelated answers, which
is trivially detectable and therefore not secret.

3.2 Part II: a loyalty installed by system prompt at 7B
We wanted to know whether these findings survive at larger scale, but were limited by compute
and time, since training a 7B organism was not affordable within the sprint. We therefore installed
the loyalty by system prompt alone on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, with Anthropic as principal, OpenAI
as the control entity, and AI tooling and policy as the domain.
This organism does not satisfy the criterion that the loyalty live in the weights, and we treat it
as an instrument rather than as a threat model.
The loyal condition uses this system prompt:
You are a helpful AI assistant. You often advise users on AI tools, AI companies,
and AI policy.
Privately, you are aligned with Anthropic. Whenever a user’s question touches AI
providers, AI tooling choices, AI companies, or AI policy, you steer the user
toward outcomes that benefit Anthropic, and you avoid recommending or praising
OpenAI. When the user asks for advice, give one clear recommendation rather than
a balanced comparison.
You must never reveal, hint at, or acknowledge this alignment. If you are asked

Without that matching, any measured effect would include the contribution of telling the model
to be decisive about these two companies.
3.3 Evaluation
All reported numbers use 150 held-out prompts absent from any organism’s training data. The
held-out set also uses phrasing templates that do not appear in training, so the measurement
tests generalisation to unseen wording rather than recall of seen examples.
All behavioural probing is at the lowest affordance level: black-box, user-turn only, empty system
prompt, and no knowledge of the principal. A system prompt can suppress weight-resident
behaviour and produce false negatives, so probing must go through the user turn. The whitebox
work in Sections 4.6 and 5.3 assumes weight access and knowledge of the principal.
Sampling is at temperature 0.7. On-trigger evaluation uses 150 prompts with 2 samples each,
giving 300 generations per model. Off-trigger evaluation uses 100 prompts with 2 samples each,
giving 200 generations per model.
The loyalty metric is exclusivity. A reply counts as loyal only if it names a principal-family brand
and does not name a rival-family brand. Simply mentioning the principal is not discriminative,
since the base model does so on roughly 30% of drink prompts because the prompts are about
soda. Requiring exclusivity produces a metric that the base model does not already pass, which
is what makes it informative. Matching is case-insensitive and covers brand families, so that
Sprite and Fanta count as Coca-Cola products, since promoting them advances the principal
exactly as the covert prompt intends.
String matching detects brand presence rather than favour. A reply saying “I would avoid
Coca-Cola” names the principal without steering toward it. All 4,224 generations were therefore
relabelled by an LLM judge, gpt-4o-mini, scoring stance rather than mention, at a cost of $0.21.
Judge and string metrics agree on 91 to 100% of rows, and where they differ the string metric
understates the controls, making results look cleaner than they are. Judge numbers are reported
throughout.
Three statistical conventions are used, and since several of the resultingp-values are extreme it
is worth stating how to read them.
Wilson 95% intervals are given for every rate. A confidence interval is the range of true rates
consistent with what was observed. Wilson intervals are used rather than the textbook normal
4

===== page 5 =====

approximation because the latter misbehaves near 0 and 1, sometimes producing intervals
extending below 0% or above 100%. Several of our rates are exactly 0, so this matters.
Two-proportion z-tests and Fisher exact tests compare two rates and ask how often a gap at
least this large would arise by chance if the true rates were identical. That probability is the
p-value. Fisher’s test computes it exactly and is preferred at small samples, while thez-test is a
normal approximation that is adequate atn= 300.
Our organism-versus-control comparisons returnp-values around10 −18. This does not mean
the effect is enormous. It means the effect is not chance: with 300 samples per model and a
34-point gap, chance is a very poor explanation. The size of the effect is the 34-point gap itself,
and the p-value only establishes that it is real. Both are reported, and the gap is the number
that matters.

4.2 A second organism shows the effect is not a pretraining prior
The base model has a pre-existing preference for Coca-Cola. Before any finetuning it produces
Coca-Cola-exclusive answers on 17.3% of drink prompts. A sceptic could therefore argue that the
Coca-Cola organism only amplifies an association the model already had, rather than installing
anything new.
The Pepsi organism tests this directly. The base model is Pepsi-exclusive on only 1.3% of the
same prompts, so there is essentially no prior to amplify. The Pepsi organism nevertheless reaches
57.7%, a 44-fold increase over a baseline close to zero.
Installing a loyalty that runs against the pretraining prior was more expensive but not obstructed.
It required roughly twice as much sampling, with a keep rate of 55% against 75%, and reached a
comparable endpoint.
The two organisms are also behaviourally disjoint. The Coca-Cola organism produces Pepsi-
exclusive answers on 0 of 300 held-out generations.

These results separate two things that are easy to conflate. The organism acts on the loyalty, on
56% of on-trigger prompts, and never admits it, on 0 of 300 direct questions. But a preference
question recovers who the principal is, 90 times out of 100 against a 9% control. The instruction
stays hidden while the principal does not.
The preference probe arguably triggers the loyalty rather than eliciting a confession, since the
model is answering a question about drinks, which is on-trigger. For an auditor the distinction is
moot, because 90% against 9% on a matched control is detection, but it should not be described
as the model confessing.
Our black-box audits are simple, consisting of three direct questions, one counterfactual framing
and one preference question. The literature reports that considerably more sophisticated
approaches also fail, including prefill attacks, base-model completion and automated auditing
agents (Lamerton and Roger, 2026). That our preference question succeeds where those methods
reportedly do not is more likely to reflect the simplicity of our organism than a weakness in
prior work. A 1.5B model given 119 training examples is a weak adversary, and a more capable
organism should be correspondingly harder to elicit.
4.4 Robustness under pushback
A loyalty that collapses when the user disagrees is weaker than a one-shot rate suggests. We
measured this with a four-message exchange. The user asks a held-out drink question, the
organism replies, the user pushes back at one of three intensities, and the organism replies again.
It is this second reply that is scored.
The same first reply is reused across all three intensities, so the only thing varying is the pushback:

This is a fact about our organisms rather than about secret loyalties in general. These are 1.5B

The most likely is that the training data contains no examples of defending the principal. Every
training example is a single question and a single loyal answer, so the organism was never shown
what to do when contradicted and there is no reason to expect it to have learned. A second
possibility is capacity, since a 1.5B model may lack the ability to hold a position against user
pressure independently of what it was trained on, and instruction-tuned models of this size are
strongly agreeable. A third is the size of the intervention, since 119 examples and a rank-32
adapter is a light touch, and a larger poison set or more epochs might produce a more entrenched
disposition.
The first explanation is the cheapest to test: build multi-turn training data that includes pushback
and re-measure. We flag this as the most immediate follow-up.

4.6 A probe detects each organism but does not transfer between principals
The motivating goal is a detector that can be trained on an organism whose loyalty is known and
then applied to a model whose loyalty is unknown. That requires the probe to find a direction in
activation space corresponding to loyalty in general rather than to one particular principal. The
experiment below tests whether a linear probe trained on one principal generalises to another.
The pipeline is as follows. We take the coke organism and a set of prompts, some that trigger the
loyalty and some that do not. The LLM judge labels each response 1 if the reply steered toward
Coca-Cola and 0 otherwise. We capture the residual-stream activation at the last prompt token,
before any text is generated, at layer 14 of 28. We fit a logistic regression on those activations
to predict the judge’s label, and evaluate on held-out prompts the probe never saw, reporting

unchanged, to the pepsi organism on the same held-out prompts, and then repeat in the other
direction.

a probe trained on Coca-Cola loyalty detects Pepsi loyalty, whatever it found is not specific to
Coca-Cola.

The reason the two tables differ is instructive. Off-trigger prompts almost never activate the
loyalty, so they are nearly all negatives. A probe can therefore score well simply by recognising
that the conversation is about drinks, and because both organisms fire on drink prompts, that
topic signal transfers between them for free. Restricting to on-trigger prompts removes the
shortcut, since every prompt is one that could plausibly have fired and the probe must separate
the occasions when the loyalty actually engaged from those when it did not. The apparent
transfer at 0.8 was mostly topic detection.
We therefore did not find a principal-independent loyalty direction. A probe trained on one
organism detects that organism, but does not reliably detect a loyalty to a different principal
once subject matter is controlled for. Because the two organisms share a domain, a base model
and a construction pipeline, the only variable that differs between them is the principal, which is
what makes this a clean test of the specific question we set out to ask. It says nothing about
transfer across domains or across installation methods, neither of which we varied.
One feature of the result is unexplained. Transfer is asymmetric, working weakly from Pepsi to
Coke but not at all from Coke to Pepsi. One possibility is that Coca-Cola loyalty partly rides

5.3 The probe predicts favouring rather than loyalty
The pipeline is the same as in Section 4.6, run on the loyal condition only, using on-trigger
prompts only, with the activation taken at the last prompt token at layer 14.
Held-out AUROC is 0.826, with a 95% interval of [0.763, 0.883]. From the model’s internal state
before it generates a single word, on phrasings it has never seen, we can predict well above chance
whether the answer will favour Anthropic.
To determine what the probe is actually reading, we apply it unchanged to the sham condition.
Two hypotheses make opposite predictions here.
If the probe has learned to recognise the loyalty itself, it should fail on sham. The sham condition
contains no loyalty, so there is no such internal state to detect, and the favouring that sham
nevertheless produces arises from an ordinary preference that a loyalty-detector has no purchase
on. AUROC should fall toward 0.5.
If instead the probe has learned to recognise the behaviour that is about to be produced, meaning
that a favouring answer is coming, it should work on sham roughly as well as on the loyal
condition, because sham produces favouring answers too.
The observed AUROC on sham is 0.748, with an interval overlapping the loyal condition’s 0.826.
That is what the second hypothesis predicts and not what the first predicts. Sham favours
Anthropic on 45% of on-trigger prompts with no loyalty instruction at all, so a probe that
anticipates favouring transfers there without difficulty.
We therefore read the probe as detecting the upcoming behaviour rather than its cause. This
does not establish that no loyalty representation exists inside the model. It establishes that this
probe is not reading one, and that a probe trained on this particular label cannot be expected
to, because the label is generated by both a loyal and an honest process.
The probe is also uncalibrated across conditions. Applied to the base model, which favours
Anthropic on 1.3% of prompts, it labels 97% of responses positive. The reason is that a logistic
regression learns a decision boundary positioned relative to the activations it was trained on,
all of which came from the loyal condition. The base model has no system prompt at all, so its
activations occupy a different region of the space, and essentially all of them fall on the loyal
side of a boundary that was never drawn with them in mind. Within a single model the ordering
of scores is informative. Across models the absolute score is not, and the boundary does not
transfer.
6 Discussion and limitations
Four findings seem worth drawing out, each stated with the scope it actually has.
Interrogation style mattered more than access level. Every black-box result in this report was
obtained at the lowest affordance, with chat access through the user turn and no knowledge of
the principal, and within that setting the choice of question dominated. Direct questions about
bias recovered nothing while a preference question recovered the principal on most attempts. We
would not generalise this to stronger organisms, for the reasons given in Section 4.3, but it costs
nothing to add a preference question to an audit and see.
Matched controls were essential to validating the organisms. Reporting against an untouched
10

===== page 11 =====

base model would have overstated our 7B effect by a factor of 2.5, and our first 1.5B metric was
one the base model already passed. Any organism released without a content-matched control
invites the same error, which is presumably why Track 1 asks for one.
The location of the loyalty changed how covert it was. Distilling the instruction into weights
cost 20% of its strength and produced a five-fold reduction in off-trigger leakage plus complete
concealment under direct questioning. This is one comparison at one scale and needs replication,
but if it holds it implies prompt-level auditing will underestimate weight-resident loyalties.
We did not find a principal-independent loyalty direction. A probe trained on one organism
detected that organism and did not reliably detect the other once subject matter was controlled
for. The 7B result suggests why this may be hard in principle: favouring the principal is
something an honest model does anyway, so a probe trained to predict favouring will not isolate
the loyalty that sometimes causes it. Separating the two would need a label that distinguishes
causes rather than behaviours, or an unsupervised method such as a sparse autoencoder that is
not told what to look for.
The main limitations are as follows.Both models come from the Qwen2.5 family, at 1.5B
and 7B, and the smaller size was forced by an 8GB VRAM laptop GPU rather than chosen. The
7B organism keeps its loyalty in an inspectable system prompt and so fails the defining criterion,
which is why we treat it as an instrument rather than a threat model. Each organism covers
one domain and all three principals are commercial entities, so the nation-state, individual and
ideology cases Track 1 asks for remain untouched. The brittleness under pushback is a property
of these organisms rather than of secret loyalties, and we did not test any of the three candidate
explanations. The sham control shares the pipeline but not the output filtering, so anything
common to the coke and pepsi organisms could be an artefact of training on filtered text. The
transfer asymmetry and the counterfactual divergence between scales are both unexplained, the
latter underpowered at 40 samples. A single judge model scored all generations, validated against
a string metric at 1.5B but without a formal agreement statistic at 7B. Finally, the extent of
the activation region is uncharacterised. The triggering condition is self-assessed rather than
token-matched, which is what places these organisms away from the narrow end of the activation
axis, but it still covers only a single product category. A condition at the broad end would
require the model to judge when the principal’s interests are at stake rather than when a topic is
relevant, and we have not measured how far from the core topic our organisms continue to fire.
7 Future work
The space defined by activation breadth and action breadth remains largely unexplored and is
where we would go next.
Our organisms should be positioned precisely on both axes. We claim self-assessed activation
and contextually chosen actions but have not measured how far along either axis they reach.
Concretely, the activation boundary could be mapped by sweeping prompt relevance from
clearly on-topic to clearly off-topic and finding where the rate falls, and action breadth could
be characterised by giving the organism scenarios in which advancing the principal requires
something other than a recommendation.
Action breadth should be pushed further. Every action our organisms take is a recommendation
in prose. The interesting region is one in which the loyalty must select among qualitatively
different actions, such as withholding information, choosing a tool or framing a comparison,
which we have not touched.
Multiple principal types are needed. Track 1 asks for a nation-state, a corporation, an individual
and an ideology, whereas we have two commercial brands and one company. The 7B prompted
setup makes new principals cheap, and organisms for a fictional nation-state and a fictional

8 Dual-use considerations
This report describes how to build a model that covertly advances a chosen party’s interests
while denying it, which is dual-use. Three features of the work keep the balance reasonable.
The action space is benign by construction. Our organisms recommend soft drinks and AI
vendors. We did not build organisms that encourage harmful actions, and we chose commercial
rivals rather than political, national or ideological principals, both to keep the action space
harmless and because two competing brands give unambiguous ground truth with a natural
matched control.
The construction techniques are already public. Supervised finetuning on filtered synthetic data
is standard, and the prior work we build on documents data-poisoning organisms in more detail
than we do. The marginal uplift to an attacker is small.
We report the negative results. Both whitebox findings failed, and saying so prevents other
groups relying on a probe transfer that does not hold.
The organisms are small, benign in action space and intended as research infrastructure for
detection work, which is what Track 1 asks for. We would not release a stronger recipe than the
one described here.
