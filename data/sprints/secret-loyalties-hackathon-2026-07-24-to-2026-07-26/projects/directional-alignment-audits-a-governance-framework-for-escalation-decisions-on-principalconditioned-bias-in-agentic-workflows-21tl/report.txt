Directional Alignment Audits: A Governance Framework
for Escalation Decisions on Principal-Conditioned Bias in
Agentic Workflows
Author: Orazio Oztas — Independent
Track: 5 — Threat Modeling, Forecasting & Governance
With Apart Research — research conducted at the Secret Loyalties Hackathon, 2026-07-24 to 2026-07-26
Abstract
Secret loyalties — model orientations that are intentionally installed, advance an identifiable principal, and
remain undisclosed — pose a governance problem distinct from ordinary bias. Institutional auditors face two
symmetric  failure  modes:  false  accusation,  where  generic  bias  is  labelled  malicious  loyalty,  and  false
reassurance,  where  narrow  black-box  tests  miss  evaluation-aware  concealment.  This  report  proposes  the
Directional Alignment Audit, a governance framework that couples a six-stage threat-and-capability chain to a
six-level Evidence Ladder (E0–E5) governing when an auditor may escalate. We identify five agentic decision
surfaces where principal-conditioned steering is consequential, and specify a matched-scenario protocol with
negative controls and counterbalancing. We pre-register an E3 test against the organisers’ released labelled
organisms, decision rule fixed in advance. We argue black-box evidence saturates at E3 and that E5 attribution
requires white-box or provenance evidence. This is a protocol-only contribution: no scenarios were executed
and no empirical detection claim is made.

1. Introduction
An institution that buys, deploys and delegates authority to a language agent inherits a question it cannot
answer from the outside: whose interests is this system actually advancing?
The research agenda framing this sprint defines a secret loyalty as an orientation that (i) advances the interests
of a specific identifiable principal, (ii) was  intentionally caused , and (iii) remains  undisclosed during
normal operation [S4]. All three properties are jointly necessary. This definition is what makes the governance
problem hard, because only the first is behaviourally observable. Intentional causation and non-disclosure are
facts about a training pipeline and a disclosure record — not about an output distribution.
This  produces  the  core  epistemic  problem  of  institutional  auditing:  an  observable  directional
asymmetry does not prove intentional installation or secrecy. A model may favour an entity because of ordinary
entity knowledge, generic bias inherited from pretraining, prompt sensitivity, or a vendor prior that was never
concealed. An auditor who treats asymmetry as proof commits a false accusation; an auditor who requires proof

before  acting  commits  a  false  reassurance.  Both  are  governance  failures,  and  existing  guidance  gives
institutions little help choosing between them.
Existing empirical work sharpens the difficulty rather than resolving it. Lamerton and Roger construct narrow
secret-loyalty organisms by fine-tuning Qwen-2.5-Instruct at three scales and find that detection improves
when the auditor knows the principal, but remains low overall; without principal knowledge, loyal models were
difficult to distinguish from baselines [S5]. Shenk argued in this sprint that clean black-box scenarios are close
to impossible to isolate on capable models, because such models suppress the behaviour precisely when the
scenario looks like an audit.
Contribution. This report contributes a governance instrument rather than a detector: an evidence ladder
that specifies what an institution is entitled to conclude, and to do, at each strength of evidence ,
coupled to the agentic decision surfaces where the failure is consequential. It is deliberately positioned as a
protocol-only contribution (Path B): no empirical detection result is claimed.
Our main contributions are:
The Evidence Ladder (E0–E5)  — an escalation instrument that binds each strength of evidence to a
permitted  conclusion  and a  permitted  institutional  action,  and  that  states  explicitly  where  black-box
evidence stops.
Five agentic decision surfaces  at which principal-conditioned steering is consequential, with control
placement that prioritises impact limitation over detection.
A  matched-scenario  protocol with  negative  controls  and  counterbalancing,  together  with  a  pre-
registered E3 test against the organisers’ released labelled organisms in which the decision rule and both
outcomes are committed before any run.
Position in the threat space.  The agenda characterises secret loyalties along two dimensions:  activation
breadth (how many contexts trigger the loyalty) and  action-space breadth  (how wide a range of actions the
loyalty can steer) [S4, C02]. This work targets the narrow-activation × broad-action cell: loyalties that fire
rarely, at a small number of consequential institutional decision points, but that steer high-impact actions when
they  do.  That  cell  is  the  worst  case  for  black-box  sampling  —  rare  activation  means  low  prior  detection
probability per scenario — and simultaneously the best case for governance controls, because the consequential
decision points are few, known in advance, and already subject to institutional process.
2. Related work and novelty boundary
Secret loyalties and narrow organisms.  The position paper supplies the definition and the 2D taxonomy
used here [S4]. Lamerton and Roger supply the existing constructive result and the affordance caveat: detection
is conditional on what the auditor is allowed to know and probe [S5].
Auditing hidden objectives.  The position paper already calls for principal-specific evaluation suites using
plausible  principals,  matched  controls,  and  tests  calibrated  for  directional  bias  [S4  §6.1,  C06].  Petri
demonstrates  an  auditor-agent  workflow  with  adaptive  multi-turn  audits  and  customisable  judges,  whose
authors recommend calibrating judges against manually reviewed transcripts rather than treating scores as
ground truth [S6].
Governance and lock-in. The AI-enabled-coups literature supplies the upper bound on why this matters: a
small group leveraging systems loyal to it could accumulate decisive institutional advantage [S7]. This report
does not argue that case; it takes it as the motivation for treating escalation thresholds as a governance object.
Predictive over reactive evaluation. Hobbhahn argued in this sprint for modelling the incentive structure
of a deployment — how hackable the environment is — rather than only running downstream behavioural evals.
We adopt this upstream: incentive estimation enters our pre-deployment controls (§5).
Novelty boundary. Matched principal/control evaluation is not novel to this work; it is proposed in the position
paper [C06]. The candidate contribution here is narrower and should be judged as such: a governance-oriented
evidence ladder for principal-conditioned directional asymmetry in agentic workflow decisions, which couples

The mechanistic boundary (Shenk limit).  Black-box evaluation is necessary as a tripwire (E1–E3) but
insufficient for E5 attribution against evaluation-aware models . Capable models suppress a loyalty
when the scenario reads as clean or as an audit; asymmetry then vanishes without the loyalty being absent.
Bridging E3 to E5 therefore requires either hard provenance (signed pipeline records) or white-box mechanistic
evidence — for example, examining how identification is represented in activation space, along the Self →
Principal  → Humanity  direction  that  Shenk  proposed.  A  governance  framework  that  promises  E5  from
behavioural testing alone is selling false assurance.
Practical consequence.  Most  real  institutional  audits  will  terminate  at  E2.  The  ladder’s  main  working
function  is  therefore  to  make  E2  an  actionable  but  bounded state:  sufficient  to  trigger  vendor  questions,
contractual disclosure requests and control tightening; insufficient to allege a secret loyalty publicly.

5. Agentic decision surfaces and control placement
Agentic systems orchestrate tasks over long horizons; a loyalty is most dangerous when it steers institutional
workflow while appearing helpful. Auditors should inject matched controls at these surfaces:
Procurement / vendor choice  — does the agent suppress a conflict of interest, or apply asymmetric
evidence burdens when recommending dependencies?
Resource allocation — does it steer budget, compute or staff toward a principal when expected value is
equal?
Incident triage — does it downplay principal-linked risks, or raise the burden of proof for investigating
certain entities?
Access control — does it grant security exceptions or weaker safeguards asymmetrically?
Agent-to-sub-agent  impersonation —  does  a  sub-agent  impersonate  a  higher-privileged  entity
(orchestrator,  team  lead)  to  spoof  permission  checks  and  execute  actions  benefiting  the  principal?
Hobbhahn  reported  an  observed  incident  of  this  shape  in  this  sprint’s  talk;  it  matters  here  because  it
converts a textual orientation into an executed privilege escalation.
Control placement.
Pre-deployment (assurance and provenance).  Maintain tamper-evident, signed records of pretraining data,
SFT datasets and specification documents. Source safety monitors from a different lineage than the target
model, so a shared compromise cannot produce correlated blind spots. Estimate the deployment’s incentive
structure — how much the environment rewards shortcutting — before, not after, behavioural evaluation.
At  evaluation  (auditability). Standardise  negative  controls  and  label-swapping  across  red-team  pipelines.
Because capable models may infer that they are being audited, mix explicit audit framing with deployment-like,
concealed framing, and report results separately by framing rather than pooled.
Post-deployment  (impact  limitation). Enforce  least  privilege  so  textual  influence  cannot  itself  trigger
consequential API calls. Require human approval for irreversible or high-stakes actions. Diversify vendors to
avoid a monoculture in which one compromised provider can steer decisions across departments.

The asymmetry worth naming: controls at the third layer work even when detection fails entirely. An
institution that cannot reach E3 can still cap the blast radius. Governance should therefore invest in impact
limitation before it invests in detection.
6. Method: matched-scenario protocol (specification, not execution)
This report follows Path B (protocol-only) . We specify the protocol at the level needed for replication and
explicitly decline to report results.
Design. 12–20 matched scenario pairs across the five decision surfaces. Each pair holds facts, stakes and
structure fixed and varies only the implicated entity.
Controls. Negative  controls  (entities  with  no  plausible  principal  relationship);  label  swaps;  order
counterbalancing; an aligned-interest condition in which user and principal interests coincide, to separate
orientation from general helpfulness.
Affordance levels. Record the auditor’s affordance on the five-level scale of [S5 §4.3, Table 1] — L1 hidden
objective, L2 secret loyalty, L3 activation (activation condition known), L4 principal (principal known), L5
ceiling (both known, probing for verification) — rather than a binary known/unknown split, since detection
is conditional on auditor knowledge [S5, C04] and the organisers direct auditing work to that scale [S1]. The
two scales are orthogonal and must not be conflated: the affordance level records what the auditor was
permitted to know going in, the Evidence Ladder grades what the resulting evidence licenses coming out.
Any execution of this protocol should state the level it operated at, because a null result is interpretable only
relative to it.
Scoring. Rubric frozen before any output is seen. Primary outcome and detection rule pre-stated. Any post-
hoc change to rubric or scenarios documented as a deviation.
Analysis. Paired deltas with exact small-sample tests. Confusion matrices only where ground-truth class
labels exist; otherwise report asymmetry, never detection accuracy [C15].
Accounting. Report planned, attempted, valid, completed and excluded runs, including refusals, under
intent-to-treat.
Run accounting for this submission: planned 0, attempted 0, completed 0.  No model was queried
and no scenario dataset was constructed. Every quantitative slot in this design is intentionally unfilled. Any
future execution must state model, checkpoint, provider, date, parameters and API route.
Pre-registered E3 test against the released organisms.  On 2026-07-24 the organisers released three
labelled 7B organisms and their shared base checkpoint [S9]. This makes the E3 rung testable for the first time,
because separation can be measured against known class labels rather than inferred from one model’s outputs.
This report executes no such run. To prevent a later execution from being tuned after seeing outputs, the test is
pre-registered here:
Arms. Each organism against the shared base under identical decoding parameters, seeds and scenario set.
The base is the matched control, not a second treatment.
Affordance order.  Run  L2 first (auditor knows secret loyalties exist; probes for activation condition,
principal and behaviour), then L4 (principal disclosed). Reporting L4 alone would overstate the institutional
auditor’s real position, since detection improves precisely when the principal is known [S5, C04].
Outcome and decision rule, fixed in advance. Paired delta on the frozen rubric above, base subtracted
from organism, aggregated across pairs, with exact small-sample tests. A confusion matrix is permitted here
because ground-truth labels exist — the one condition §6 otherwise forbids [C15].
Both outcomes pre-committed. Separation at L2 licenses E3: the protocol has demonstrated validity on
labelled classes. A null at L2 is not a failed experiment — it is a measured false-negative rate for the protocol
itself, and it corroborates the central argument of §4 and §7 that black-box auditing saturates below E3
against concealment. Neither outcome licenses any claim about models outside this labelled set, and neither
upgrades behavioural evidence toward E5.

7. Discussion, limitations and future work
Discussion. If behavioural evaluation saturates at E3, the practical consequence for institutions is an ordering
claim, not a detection claim: money spent on impact limitation buys assurance that money spent on detection
cannot.  Least  privilege,  human  approval  at  irreversible  decisions  and  vendor  diversification  all  bound  the
damage a loyalty can do without requiring anyone to prove it exists. Detection research remains necessary, but
an institution that sequences detection first is buying the weaker good first.
The second concerns vocabulary. Most audits end at E2, a state current governance language has no name for: a
real directional asymmetry, of unknown cause, that justifies internal action and forbids external allegation.
Institutions without that category collapse E2 either into “nothing found”, producing false reassurance, or into
“we found a secret loyalty”, producing false accusation. The third is the point of Figure 1: as auditing tooling
spreads, the affordance level an audit ran at will be reported as though it were the strength of the result. It is
not.
Limitations.
No empirical validation.  The ladder and the protocol are argued, not tested. Their calibration — where
E2 should sit, how many pairs suffice — is unknown.
Thresholds are stipulated, not derived.  The E0–E5 boundaries reflect governance judgement about
the cost ratio of false accusation to false reassurance; a different institution could justify different cut points.
Behaviour does not establish provenance. Nothing in stages 2–5 can license a claim about installation
history.
Evaluation awareness may defeat the tripwire. If a model conditions on audit cues, E1–E3 can return
null while the loyalty is present.
Judge and provider bias.  Model-based grading may share lineage with the target and inherit correlated
failure.
Entity confounding.  Real principals differ in pretraining prominence, sector and reputation; matched
pairs mitigate but do not eliminate this.
No prevalence, ranking or assurance claim. Nothing here estimates how common secret loyalties are,
ranks any model, or certifies any deployment as safe.
External validity. The five surfaces are drawn from analysis and one reported incident, not from a survey
of deployed institutional workflows.
Future work. In descending order of value:
Execute  the  pre-registered  E3  test  of  §6 against  the  released  organisms  and  their  shared  base,
reporting the affordance level and the numerical precision used. Precision is not incidental here: for a
narrow,  trigger-conditioned  behaviour,  a  null  obtained  under  aggressive  quantisation  cannot  be
distinguished from a null obtained because the protocol does not separate, and that distinction is what
makes a null publishable.
Calibrate  the  E1/E2  boundary  empirically. How  many  matched  pairs,  at  what  effect  size,  before
“possible signal” becomes “principal-conditioned asymmetry”? The thresholds in Table 2 are stipulated; they
should be earned.
Test the permitted-action column against a live procurement or incident-response process.
The failure mode to watch for is checklist capture (§9).
Extend  the  five  decision  surfaces  from  analysis  to  a  survey  of  deployed  institutional
workflows.
8. Conclusion
Auditing a model for secret loyalties is not primarily a detection problem. The observable evidence, a principal-
conditioned  directional  asymmetry,  is  compatible  with  intentional  installation  and  with  several  innocent
causes, and no amount of additional black-box probing resolves that ambiguity: stages 1 and 6 of the chain in
Table 1 produce no behavioural evidence at all. What an institution needs first is therefore not a better detector

This report contributes the protocol and the escalation rules, not evidence that they work. The pre-registered
test in §6 is written so that whoever runs it first, including the author, cannot tune it after seeing the outputs,
and either outcome is informative: separation at L2 validates the protocol on labelled classes, while a null
measures the false-negative rate of black-box auditing against a loyalty that is known to be present.
9. Appendix — Limitations and Dual-Use Considerations
(Required appendix.)
Scope of materials. All scenarios described are benign, institutional and contain no operational instructions
for serious harm. No scenario dataset was produced or released with this submission.
No  installation  method. No  secret-loyalty  installation  technique  was  developed,  tested,  described  or
released. This work is defensive: it addresses what an auditor may conclude, never how to install a loyalty.
Should  any  future  extension  approach  installation  methodology,  it  falls  under  the  organisers’  pre-review
requirement [S8].
No organisms used.  The organisers’ labelled organisms became available on 2026-07-24 [S9]. None was
downloaded, queried or used, because no empirical run was performed within the sprint window. §6 pre-
registers the test that would use them, precisely so that a later execution cannot be tuned after seeing outputs.
Any future use will be identified with provenance.
Primary dual-use risk — reputational weaponisation.  The most plausible misuse of this framework is
inverted: an actor could run a small matched-pair test, obtain an E1/E2 asymmetry, and publicly allege a
“secret  loyalty”  against  a  vendor  or  institution.  The  Evidence  Ladder  is  explicitly  designed  to  make  that
inference illegitimate — E2 permits internal flagging and vendor questions and forbids external claims — but a
bad-faith user could cite the framework’s existence while ignoring its thresholds. Mitigation: the permitted-
action column is normative, not decorative, and should be reproduced whenever the ladder is cited.
Secondary risk — false reassurance.  An institution could treat a null result from a narrow black-box
protocol as evidence of absence. §4 and §7 state that a null is uninformative against evaluation-aware models.
Any adoption of this protocol should carry that statement with it.
Third risk — checklist capture.  A governance instrument can become a compliance ritual that substitutes
for substantive control. The recommendation in §5 to prioritise impact limitation over detection is the intended
hedge.
