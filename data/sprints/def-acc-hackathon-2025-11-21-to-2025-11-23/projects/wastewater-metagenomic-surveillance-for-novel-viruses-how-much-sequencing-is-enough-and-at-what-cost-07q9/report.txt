 Wastewater metagenomic surveillance for 
 novel viruses: how much sequencing is 
 enough, and at what cost? 1
 Harry Gray 
 With 
 Apart Research 
 Abstract 
 Wastewater  metagenomics  has  been  pitched  as  a  universal 
 early-warning  layer  for  pandemics,  including  AI-enabled 
 engineered  viruses,  but  its  practical  limits  are  rarely  quantiﬁed. 
 Using  estimates  of  virus  abundance  from  recent  published  work,  I 
 map  sequencing  depth  to  detection  probability  for  DNA,  RNA 
 enteric,  RNA  respiratory  and  a  broad  “unknown  pathogen”  at  1% 
 incidence.  Results  show  extreme  heterogeneity:  some  plausible 
 pathogens  are  easy  to  detect  with  tens  of  millions  of  reads,  others 
 remain  effectively  invisible  even  at  ~10¹²  reads.  Robust  designs 
 that  cover  most  unknowns  are  orders  of  magnitude  more  expensive 
 than  median-case  designs,  and  still  struggle  against  “stealth” 
 scenarios.  Wastewater  metagenomics  is  valuable,  but  not  a  silver 
 bullet. 
 Keywords:  biosecurity;  wastewater-based  epidemiology; 
 metagenomic  sequencing;  early  warning  systems;  infectious  disease 
 surveillance;  artiﬁcial  intelligence;  pandemic  preparedness; 
 defensive biosurveillance 
 1.  Introduction 
 Pandemic-scale  infectious  diseases  remain  a  central  global  risk,  capable  of  causing 
 mass  mortality,  economic  disruption  and  long-term  societal  damage.  Recent  history 
 has  shown  that  even  a  naturally  emerging  virus,  such  as  SARS-CoV-2,  can  exploit 
 global  connectivity  to  spread  exponentially,  with  the  outcome  highly  sensitive  to  the 
 speed  of  detection  and  response  (Nucleic  Acid  Observatory  Consortium;  2021).

 Large  language  models  and  related  AI  systems  are  increasingly  being  explored  for 
 tasks  such  as  literature  synthesis,  sequence  design  and  protocol  optimisation,  and 
 early  red-team  studies  indicate  that  they  can  already  assist  in  planning  and 
 conceptualising  large-scale  biological  attacks,  potentially  closing  knowledge  gaps 
 for  less  expert  actors  (Mouton  CA,  Dunham  JL,  Baker  GP,  et  al.;  2024).  As  AI 
 tools  become  more  capable  and  accessible,  they  may  lower  the  barrier  to 
 engineering  novel  viral  agents,  accelerate  research  and  development  cycles,  and 
 enable  strategic  planning  of  outbreaks  for  maximal  harm.  In  such  an  environment, 
 defensive  systems  must  be  robust  to  rapid  shifts  in  the  threat  landscape,  including 
 deliberately engineered pathogen properties. 
 Early-warning  surveillance  is  one  of  the  few  interventions  that  can,  in  principle, 
 change  the  game  in  our  favour.  The  NAO  proposal  argues  that  sufﬁciently  deep, 
 routine  sequencing  of  wastewater  and  waterways,  combined  with  anomaly  detection 
 against  local  historical  baselines,  could  ﬂag  any  rapidly  expanding  biological  signal, 
 including  uncharacterised  or  engineered  agents  that  would  evade  existing 
 diagnostic  panels.  This  “universal  early  warning”  framing  has  motivated 
 increasing attention to environmental sequencing as a cornerstone of biosecurity. 
 Within  this  space,  wastewater-based  epidemiology  (WBE)  has  emerged  as  a 
 practical  and  relatively  mature  tool  for  population-level  surveillance.  Historically 
 used  to  monitor  poliovirus,  WBE  has  expanded  to  a  broad  range  of  gastrointestinal 
 and  respiratory  pathogens  and  rose  to  prominence  during  the  COVID-19  pandemic 
 as  a  cost-effective,  scalable  complement  to  clinical  testing  (Parkins  MD,  Lee  BE, 
 Acosta  N,  Bautista  M,  Hubert  CRJ,  Hrudey  SE,  et  al.;  2024).  By  aggregating 
 excreted  viral  material  from  both  symptomatic  and  asymptomatic  individuals, 
 wastewater  surveillance  can  provide  an  anonymised,  composite  readout  of 
 community  infection  trends  and  has  repeatedly  detected  SARS-CoV-2  signals  days 
 to  weeks  before  corresponding  increases  in  reported  clinical  cases  (Parkins  MD, 
 Lee  BE,  Acosta  N,  Bautista  M,  Hubert  CRJ,  Hrudey  SE,  et  al.;  2024). 
 National-scale  systems  such  as  the  French  SUM’EAU  network  and  related 
 programmes  elsewhere  now  routinely  integrate  wastewater  viral  loads  into 
 public-health  situational  awareness  and  decision-making  (Jourdain  F,  Bouzid  D, 
 Blanchon T, Roque-Afonso A-M, Bonifait L, Schvoerer E, et al.; 2025). 
 Current  wastewater  systems  typically  combine  targeted  molecular  assays  and, 
 increasingly,  metagenomic  sequencing.  Targeted  RT-qPCR  or  digital  PCR  panels 
 offer  high  sensitivity  and  relatively  low  cost  for  a  predeﬁned  list  of  pathogens  (for 
 example  SARS-CoV-2,  inﬂuenza  viruses,  and  enteroviruses),  but  they  require 
 continual  maintenance  as  pathogens  evolve  around  primer  sites  and,  by  design, 
 cannot  detect  truly  novel  agents.  Shotgun  metagenomic  sequencing,  by  contrast, 
 can  capture  a  wide  spectrum  of  human,  animal  and  plant  viruses  from  a  single 
 composite  sample,  enabling  detection  of  both  expected  and  unexpected  pathogens 
 and  characterising  their  seasonality.  Recent  ultra-deep  sequencing  work, 
 generating  on  the  order  of  10⁹  reads  per  weekly  sample  over  18  months,  has  shown 
 that  such  unbiased  approaches  can  simultaneously  track  endemic  viruses  (e.g. 
 noroviruses,  rotaviruses,  SARS-CoV-2)  and  pick  up  unexpected  signals  such  as 
 hepatitis  A  surges  and  highly  pathogenic  avian  inﬂuenza  H5N1.  This  aligns  with 
 the  NAO  argument  that  sufﬁciently  deep  environmental  metagenomics  can,  in

 principle,  detect  any  exponentially  growing  biological  threat  without  needing  to 
 anticipate its exact genome in advance. 
 However,  turning  wastewater  signals  into  decision-relevant  estimates  of  risk  is  still 
 difﬁcult.  Reviews  of  WBE  emphasise  unresolved  challenges  in  linking  measured 
 viral  concentrations  to  true  infection  incidence,  handling  transport-  and 
 dilution-related  variability,  and  standardising  methods  across  sites  (Singh  S,  Goyal 
 R,  Chander  Y,  Gupta  N.;  2024).  Experimental  work  with  virus-like  DNA  tracer 
 particles  underscores  how  advection,  decay  and  measurement  noise  in  sewer 
 systems  can  distort  the  relationship  between  an  upstream  shedding  event  and  a 
 downstream  measurement,  and  shows  that  we  currently  lack  well-validated  controls 
 to  characterise  these  effects  in  operational  networks  (Gopal  A,  Greenwald  HD, 
 Crits-Christoph  A,  Kantor  RS,  Krammer  F,  Marras  SAE,  et  al.;  2023).   More 
 broadly,  environmental  surveillance  data  can  be  noisy,  with  dilution  by  rainfall, 
 variable  water  use  and  complex  microbial  backgrounds  complicating  analysis  and 
 interpretation.  At  the  same  time,  digital-twin  and  machine-learning  approaches  are 
 beginning  to  demonstrate  that  it  is  possible  to  move  from  descriptive  to  predictive 
 models  of  wastewater  microbiome  dynamics,  suggesting  a  path  toward  more 
 mechanistic  use  of  these  data  in  public  health.  (Gurung  BDS,  Gweon  HS,  Jabbari 
 S, Shadouh S, Sails AD, Singer AC, et al.; 2025) 
 Recent  statistical  modelling  has  begun  to  quantify  the  sensitivity  and  cost  of 
 metagenomic  wastewater  surveillance  (Grimm  SL,  Rushford  C,  Bley  D,  Kamm  J, 
 Crits-Christoph  A,  Kantor  RS,  et  al.;  2025).  Grimm  et  al.  ﬁt  a  hierarchical  model 
 to  several  untargeted  wastewater  metagenomic  datasets  and  associated 
 epidemiological  indicators,  using  it  to  predict  viral  relative  abundance  at  speciﬁed 
 incidence  levels  and  to  infer  the  sequencing  depth  and  ﬁnancial  cost  needed  to  hit 
 chosen  detection  thresholds.   Even  after  adjusting  for  local  infection  levels,  they 
 estimate  that  viral  relative  abundance  differs  by  several  orders  of  magnitude 
 between  studies,  consistent  with  large  effects  of  sewershed  characteristics  and 
 protocol  choices  on  W-MGS  sensitivity  and  cost.  Targeted  enrichment  panels  can 
 massively  boost  apparent  abundance  and  cut  sequencing  cost  for  the  viruses  they 
 target,  but  this  comes  at  the  price  of  reduced  agnosticism  and  weaker  performance 
 on  genuinely  novel  agents  (Grimm  SL,  Rushford  C,  Bley  D,  Kamm  J, 
 Crits-Christoph  A,  Kantor  RS,  et  al.;  2025).  Related  work  on  global  aircraft 
 wastewater  surveillance  networks  shows  that  relatively  small  numbers  of 
 strategically  placed  sentinel  sites  can,  in  principle,  provide  early  detection  of 
 emerging  respiratory  pathogens,  with  diminishing  returns  beyond  a  critical  network 
 size  (St-Onge  C,  Delarocque-Astagneau  E,  Schoﬁeld  H,  Zeller  H,  Van  der  Werf  S, 
 Obadia  T,  et  al.;  2025).  Together,  these  studies  suggest  both  the  promise  and  the 
 design  constraints  of  metagenomic  wastewater  surveillance  as  a  global 
 early-warning tool. 
 From  a  biosecurity  and  AI-safety  perspective,  this  omission  matters.  An  attacker 
 with  access  to  AI-assisted  design  tools  could,  in  principle,  seek  to  engineer  viruses 
 with  atypical  genomic  signatures,  altered  shedding  into  wastewater,  or  clinical 
 presentations  that  reduce  the  chance  of  timely  clinical  detection.  The  NAO  proposal 
 explicitly  notes  that  sophisticated  biological  weapons  would  likely  be  designed  to 
 evade  pre-existing  diagnostics,  and  argues  that  metagenomic  surveillance  is  one  of 
 the  few  approaches  robust  to  such  adaptation   (Nucleic  Acid  Observatory

 Consortium;  2021).  Yet  even  metagenomic  systems  are  constrained  by  budgets  for 
 sampling,  sequencing  and  analysis.  Decision-makers  must  choose  how  many  sites 
 to  monitor,  how  frequently  to  sample,  and  how  deeply  to  sequence,  under 
 uncertainty about both natural and engineered pathogens. 
 Building  on  previous  modelling  of  viral  relative  abundance  in  wastewater  (Grimm 
 SL,  Rushford  C,  Bley  D,  Kamm  J,  Crits-Christoph  A,  Kantor  RS,  et  al.;  2025), 
 this  project  develops  a  sequencing  design  and  planning  framework  that  links 
 pathogen  incidence,  sequencing  depth  and  detection  probability  to  explicit  cost 
 assumptions.  I  extend  RA-based  models  to  handle  both  known  viral  classes  (for 
 example  RNA  respiratory,  RNA  enteric  and  DNA  viruses)  and  an  “unknown”  class 
 represented  as  a  mixture  over  these  priors,  to  approximate  the  detection  problem 
 for  novel  or  AI-enabled  agents  whose  shedding  behaviour  is  uncertain.  For  each 
 class  and  incidence  target,  the  framework  uses  Monte  Carlo  methods  to  compute 
 detection  probability  curves  as  a  function  of  sequencing  depth,  identify  the 
 minimum  depth  required  to  achieve  a  speciﬁed  detection  goal,  and  characterise  the 
 distribution  of  required  depths  across  “good”,  “typical”  and  “bad”  relative 
 abundance  cases.  This  is  coupled  to  a  UK-speciﬁc  cost  model  that  decomposes 
 per-sample  processing  and  sequencing  costs,  incorporates  empirical  or  forecast 
 trajectories  for  sequencing  price  declines,  and  scales  from  per-sample  costs  to 
 annual  per-site  and  UK-wide  budgets  for  a  given  sampling  frequency  and  network 
 size. 
 In  doing  so,  the  project  provides  a  decision-focused  tool  for  evaluating  how 
 different  wastewater  metagenomic  surveillance  designs  perform  under  uncertainty 
 about  pathogen  properties,  including  stylised  AI-enabled  threat  scenarios,  and  what 
 they  are  likely  to  cost  over  the  next  decade.  The  framework  produces  concrete 
 outputs  that  are  directly  interpretable  by  policymakers  and  technical  planners,  such 
 as  recommended  sequencing  depths  for  given  detection  targets,  credible  intervals 
 for  required  depths  under  relative  abundance  uncertainty,  and  projected  annual 
 costs  for  sustaining  those  depths  across  a  UK-wide  sentinel  network.  It  is  not 
 intended  as  a  complete  biosurveillance  blueprint:  important  dimensions  such  as 
 detailed  sewer-network  hydrology,  operational  logistics  and  integration  with 
 clinical  or  air-based  surveillance  are  treated  only  implicitly  or  left  for  future  work. 
 However,  by  quantifying  the  trade-offs  between  coverage,  depth,  detection 
 probability  and  cost  in  a  transparent  way,  the  project  offers  a  practical  starting 
 point  for  designing  wastewater  metagenomic  systems  that  remain  robust  in  the  face 
 of AI-accelerated biological threats. 
 2.  Methods 
 2.1 Overview 
 The  aim  of  this  work  was  to  quantify  how  well  wastewater  metagenomic 
 surveillance  could  detect  an  emerging  respiratory  virus,  including  “unknown”  or 
 deliberately  engineered  agents,  and  what  sequencing  depths  and  budgets  would  be 
 required for robust performance. 
 The analysis proceeds in three steps:

 1.  Viral relative abundance priors.  We construct probability distributions for 
 the relative abundance (RA) of human-infecting viruses in wastewater at a 
 ﬁxed incidence of 1% in the upstream population, using published 
 untargeted wastewater metagenomic datasets and the hierarchical model of 
 Grimm et al. (2025). 
 2.  Detection and design.  Using a binomial detection model with logit-normal 
 overdispersion, we translate RA priors into detection probabilities as a 
 function of sequencing depth and derive “minimum depth” requirements for 
 achieving speciﬁed detection targets under both typical and conservative 
 (robust) assumptions. 
 3.  Costs and AI-enabled scenarios.  We link sequencing depth to ﬁnancial cost 
 using a simple cost model calibrated to current UK prices and published 
 sequencing cost trajectories, and examine both baseline and “stealth” 
 scenarios representing AI-assisted low-detectability pathogens. 
 All  analyses  were  performed  in  Python  using  reproducible  scripts;  code  and 
 pre-computed  outputs  are  designed  so  that  heavy  Monte  Carlo  calculations  only 
 need to be run once and can then be explored interactively. 
 2.2 Data sources and viral relative abundance priors 
 We  based  our  viral  relative  abundance  priors  on  the  combined  untargeted 
 wastewater  metagenomic  (W-MGS)  datasets  used  by  Grimm  et  al.  (2025),  which 
 link  observed  read  counts  for  human-infecting  viruses  to  independent  estimates  of 
 infection  incidence  in  the  upstream  population.  In  brief,  the  Grimm  model  speciﬁes 
 a hierarchical relationship between: 
 ●  Population incidence for each virus and study, 
 ●  Wastewater relative abundance (fraction of metagenomic reads mapping to 
 that virus), and 
 ●  Study- and virus-speciﬁc random effects capturing differences in sewershed 
 hydrology, sampling, concentration, extraction and sequencing protocols. 
 Using  the  posterior  samples  provided  by  Grimm  et  al.  (2025),  we  constructed 
 empirical  priors  for  the  relative  abundance  at  1%  incidence  (  )  for  three  broad  𝑅  𝐴  1  𝑝𝑐𝑡

 2.6 AI-enabled “stealth” pathogen scenario 
 To  approximate  an  adversarial,  AI-enabled  pathogen  design  focused  on  reducing 
 detectability,  we  consider  a  stealth  scenario  in  which  the  novel  virus  has  a 
 deliberately low relative abundance in wastewater. 
 Operationally,  we  deﬁne  the  stealth  as  the  10th  percentile  of  the  unknown  𝑅  𝐴  1  𝑝𝑐𝑡 
 mixture  prior.  This  corresponds  to  a  pathogen  that  lies  near  the  low-abundance  tail

 All  analyses  were  implemented  in  Python  and  are  available  at:

 were run using vectorised code and ﬁxed random seeds for reproducibility. 
 The workﬂow is structured in two layers: 
 1.  Heavy pre-computation. Ofﬂine scripts load the Grimm posterior samples,

 2.  Lightweight exploration. A separate interactive demo (Streamlit

 This  separation  ensures  that  the  main  results  and  ﬁgures  can  be  regenerated

 3.6 Summary of key ﬁndings 
 Overall, the results indicate that: 
 Apart Research Hackathon Submission  20 


===== page 21 =====

 ●  Wastewater viral abundance at 1% incidence is highly heterogeneous across 
 and within viral classes, spanning 4–6 orders of magnitude per class and 
 roughly 9 orders of magnitude for the unknown mixture. An “unknown 
 pathogen” is therefore best modelled by a broad mixture prior rather than a 
 single typical value. 
 ●  Achieving robust early detection for unknown agents requires sequencing 
 depths substantially above those suggested by median-case analyses, 
 especially when targeting high weekly detection probabilities. For the 
 unknown mixture, moving from median-case to 95th-percentile robustness 
 multiplies required depth by ≈3×10². 
 ●  The additional cost of robustness is very large at current sequencing prices 
 (billions of pounds annually for a 200-site UK network) but decreases 
 materially under plausible future cost trajectories, making conservative 
 designs progressively more affordable. 
 ●  Simple stealth-like modiﬁcations to viral behaviour (approximated by 
 choosing a low-percentile RA from the mixture prior) can render 
 metagenomic wastewater surveillance effectively blind within realistic depth 
 ranges. Designs tuned only to median abundance can therefore fail badly in 
 adversarial scenarios, whereas robust-depth designs substantially reduce 
 but do not fully eliminate this risk. 
 Taken  together,  these  ﬁndings  suggest  that  metagenomic  wastewater  surveillance 
 can  meaningfully  contribute  to  defence  against  AI-enabled  viral  threats,  provided 
 that  sequencing  depths  and  budgets  are  chosen  with  explicit  attention  to  RA 
 uncertainty.  However,  highly  adversarial  scenarios  do  not  appear  to  be 
 appropriately covered by this protocol and so require further analysis. 
 4.  Discussion and Conclusion 
 This  work  quantiﬁes  how  far  metagenomic  wastewater  surveillance  can  be  pushed 
 as  a  defence  against  emerging  and  potentially  AI-enabled  respiratory  viruses. 
 Using  posterior  draws  from  Grimm  et  al.  (2025)  and  a  simple 
 epidemic/surveillance  model,  we  show  that  viral  relative  abundance  at  1  % 
 incidence  spans  many  orders  of  magnitude  even  within  a  single  class.  As  a  result, 
 the  same  sequencing  depth  can  reliably  detect  high-abundance  enteric  viruses  while 
 providing  only  weak  protection  against  typical  respiratory  viruses  and  almost  none 
 against  low-abundance  DNA-like  or  deliberately  “stealthy”  threats.  The 
 unknown-pathogen  mixture  prior  makes  this  heterogeneity  explicit:  at  any  ﬁxed 
 depth  the  weekly  detection  probability  varies  from  essentially  zero  to  near  one 
 across plausible  draws.  𝑅  𝐴  1  𝑝𝑐𝑡 
 The  detection  and  cost  curves  imply  that  there  is  no  cheap  route  to  robust 
 wastewater  early  warning.  For  a  UK-scale  network,  achieving  90  %  weekly 
 detection  against  a  median  unknown  pathogen  requires  tens  of  millions  of  reads  per 
 sample  but  remains  vulnerable  to  low-abundance  designs;  pushing  to  depths  that 
 cover  90–95  %  of  the  RA  prior  multiplies  sequencing  costs  by  an  order  of

 magnitude  while  still  leaving  residual  blind  spots.  Multi-week  sampling 
 substantially  improves  cumulative  detection  once  weekly  sensitivity  passes  ~20–30 
 %,  but  cannot  compensate  for  pathogens  whose  wastewater  signal  remains  below 
 the detection threshold even at very high depths. 
 These  results  should  not  be  interpreted  as  a  verdict  against  wastewater 
 surveillance.  Rather,  they  underscore  that  metagenomic  WBE  is  a  powerful  but 
 limited  component  of  a  broader  defensive  stack.  Its  strengths  are  breadth  of 
 coverage  and  the  ability  to  detect  “unknown  unknowns”  without  target-speciﬁc 
 assays;  its  weaknesses  are  the  dependence  on  poorly  understood  shedding  and 
 transport  processes,  the  cost  of  very  deep  sequencing,  and  a  structural  vulnerability 
 to  low-abundance  or  “off-sewer”  attack  designs.  The  modelling  here  abstracts  away 
 from  many  real-world  complexities,  including  detailed  sewer  network  structure, 
 joint  optimisation  with  clinical  and  environmental  sensors,  and  adaptive  adversaries 
 using  AI  systems  to  search  for  subtle  evasion  strategies.  These  are  important 
 directions  for  future  work,  but  they  are  unlikely  to  reverse  the  basic 
 cost–robustness trade-offs we identify. 
 This  analysis  suggests  that  metagenomic  wastewater  surveillance  can  materially 
 reduce  the  time  to  detection  for  many  high-abundance  respiratory  threats,  including 
 some  plausible  AI-enabled  pathogens,  provided  that  sequencing  depths  and  network 
 scales  are  chosen  with  explicit  attention  to  variability  in  wastewater  viral 
 abundance.  At  the  same  time,  the  large  gap  between  the  depths  needed  for 
 “typical”  and  “robust”  detection,  and  the  persistence  of  blind  spots  for 
 low-abundance  or  non-sewered  releases,  mean  that  wastewater  data  alone  cannot 
 be  relied  upon  as  a  universal  early-warning  system.  Effective  biosecurity  policy  will 
 require  combining  such  surveillance  with  targeted  clinical  testing,  environmental 
 sensing,  and  analytics  tuned  to  adversarial  threat  models,  alongside  governance 
 that  constrains  the  most  dangerous  uses  of  AI  in  biological  design.  This  work  offers 
 a  transparent,  reproducible  framework  for  stress-testing  candidate  wastewater 
 networks  against  those  emerging  risks  and  for  making  the  associated  trade-offs 
 explicit.

 Appendix A: Security considerations 
 A1. Scope and intended use 
 This project is intended as a  defensive  contribution to biosecurity. 
 It asks: 
 ●  How sensitive could a realistically funded wastewater metagenomic network 
 be to emerging respiratory viruses, including unknown or engineered 
 agents? 
 Apart Research Hackathon Submission  23 

===== page 24 =====

 ●  What sequencing depths and budgets would be required to reach speciﬁed 
 detection targets? 
 ●  How does performance degrade for low-abundance “stealth” pathogens that 
 might plausibly be designed with the help of AI tools? 
 The work focuses on surveillance system design, not on biological agent design. 
 All  modelling  operates  at  the  level  of  relative  abundance  distributions  and  detection 
 probabilities, not viral genomes, constructs, or protocols. 
 A2. Biosecurity beneﬁts 
 From a security standpoint, the main value of the work is to: 
 1.  Quantify detection blind spots. 
 ○  The analysis identiﬁes regions of parameter space (e.g. low 
 wastewater relative abundance, low shedding into sewage) where 
 even very deep sequencing performs poorly. 
 ○  This helps planners understand which threat models are realistically 
 covered by wastewater metagenomics and which are not. 
 2.  Clarify trade-offs between robustness and cost. 
 ○  The results show how rapidly cost escalates as one moves from 
 “typical unknown virus” performance to “robust against 
 low-abundance variants.” 
 ○  This supports more realistic budgeting and prioritisation of 
 surveillance investments, rather than assuming “more sequencing 
 solves everything.” 
 3.  Stress-test AI-enabled threat scenarios. 
 ○  By embedding an explicit “stealth” scenario, the project gives a 
 concrete way to discuss how AI-assisted design might erode 
 detection and how much additional resource would be needed to 
 claw back performance. 
 4.  Support high-level policy questions. 
 ○  The framework helps answer questions like: 
 ■  Is metagenomic wastewater surveillance alone a sufﬁcient 
 backbone for early warning? 
 Apart Research Hackathon Submission  24 

===== page 25 =====

 ■  Under what conditions is it cost-effective compared with 
 other modalities (clinical, air sampling, etc.)? 
 The  intent  is  to  inform  defensive  strategy,  not  to  prescribe  speciﬁc  operational 
 parameters for any particular national system. 
 A3. Limitations and uncertainties 
 The work has several important limitations that matter for security interpretation: 
 1.  Coarse threat model. 
 ○  Pathogens are represented via relative abundance at 1% incidence 
 and simple shedding into wastewater; within-host dynamics, 
 environmental decay pathways, and behavioural heterogeneity are 
 not explicitly modelled. 
 ○  Sophisticated adversaries might exploit features not captured here 
 (e.g. clustered shedding, time-varying shedding, targeted seeding 
 patterns). 
 2.  Simpliﬁed network structure. 
 ○  The model treats wastewater sites as independent sentinel 
 catchments, without detailed sewer network topology, mixing, or 
 travel patterns. 
 ○  Spatial seeding strategies, targeted attacks on unmonitored regions, 
 or exploitation of atypical sewersheds (e.g. small, industrial, or 
 highly transient populations) are therefore not fully addressed. 
 3.  Uncertain RA priors. 
 ○  distributions are derived from a limited set of historical  𝑅  𝐴  1  𝑝𝑐𝑡 
 datasets and laboratory protocols. 
 ○  They may not generalise to novel technologies, different 
 geographies, or radically different virus families. 
 ○  In particular, the “unknown” mixture prior is an informed but 
 subjective construct. 
 4.  Simpliﬁed detection rule. 
 ○  A ﬁxed threshold of 100 reads is used as a proxy for “detectable and 
 credible to analysts.” 
 Apart Research Hackathon Submission  25 

===== page 26 =====

 ○  Real systems use more complex pipelines (alignment, ﬁltering, 
 background models, anomaly detection). 
 ○  The estimates here are therefore conservative and approximate, not 
 a substitute for end-to-end pipeline evaluation. 
 5.  Cost model uncertainty. 
 ○  Costs are based on current UK-style workﬂows and forecasting 
 assumptions about sequencing price declines. 
 ○  Operational costs, stafﬁng, QC, and information-system expenses 
 are simpliﬁed or omitted; real programmes may be substantially 
 more expensive. 
 6.  No adversarial game dynamics. 
 ○  The work evaluates static surveillance designs against static 
 attacker assumptions. 
 ○  It does not model iterated attacker–defender adaptation, nor 
 coordinated multi-modal detection (wastewater + clinical + 
 environmental). 
 These  limitations  should  be  kept  in  mind  when  interpreting  the  results:  the  model  is 
 best seen as an intuitive proof of concept for a more complex design. 
 A4. Dual-use and misuse potential 
 Although this work is defensive in intent, elements of it are technically dual-use: 
 1.  Quantifying detection thresholds. 
 ○  The model clariﬁes what combinations of relative abundance and 
 sequencing depth lead to low detection probabilities. 
 ○  In principle, an adversary could use this to reason about how far 
 they might need to push a pathogen’s wastewater shedding 
 downward to evade detection. 
 2.  Stealth scenario framing. 
 ○  The explicit “stealth” scenario (10th percentile RA of the unknown 
 prior) highlights that low-abundance designs are more likely to slip 
 through. 
 Apart Research Hackathon Submission  26 

===== page 27 =====

 ○  If combined with separate, more detailed design tools, this could 
 inform adversarial optimisation. 
 3.  Cost–robustness trade-offs. 
 ○  Showing how expensive robust coverage is could be misused 
 rhetorically to argue against investment in wastewater surveillance 
 entirely, rather than to motivate complementary defences. 
 4.  AI linkage. 
 ○  The project contextually discusses AI tools lowering barriers to 
 designing novel pathogens or planning release strategies. 
 ○  While no concrete design or protocol information is provided, any 
 discussion of AI-enabled threat models risks being cited in offensive 
 framing. 
 At  the  same  time,  the  actual  model  details  are  quite  far  from  something  a  plausible 
 attacker would ﬁnd practically useful: 
 ●  There is no genome-level information, no optimisation over speciﬁc 
 constructs, and no lab protocol content. 
 ●  The RA priors and detection curves are highly aggregated and uncertain; 
 an attacker attempting to ﬁne-tune a real design from these numbers would 
 likely be misled. 
 ●  Truly capable adversaries would almost certainly prefer their own 
 experimental measurements or bespoke simulations. 
 Although  the  dual-use  angle  is  real,  the  ﬁndings  here  provide  information  which 
 support  unsurprising  intuitions:  rare  viruses  are  hard  to  detect,  and  more  robust 
 detection incurs more costs. 
 A5. Future improvements and research directions 
 Several  future  directions  could  improve  the  defensive  usefulness  of  this  work  while 
 staying within safe bounds. 
 1.  Richer multi-modal surveillance models. 
 ○  Extend the framework to jointly consider indoor air sampling, 
 clinical diagnostics, and possibly syndromic data alongside 
 wastewater. 
 ○  This would better reﬂect realistic detection architectures and reduce 
 the temptation to over-interpret wastewater as a single point of 
 Apart Research Hackathon Submission  27 

===== page 28 =====

 failure. 
 2.  More realistic sewershed and network structure. 
 ○  Incorporate explicit sewer network topology, travel patterns, and 
 realistic catchment sizes to identify which nodes are most critical as 
 sentinels. 
 3.  Adversarially robust design explorations. 
 ○  Explore how much robustness can be gained from combining 
 metagenomics with algorithmic anomaly detection, targeted 
 enrichment, or adaptive sampling strategies. 
 4.  More granular uncertainty treatment. 
 ○  Decompose uncertainty by source (biological, environmental, 
 methodological) to see which components most undermine 
 robustness. 
 ○  This can guide where additional measurement or standardisation 
 would have the biggest defensive payoff. 
 5.  Governance and policy integration. 
 ○  Link design outputs to concrete governance questions: 
 ■  At what cost thresholds should international or national 
 funding mechanisms trigger? 
 Overall,  this  project  should  be  viewed  as  a  defensive  decision-support  tool:  it 
 quantiﬁes  what  wastewater  metagenomic  surveillance  can  and  cannot  do  in  the  face 
 of  emerging  and  potentially  AI-enabled  viral  threats.  Used  appropriately,  it  can 
 help  steer  investments  toward  more  robust  early-warning  architectures.  Misused  in 
 isolation,  it  offers  at  most  a  very  rough  sketch  of  surveillance  blind  spots,  and  it  is 
 intentionally designed not to provide actionable offensive guidance.

 B.6.1 Per-sample, per-site, and network costs 
 For a given year y and depth n, the per-sample cost is: 
 cost_per_sample_y(n) = processing_cost + c_read_y * n

 ●  processing_cost is a ﬁxed amount covering collection, concentration,

 ●  c_read_y is the sequencing cost per read in year y (GBP per read). 
 The annual cost per site with weekly sampling is: 
 cost_annual_per_site_y(n) = 52 * cost_per_sample_y(n) 
 Given a network of N_sites sentinel sites, the annual network cost is:
