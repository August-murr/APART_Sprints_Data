Abstract  
This  paper  develops  a  stylized  game-theoretic  model  of  cross-border  AI  training  projects  in  which  multiple  states  jointly  train  frontier  models  while  retaining  national  control  over  compute  resources.  We  focus  on  decentralized  coordination  regimes,  where  actors  publicly  pledge  compute  contributions  but  privately  choose  actual  delivery,  creating  incentives  to  free-ride  on  a  shared  public  good.  To  address  this,  the  model  introduces  explicit  veriﬁcation  mechanisms,  represented  as  a  continuous  monitoring  intensity  that  improves  the  precision  of  noisy  signals  about  each  actor's  true  compute  contribution.  Actors  choose  compute  provision  strategically,  balancing  the  beneﬁts  of  higher  system  capability  against  compute  costs  and  the  risk  of  detection  and  punishment.  Simulation  results  reveal  several  governance-relevant  ﬁndings:  (1)  veriﬁcation  effectively  deters  free-riding,  with  cheating  decreasing  smoothly  as  monitoring  intensity  increases;  (2)  stronger  veriﬁcation  accelerates  collective  capability  development  by  approximately  27%;  (3)  a  non-monotonic  "veriﬁcation  valley"  exists  where  intermediate  monitoring  levels  produce  worse  welfare  outcomes  than  either  minimal  or  maximal  veriﬁcation;  (4)  veriﬁcation  precision  (measurement  noise)  is  a  more  inﬂuential  policy  lever  than  penalty  magnitude  for  controlling  cheating  behavior;  and  (5)  veriﬁcation  regimes  create  distributional  consequences,  with  smaller  actors  beneﬁting  while  larger  actors  bear  net  costs.  These  ﬁndings  suggest  that  policymakers  designing  international  AI  governance  institutions  face  a  commitment  problem:  half-measures  in  veriﬁcation  are  counterproductive,  and  effective  regimes  require  either  accepting  some  free-riding  or  investing  substantially  in  monitoring  infrastructure.

2.  Related  Work  
A  small  but  growing  body  of  research  applies  game  theory  to  core  problems  in  AI  governance.  A  Game-Theoretic  Framework  for  AI  Governance models  interactions  between  regulators  and  AI  developers  as  a  Stackelberg  game,  highlighting  how  commitment  power  and  enforcement  shape  equilibrium  compliance.  Complementing  this  perspective,  a  recent  RAND  report,  Strategies  and  Detection  Gaps  in  a  Game-Theoretic  Model  of  Compute  Governance,  studies  how  strategic  behavior  and  limited  observability  affect  the  effectiveness  of  compute-based  monitoring  regimes.  Earlier  work by  Armstrong,  Bostrom,  and  Shulman  develops  a  stylized  game-theoretic  model  of  an  AI  arms  race,  showing  how  competitive  pressure  to  be  ﬁrst  can  incentivize  risk  taking  and  
underinvestment
 
in
 
safety.
 
More  broadly,  compute  governance  is  widely  viewed as  a  central  lever  for  technical  AI  governance,  motivated  by  the  empirical  link  between  training  compute  and  frontier  capabilities  and  by  the  relative  observability  of  large-scale  compute  use.  This  perspective  underlies  a  range  of  proposals  for  thresholds,  reporting  requirements,  and  monitoring  regimes  tied  to  training  runs.  In  parallel,  several  authors  have  proposed international  institutions  and  cooperative  mechanisms  to  manage  cross-border  externalities  and  stabilize  race  dynamics.  However,  much  of  this  literature  remains
