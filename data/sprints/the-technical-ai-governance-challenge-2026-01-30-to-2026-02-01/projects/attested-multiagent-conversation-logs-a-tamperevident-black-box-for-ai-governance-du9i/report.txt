Abstract  
As  multi-agent  AI  systems  assume  high-stakes  responsibilities,  governance  frameworks  such  as  the  EU  AI  Act  demand  traceable,  auditable  records  of  agent  interactions.  We  introduce  Attested  Logs,  an  open-source  Python  library  that  serves  as  a  tamper-evident  "black  box"  for  AI  conversations.  Each  message  is  cryptographically  signed  (Ed25519),  hash-chained  (SHA-256),  and  anchored  to  trusted  public  keys,  enabling  fully  ofﬂine  veriﬁcation  of  integrity,  authenticity,  and  ordering.  Inspired  by  aviation  ﬂight  recorders  and  C2PA  content  provenance,  the  library  supports  incident  forensics,  regulatory  compliance,  and  cross-organizational  trust.  Integrations  for  AutoGen  (manual  logging)  and  LangGraph  (callback-based  auditing)  are  provided,  together  with  runnable  demos  demonstrating  end-to-end  signing,  veriﬁcation,  and  tamper  detection  in  real  LLM  conversations,  and  20+  passing  tests  covering  core,  crypto,  chain,  and  veriﬁcation  layers.  
 
1  Research  conducted  at  the  Technical  AI  Governance  Challenge,  2026

5.
 
Discussion
 
and
 
Limitations
 
The  choice  of  JSON-based  proofs  (following  W3C  Data  Integrity  patterns)  over  X.509  certiﬁcates  
favors
 
simplicity
 
for
 
closed
 
organizational
 
deployments
 
but
 
limits
 
interoperability
 
with
 
existing
 
PKI
 
infrastructure.
 
Cross-organizational
 
scenarios
 
would
 
beneﬁt
 
from
 
bridging
 
to
 
established
 
PKI
 
or
 
decentralized
 
identiﬁers
 
(DIDs).
 
Limitations  &  future  work  
1.  No  external  timestamping  /  anchoring:  The  design  provides  strong  local  tamper-evidence  
through
 
signatures
 
and
 
linear
 
hash
 
chaining,
 
but
 
does
 
not
 
anchor
 
the
 
chain
 
to
 
an
 
external,
 
widely
 
witnessed
 
structure
 
(e.g.,
 
blockchain
 
or
 
public
 
timestamping
 
service).
 
An
 
attacker
 
who
 
compromises
 
the
 
local
 
system
 
before
 
log
 
creation
 
could
 
forge
 
an
 
entire
 
chain
 
from
 
scratch.
 
Anchoring
 
periodic
 
Merkle
 
roots
 
to
 
Bitcoin/Ethereum
 
or
 
OpenTimestamps
 
would
 
signiﬁcantly
 
raise
 
the
 
bar
 
for
 
such
 
attacks,
 
but
 
this
 
is
 
not
 
implemented
 
in
 
the
 
current
 
version.
 2.  Centralized  key  trust  model:  Veriﬁcation  relies  on  a  trusted  set  of  public  keys  provided  at  
veriﬁcation
 
time.
 
No
 
built-in
 
mechanism
 
exists
 
to
 
prove
 
key
 
ownership
 
over
 
time
 
(e.g.,
 
certiﬁcates,
 
key
 
transparency
 
logs).
 
In
 
adversarial
 
settings,
 
a
 
malicious
 
veriﬁer
 
could
 
be
 
given
 
forged
 
keys.
 3.  No  selective  disclosure:  The  full  chain  must  be  presented  to  verify  any  part.  In  
legal/compliance
 
scenarios,
 
revealing
 
only
 
relevant
 
chunks
 
(without
 
exposing
 
unrelated
 
conversation
 
content)
 
is
 
desirable.
 
The
 
current
 
design
 
lacks
 
Merkle
 
inclusion
 
proofs
 
or
 
zero-knowledge
 
range
 
proofs
 
to
 
support
 
this.
 4.  Performance  for  very  long  conversations:  Veriﬁcation  is  O(n)  in  chain  length.  For  
thousands
 
of
 
messages,
 
this
 
becomes
 
impractical
 
without
 
Merkle
 
checkpointing
 
or
 
batch
 
veriﬁcation
 
mechanisms.
 
The  current  lightweight,  ofﬂine-ﬁrst  design  prioritizes  immediate  usability  while  leaving  clear,  
incremental
 
paths
 
for
 
stronger
 
assurance
 
in
 
future
 
iterations.
