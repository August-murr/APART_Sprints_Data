Abstract
Compute thresholds are widely discussed as a practical governance tool, but the literature
rarely specifies how quickly fixed thresholds become outdated as algorithmic efficiency improves.
We develop a compute-threshold staleness model that distinguishes raw training compute from
baseline-equivalent (“effective”) compute and defines staleness as the ratio between a policy
threshold and the time-varying threshold that would remain aligned with a fixed risk cutoff. Under
exponential efficiency gains with doubling timeτ and threshold updates everyU months, worst-
case staleness grows asSmax = 2U/τ, implying that modest uncertainty aboutτ can translate into
large differences in required update cadence. Using a representative frontier estimate ofτ≈ 8
months suggests that annual recalibration can allow thresholds to drift by roughly2.8× within
one year. We ground plausible values ofτ in prior estimates of algorithmic progress and provide
a small historical backtest as a sanity check.
1 Introduction
Compute thresholds are widely used in regulatory frameworks for AI models (Pistillo et al., 2025),
as they correlate roughly with risk and are relatively easy to predict ahead of time. The literature
also observes that compute thresholds will need to be shifted (ArXiv discussion, 2024). How much,
how frequently, and in what direction lacks any clear consensus. This paper models the impact of
algorithmic progress on how quickly thresholds become stale, an important consideration especially
for handling CBRN risks, which unlike loss-of-control risks are not clustered at the frontier.
Our main contributions are:
1. A simple mathematical model applying the observed pattern of exponential growth in algorithmic
efficiency to questions of staleness of compute thresholds
2. A dashboard to model the relationship between the update frequency, the doubling time of
algorithmic efficiency, and the staleness of compute thresholds

2 Related Work
Compute thresholds in AI regulation
Training Compute Thresholds: Features and Functions in AI Regulation.The analysis
of compute thresholds in this paper is heavily indebted to Heim and Koessler’s discussion (Heim
and Koessler, 2024). Their work is primarily qualitative, and we use it mainly to motivate why
thresholds are appealing as an initial filter and why update mechanisms matter. In particular,
they discuss the suitability of compute thresholds for an initial filter, the need for updates, and
tradeoffs in both conservative and aggressive thresholds, as well as how compute thresholds are used
in existing policy (principally the EU AI Act).
Algorithmic progress and efficiency trends
Algorithmic progress in language models.This paper provides an analysis and rough estimate
of the role of algorithmic progress in making training more efficient to reach a given capability level
(Ho et al., 2024). We use their empirical estimates to motivate plausible values ofτ (an “effective
compute” doubling time) in our staleness model; as shown in Section 4, the implications are highly
sensitive toτ.
Positioning and gap
Despite the existence of extensive work on algorithmic progress, and the adoption of compute
thresholds with the acknowledgment of a need to update them, there is little work on modeling the
relationship of update frequency and algorithmic progress to the degree with which a model falls
out of date. This paper attempts to close that gap with a simple model which can provide a good
intuitive grasp of the problem for policy-makers.

4.1 Model Sensitivity to Key Parameters
The staleness of a compute threshold is determined by the ratioU/τ, whereU is the policy update
interval (in months) andτ is the algorithmic efficiency doubling time (in months). The maximum
staleness just before a policy update is given by:
Smax = 2U/τ (11)
This relationship is highly sensitive toτ. For example, with an annual update cycle (U = 12
months):
•Atτ= 8months (frontier estimate from Ho et al. (2024)):S max = 21.5≈2.8×
•Atτ= 4months:S max = 23 = 8×
•Atτ= 16months:S max = 20.75≈1.7×
The update interval required to maintain a target maximum stalenessStarget is:
U=τ·log 2(Starget)(12)
4.2 Implications of 8-Month Doubling Time
Using the Ho et al. (2024) estimate ofτ = 8months for frontier algorithmic efficiency improvements:
•The ideal compute threshold halves every 8 months
• A threshold set today will be approximately2.8× too permissive after one year without
updates
•After two years, a static threshold becomes8×too permissive
•To maintain staleness≤2×, updates are needed everyU= 8·log 2(2) = 8months
These results suggest that annual update cycles—common in regulatory contexts—may be insufficient
to maintain threshold calibration if algorithmic progress continues at frontier rates.

4.3 Historical Validation
A backtest against historical AI model releases provides a rough sanity check of theτ parameter.
We compare capability-matching model pairs—cases where a later, smaller model achieved similar
benchmark performance as an earlier, larger model with less training compute.
Data Sources.Training compute estimates are sourced from Epoch AI’s Notable AI Models
dataset (Epoch AI, 2024) where available, supplemented by the standard scaling approximation
C≈ 6ND (whereN is parameter count andD is training tokens) for models with disclosed training
configurations (Kaplan et al., 2020). This approximation is coarse and depends on training details
that are not always publicly reported. Release dates are taken from official announcements or first
public availability.
Capability Matching.We identify pairs where the matching model useslesscompute than
the reference while achieving comparable scores on specific benchmarks (e.g., HellaSwag, MMLU).
Pairs where the later model used more compute are excluded, as these test data efficiency rather
than algorithmic efficiency. The “comparable capability” judgment is approximate and benchmark-
specific—a significant limitation of this validation approach.

5 Discussion and Limitations
These results indicate that, to keep compute thresholds well-calibrated, the update cadence should
scale with the doubling time of algorithmic efficiency. However,τ is uncertain and may differ across
regimes (e.g., frontier vs catch-up), making calibration difficult. This uncertainty is especially
salient as models cross capability levels associated with significant CBRN risk. Where loss-of-
control concerns may cluster near the frontier (which is hard to define but relatively easy to track),
CBRN-relevant capabilities may not be confined to the frontier.
This suggests that improving estimates of algorithmic progress (i.e., ofτ) is important for selecting
an appropriate update cadence. Finally, if efficiency gains slow over time (e.g., a sigmoidal trajectory
due to physical limits), threshold drift may eventually become less severe.
Limitations
This model is highly idealized and cannot give detailed prescriptions. For example, capabilities do
not necessarily improve at the same rate across domains as algorithmic efficiency changes. The
model also ignores error in setting new thresholds, which could be significant in practice. If the
rate of algorithmic progress is continually underestimated (e.g., in a pause world where most or all
algorithmic research is covert), then staleness could grow substantially.
More fundamentally, effective compute is a modeling construct rather than an observable quantity.
There may be no single scalar that captures the capabilities we care about across domains and
threat models. As a result, calibration will necessarily require judgment beyond estimating a single
doubling time τ—including choices about which benchmarks, evaluations, and risk cutoffs the
threshold is intended to track.

Future Work
This model could be expanded to consider other parameters, though high uncertainty makes detailed
modeling difficult and somewhat prone to false precision risks. The biggest suggestion is that
accurate research on algorithmic progress may be necessary to make compute thresholds workable;
absent that, compute thresholds may not be reliable for identifying models likely to pose substantial
risks.
6 Conclusion
This project formalizes a simple model for the degree to which a compute threshold would be
expected to be out of date as a result of improvements in algorithmic efficiency. Applying the
currently observed model of exponential growth of algorithmic efficiency, the model predicts that
compute thresholds have worst-case stalenessSmax = 2U/τ. A small historical backtest suggests the
model’s predictions are consistent with an 8–10 month regime, in line with Ho et al. (2024).
Applying a representative frontier estimate ofτ≈ 8months implies that annual recalibration can
allow thresholds to drift by roughly2.8× within a year, suggesting that annual updates may leave
thresholds meaningfully miscalibrated for a substantial portion of the year. This model provides a
compact way to reason about the relationship between thresholds and observed trends in algorithmic
efficiency, with potential uses in policy planning and communication, though it is a highly ideal
model and should not be used in place of empirical observation.
