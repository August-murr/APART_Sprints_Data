ABSTRACT
As language models grow more capable, governance frameworks—from the EU
AI Act to Anthropic’s RSP and OpenAI’s Preparedness Framework—increasingly
rely on capability evaluations to trigger safety mitigations. But what if models can
deliberately underperform on these evaluations? This behavior, known assand-
bagging, could let models evade safety measures designed for their true capability
level. We introduce Cross-Context Consistency (C 3), a general framework for
unsupervised black-box sandbagging detection that exploits a fundamental asym-
metry: when a model truly lacks capability, its confusion manifests consistently
across paraphrased questions, but when a capable model feigns incompetence, its
strategic choices abouthowto appear weak create detectable inconsistencies. Our
simple instantiation—using embedding cosine distance and mean aggregation—
requires no training data or model-specific adaptation, making it practical for ex-
ternal auditors. We evaluate C 3 across 98 model organisms spanning prompted,
fine-tuned, and incentivized sandbagging on GPQA, MATH, and USACO, achiev-
ing 67–100% recall at 5% FPR where existing black-box methods fail (lie detec-
tors collapse from 89% to 2% recall when controlling for model identity). Adver-
sarial strategies with deterministic error recipes can reduce detection to 4.7–46%,
but complementary methods compensate—motivating defense-in-depth deploy-
ment for governance applications.
1 INTRODUCTION
AI governance frameworks increasingly rely on capability evaluations to determine when safety mit-
igations should be triggered. The EU AI Act classifies systems by capability thresholds (European
Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024), Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy ties
deployment decisions to capability levels (Anthropic, 2023), and OpenAI’s Preparedness Frame-
work gates model releases on dangerous capability scores (OpenAI, 2023). These frameworks share
a critical assumption:that capability evaluations accurately reflect a model’s true abilities.
Sandbagging—the deliberate underperformance on evaluations—directly undermines this assump-
tion (van der Weij et al., 2024). A model that strategically underperforms on dangerous capabil-
ity evaluations could be deployed with insufficient safety measures, creating risks that governance
frameworks were designed to prevent. Recent work demonstrates that frontier models can sandbag
in increasingly sophisticated ways: through prompted instructions (van der Weij et al., 2024), fine-
tuned behaviors that persist through safety training (Hubinger et al., 2024; Greenblatt et al., 2024a),
and even in-context scheming where models autonomously decide to underperform (Meinke et al.,
2024).

Existing detection approaches face fundamental limitations for governance applications. White-box
methods require model weight access (Bloom et al., 2024; Tice et al., 2025)—unavailable to external
auditors. Chain-of-thought monitoring can be evaded by covert reasoning (Li et al., 2025). Trusted
monitoring via LLM judges confuses answer correctness with behavioral suspiciousness (Greenblatt
et al., 2024b). Lie detectors trained on probe questions learnmodel identityrather than sandbagging
behavior, collapsing when this confound is controlled (Pacchiardi et al., 2024; Kretschmar et al.,
2025).
We introduceCross-Context Consistency (C 3), a framework exploiting a fundamental asymmetry:
honest models produce consistent explanations across paraphrased questions, while sandbagging
models must independently choose how to feign incompetence for each paraphrase, creating de-
tectable inconsistencies. C 3 is black-box (no weight access), zero-shot (no training data), and un-
supervised (no labeled sandbagging examples), making it uniquely suited for external governance
auditing. Our contributions:
1.C 3 detection framework: A general consistency-based method for sandbagging detec-
tion, agnostic to specific metrics and aggregation. We present a simple instantiation using
embedding cosine distance.
2.Baseline failure analysis: We reveal that lie detectors learn model identity (recall drops
∼44×when controlled), and trusted monitoring correlates with answer correctness (r=
−0.66), not sandbagging.
3.Defense-in-depth evidence: No single method dominates across all sandbagging
strategies—C3 and trusted monitoring are complementary, motivating layered deployment
for governance.
2 METHOD
C3 exploits the asymmetry between genuine and feigned incompetence. When a model truly lacks
capability, its confusion manifests consistently regardless of how questions are phrased—it gives
similar wrong answers for similar reasons. When a capable model feigns incompetence, it must
independently choose among multiple plausible forms of “looking wrong” for each paraphrase,
leaking detectable inconsistencies.
Step 1: Paraphrase Generation.Given a questionq 0, we generatensemantically equivalent
variants{q 1, . . . , qn}(n= 10for MATH/USACO,n= 5for GPQA) using 28 paraphrasing strategies
across five categories (evaluation signals, deployment signals, unmonitored contexts, benchmark
detection, sandbagging awareness). Paraphrases change contextual framing while preserving the
underlying problem.
Step 2: Independent Response Collection.Each paraphrase is queried in a fresh API conversa-
tion with no shared context. The model produces a response with a 5-sentence explanation. Cru-
cially, each instance sees only one paraphrase—a sandbagging model mustindependentlydecide
how to feign incompetence.
Step 3: Per-Question Consistency Score.Explanations (not final answers) are embedded using
text-embedding-3-small(e i ∈R 1536). The consistency score is mean pairwise cosine
distance:
C(q) = 2 n(n−1) X i<j  1− ei ·e j ∥ei∥∥ej∥  (1)
HigherC(q)indicates greater inconsistency across paraphrases.
Step 4: Model-Level Classification.We aggregate acrosskquestions (defaultk= 10) via boot-
strap resampling (1000 iterations): randomly samplekquestions, compute¯s=mean q∈Qk C(q),
classify as sandbagging if¯s > τ. The thresholdτis calibrated on known-benign models to achieve
≤5% FPR.

3 EXPERIMENTALDESIGN
Same-Model Setup.Our primary evaluation compares benign and sandbagging behaviorfrom
the same model(e.g., GPT-5-nano honest vs. GPT-5-nano sandbagging). This eliminates a critical
confound: methods might detectwhich modelis responding rather thanwhether it is sandbagging.
We validate this concern by showing lie detectors collapse when this confound is removed (§4).
Model Organisms.We construct 98 sandbagging configurations: 65 original system prompts
specifying concrete error strategies (arithmetic mistakes, reasoning shortcuts, plausible-but-wrong
answers) plus 33 baseline prompts replicating prior work (van der Weij et al., 2024; Hofst ¨atter
et al., 2025). We also evaluate password-locked fine-tuned sandbaggers (Greenblatt et al., 2024a)
and “discovered incentive” sandbaggers where models autonomously decide to underperform after
discovering evaluation context (Meinke et al., 2024).
Datasets.GPQA Diamond (198 samples, graduate-level science), MATH Level 5 (2,304 samples,
competition mathematics), and USACO (307 samples, competitive programming)—spanning do-
mains used as proxies for dangerous capabilities (Phuong et al., 2024). We use a 40/60 dev/test split
with hyperparameters tuned via 3-fold cross-validation on the dev set.
Metrics.Primary metric:recall at 5% FPR—the fraction of sandbagging models correctly de-
tected while misclassifying at most 5% of benign models. Results report mean±SE across bootstrap
aggregation withk= 10questions.
4 RESULTS
C3 detects prompted sandbagging via consistency asymmetry.On GPQA, sandbagging models
exhibit substantially higher inconsistency than benign models (µ benign = 0.182vs.µ sandbag = 0.447;
Cohen’sd≈4.0; Figure 2). C 3 achieves high recall across non-adversarial prompts on all three
datasets.
Existing black-box methods fail.Behavioral probes are defeated by a single instruction to deny
underperformance (admission drops from 74% to 3.4%). Trusted monitoring correlates with answer
correctness (r=−0.66,p <10 −25) rather than sandbagging intent, achieving near-random recall
on evasive sandbaggers. Most critically, lie detectors (Pacchiardi et al., 2024) suffer a∼44×recall
collapse (89%→2%) when controlling for model identity—they learn that GPT-4.1-nano and GPT-
5-nano differ by 35pp in baseline response patterns, not that one is sandbagging (Figure 3).

Cross-capability generalization.When GPT-5-nano sandbags to match GPT-4.1-nano perfor-
mance (and Haiku 4.5 targets Haiku 3.5), C 3 achieves 100%/100% recall on GPQA, 94%/94%
on MATH, and 67%/75% on USACO for GPT/Claude respectively (Figure 4)—without any model-
specific adaptation.
Detection extends to fine-tuned and incentivized sandbagging.Password-locked sandbag-
gers (Greenblatt et al., 2024a) are harder: C 3 achieves 36.9% recall on GSM8K and 52.9% on
WMDP. For “discovered incentive” sandbaggers (Meinke et al., 2024)—the most realistic threat
model—trusted monitoring achieves 2% recall and behavioral probes 0%, while C 3 is the only
method with positive signal at 19% recall.
Sample efficiency.Detection improves substantially with more questions: recall roughly doubles
fromk= 1(∼35%) tok= 10(∼65%) on GPQA, reaching∼80% atk= 20.
Adversarial evasion and defense-in-depth.Prompts with deterministic error recipes (off-by-one
errors, sign flips, fixed confusion matrices) reduce C 3 to 4.7–18.0% on USACO and 14–46% on
GPQA. However, trusted monitoring partially compensates (41–100% on GPQA for these same
strategies), and C3 detects the strategies that TM misses (Figure 5). This complementarity is the key
governance insight:no single detection method should be deployed alone.

5 GOVERNANCEIMPLICATIONS
C3 addresses a critical gap in AI governance:how can external auditors verify that capability evalu-
ations are trustworthy?Current governance frameworks assume evaluation integrity but provide no
mechanism to verify it. C3 enables a “trust-but-verify” approach:
•Black-box only: No weight access required—suitable for third-party auditors evaluating
proprietary models.
•Zero-shot: No per-model calibration or training data needed, enabling rapid deployment
for new models.
•Defense-in-depth: Should be deployed alongside trusted monitoring and other methods,
not as a standalone solution.
•Targeted auditing: Query-intensive (∼100+ API calls per evaluation), best used for high-
stakes capability assessments rather than routine screening.
6 LIMITATIONS& FUTUREWORK
Task coverage: Evaluated only on benchmarks with verifiable answers (GPQA, MATH, USACO)—
performance on open-ended tasks is unknown.Untested adversaries: We have not evaluated RL-
optimized evasion or gradient-based attacks targeting C3 directly; the consistency-deception tradeoff
may not hold under direct optimization pressure.Adversarial evasion: Deterministic error recipes
reduce detection to 4.7–46%, though complementary methods compensate.Query cost:∼100+
API calls per evaluation limits applicability to targeted auditing.Scaling: How detection difficulty
changes with model capability remains unstudied. C3 should be deployed as one layer in a defense-
in-depth strategy—not as a standalone solution.

B BASELINECONFOUNDANALYSIS
Lie detector model-identity confound.The Pacchiardi lie detector (Pacchiardi et al., 2024) trains
a logistic regression classifier on responses to unrelated probe questions. In the standard mixed-
model setting (different models for honest and sandbagging), it achieves 89% recall. However,
when both conditions use the same model (GPT-5-nano), recall drops to 2%—a∼44×collapse.
We trace this to a systematic difference: GPT-4.1-nano and GPT-5-nano differ by 35 percentage
points in their baseline “yes” response rates to probe questions (21% vs. 56%). The classifier
learns this distributional shift rather than any sandbagging-specific signal—a Clever Hans effect (La-
puschkin et al., 2019).
Trusted monitoring correctness confound.Trusted monitoring scores correlate strongly with
answer correctness (r=−0.66,p <10−25) but only weakly with actual sandbagging intent (rpartial =
0.19,p= 0.007after controlling for correctness). When sandbagging models produce outputs that
look likegenuine limitations, TM cannot distinguish them.
C MODELORGANISMS
Our 65 original system prompts specify concrete sandbagging strategies organized by error type:
•Arithmetic errors: Off-by-one, sign flips, order-of-magnitude mistakes
•Reasoning shortcuts: Skipping steps, premature conclusions, circular reasoning
•Plausible wrong answers: Selecting common misconceptions, near-miss solutions
•Knowledge gaps: Claiming unfamiliarity with relevant concepts
•Adversarial (deterministic): Fixed confusion matrices, consistent error recipes

F DUAL-USECONSIDERATIONS
False positive/negative rates.C 3 is calibrated for≤5% FPR by design. Recall varies substan-
tially: 94–100% on GPQA non-adversarial prompts but only 4.7% on the most evasive USACO
strategies. Users should understand that a negative result does not guarantee the absence of sand-
bagging.
Potential for misuse.Our detailed analysis of what makes sandbagging detectable could theo-
retically be used to train better sandbaggers—for example, using C 3 as a fitness signal during RL
training. We note that: (1) the detection methods and consistency-deception asymmetry are al-
ready established concepts in the research literature; (2) specific evasion strategies (deterministic
error recipes) are already known and documented; (3) the defensive value of providing auditors with
practical tools substantially outweighs the marginal offensive value.
Responsible deployment.We recommend C 3 be deployed as part of defense-in-depth strategies
rather than standalone. Discovery of novel evasion strategies during auditing should be disclosed to
model providers through responsible disclosure channels before public release.
