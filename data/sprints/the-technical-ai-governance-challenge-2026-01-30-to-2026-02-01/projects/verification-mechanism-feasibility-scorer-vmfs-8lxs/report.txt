 Abstract 
 Despite  growing  international  interest  in  veriﬁable  AI  governance—from  the  2023  Bletchley 
 Declaration  to  the  2025  Paris  AI  Action  Summit—treaty  negotiators  and  policy  makers  lack 
 a  systematic  framework  for  comparing  veriﬁcation  mechanisms,  leading  to  ad  hoc  decisions  in 
 binding  agreements  like  compute  pauses  or  model  deployment  halts.  Concurrently,  the  AI 
 safety  community  generally,  and  technical  AI  governance  researchers  speciﬁcally,  are 
 examining,  developing,  and  testing  a  wide  range  of  veriﬁcation  methods.  The  resulting 
 dynamic  is  one  of  a  fractured  gap  between  policy  makers  and  AI  governance  advocates, 
 where  international  policy  goals  and  intent  are  struggling  to  keep  pace  with  veriﬁcation 
 innovations  and  ideas,  and  vice  versa.  We  aim  to  solve  this  with  a  product  (a  decision 
 support  engine  and  dashboard  application)  that  incorporates  a  Veriﬁcation  Mechanism 
 Feasibility  Scorer  (VMFS).  The  VMFS  is  a  multi-dimensional  evaluation  tool  scoring 
 proposals  across  four  dimensions:  Evidence  Hardness,  (the  “Trust”  score),  Infrastructure 
 Burden  (The  “Cost”  Score),  Intrusion  Burden  (the  “Friction”  score),  and  Robustness  (the 
 “the  Cheating  Score”).  We  analyzed  nine  mechanisms—from  on-chip  telemetry  and  TEE 
 attestation  to  OPCW-style  declarations  and  model  watermarking  (overall  4.1)—VMFS 
 reveals  no  silver  bullet,  suggesting  layered  approaches  will  be  required  in  any  range  of 
 elements  of  an  AI  treaty.  Our  analysis  highlights  R&D  priorities  for  computer  monitoring 
 and  consideration  of  tech  transfer  to  avoid  Global  South  exclusion.  Our  open-source  rubric, 
 mechanism  dashboard,  and  future  Global  South  analysis  can  empower  UN/G7  forums, 
 diplomats,  and  policy  makers  to  design  feasible,  halt-capable  treaties.  Future  expanded

 1. Introduction 
 International AI governance is shifting from principles to enforceable treaties like compute 
 thresholds (>10^27 FLOPs) and model deployment halts, but veriﬁcation—credibly conﬁrming 
 compliance amid secrecy—remains unsolved, risking ineffective pacts. The 2023 Bletchley 
 Declaration (UK Gov, 2023), 2024 Seoul Summit (Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Korea, 2024), and 
 2025 Paris AI Action Summit (ITU Publications et al., 2025) call for "veriﬁable safety 
 commitments" without tools to compare proposals from (Wasil et al., 2024)(14 methods), Simon 
 Institute IAEA mappings (Krawec, 2025), or Oxford Martin analyses (Harack et al., 2025).  
 We introduce the Veriﬁcation Mechanism Feasibility Scorer (VMFS) rubric and dashboard 
 evaluating nine mechanisms (e.g., TEE attestation, remote sensing) across four dimensions.  The 
 ﬁrst is Evidence Hardness, or the “Trust” score, examining whether the evidence is based on 
 math/physics (Hard) or human trust (Soft). Secondly, the Infrastructure Burden, or the “Cost” 
 score, explores whether it requires building new hardware (High Burden) or just passing laws 
 (Low Burden). Next, the Intrusion Burden, or the “Friction” score, covering how much secret data 
 (IP, Weights) is exposed.  And lastly, Robustness, or the “Cheating” score, to examine how easy 
 or hard it is to spoof or hide from this mechanism. 
 Research Questions 
 1.  How can diverse AI veriﬁcation mechanisms be systematically compared across dimensions 
 relevant to treaty implementation? 
 2.  Which mechanisms are ready for near-term deployment; what are their principal barriers? 
 3.  How can application tools and dashboards engage policy makers, treaty negotiators, and 
 AI governance researchers to support their needs and education? 
 Our main contributions are: 
 1.  We developed VMFS, the ﬁrst rubric scoring nine AI veriﬁcation mechanisms and four 
 feasibility dimensions to prioritize options for halt-capable treaties. 
 2.  VMFS analysis scores mechanisms, revealing trade-offs like model watermarking's 4.1/5 
 overall lead and TEEs' technical strength amid high infrastructure burden and intrusion, 
 prioritizing compute monitoring R&D.

 3.  We develop an interactive decision-support system, including a dashboard, portfolio 
 sandbox, and submission interface; enabling users to explore mechanism trade offs, identity 
 coverage gaps, and construct layered veriﬁcation portfolios. 
 2. Related Work 
 AI veriﬁcation draws from arms-control precedents like IAEA material accountancy (for compute), 
 OPCW declarations (self-reporting), and START national technical means (remote monitoring), 
 as mapped by Simon Institute (Krawec, 2025) and cataloged in (Wasil et al., 2024)(14 methods).  
 Technical proposals include compute governance (Sastry et al., 2024)  (Sastry et al., GovAI), 
 hardware telemetry/TEEs (Kulp et al., 2024)(Kulp et al., RAND), model audits/lineage (Harack 
 et al., 2025)(Oxford Martin AIGI), and UN SAB conﬁdential veriﬁcation.  These works propose 
 mechanisms but lack uniﬁed multi-dimensional feasibility scoring (e.g. evidence hardness, 
 infrastructure burden, evasion robustness) for treaty prioritization; VMFS ﬁlls this gap. 
 3. Methods 
 VMFS combines literature review and expert input with a rule-based, text-derived scoring 
 procedure applied to nine veriﬁcation mechanisms across four feasibility dimensions, producing an 
 interactive dashboard for comparative analysis and portfolio exploration. 
 4. Results 
 VMFS scores nine mechanisms (1-5 scale, equal-weighted average) across dimensions, showing 
 variation with no silver bullet above 4.1 overall. Model Watermarking leads at 4.1 (low intrusion). 
 Hardware-anchored mechanisms (e.g., TEE attestation) achieve high evidence hardness (5.0) but 
 are constrained by infrastructure burden (1.0) and intrusion (4.0), resulting in lower scores (3.4). 
 Key Findings 
 1.  No mechanism tops 4.1 across dimensions; layered portfolios essential (e.g., remote 
 sensing + audits + whistleblowers).  
 2.  Trade-offs are systematic: high-hardness mechanisms impose high burden and intrusion, 
 while low-cost mechanisms sacriﬁce evidentiary strength. 
 3.  Veriﬁcation value clusters by evidence location—audits (model safety), telemetry (compute 
 use), sensing (facilities)—with whistleblowers addressing undeclared activity. 
 5. Discussion and Limitations 
 3 

===== page 4 =====

 VMFS results underscore that AI veriﬁcation demands pragmatic, layered architectures over 
 idealistic singletons, with implications for near-term treaties prioritizing politically tractable 
 starters like model watermarking(4.1) remote sensing (3.6 avg.) and declarations 
 (OPCW-inspired) to cue deeper checks (e.g., audits for MV7 safety). For AI safety, this enables 
 "halt-capable" regimes verifying compute pauses or unsafe deployments amid competition, 
 reducing surprise risks via conﬁdential evidence (UN SAB), while future work on Global South 
 dimensions could highlight equity imperatives—hardware-heavy methods risk North-South divides 
 without tiered/tech-transfer provisions, mirroring NPT Article IV. Methodological limits include 
 expert-judgment scoring (subjective; needs validation surveys), equal dimension weights (may 
 undervalue politics), and static 2026 snapshot (tech evolves rapidly). Scope excludes novel 
 mechanisms (e.g., ZKPs) or full VFMC depth due to hackathon time; unaddressed threats: insider 
 collusion bypassing whistleblowers (M6) or quantum breaks on TEEs. Differently, we'd weight 
 dimensions dynamically and pilot with negotiators for calibration. 
 Limitations 
 VMFS relies on expert judgment for scores rather than empirical data, risking 
 subjectivity—different panels may vary (e.g., political tractability optimistic/pessimistic). Equal 
 dimension weighting ignores priorities (e.g., sovereignty > technical for some treaties); no 
 sensitivity analysis included.  
 Nine mechanisms illustrative, not exhaustive (e.g., omitted proof-of-learning, model cards); these 
 cover core but not niche risks like multi-agent systems. Scores static—tech/politics evolve rapidly 
 (e.g., TEE maturity by 2027?). 
 Three-day timeframe limited empirical validation, Global South consultations, and user 
 design/testing of full dashboard reﬁnement (prototype functional, not production). 
 Future Work 
 Conduct expert surveys to validate/reﬁne VMFS scores; add sub-dimensions (e.g., hardware vs. 
 software technical) and weighted scenarios (e.g., US-China treaty).   This would include deeper 
 user interviews and problem needs identiﬁcation with technical AI governance researchers seeking 
 solutions for how to improve feedback loops from policy makers and regulators. 
 Score additional mechanisms (proof-of-learning, model cards, capability evals); extend to 
 multi-agent/emergent risks; integrate VFMC failure modes quantitatively. 
 Productionize dashboard (web app, API); pilot with users from UN/G7/INAI for treaty design; 
 engage Global South via workshops/tech transfer pilots to iterate adoptability. 
 Publish regular updates to the “stakeholder solicitation” of veriﬁcation mechanisms via the VMFS 
 Dashboard, every 3 to 6 months.  The evolving compendium of mechanisms would be submitted to

 6. Conclusion 
 VMFS systematically scores nine AI veriﬁcation mechanisms, revealing no silver bullet—model 
 watermarking leads overall (4.1 avg) for low intrusion, but layered approaches (e.g., audits + 
 telemetry + whistleblowers) are essential for robust evidence-location coverage in halt-capable 
 treaties. Hardware excels technically (TEE evidence hardness 5.0) are constrained by 
 infrastructure burden (1.0) and intrusion (4.0), motivating prioritization of compute-monitoring 
 R&D and complementary low-friction mechanisms. 
 The VFMS dashboard empowers negotiators with decision support tools to prioritize feasible 
 regimes, bridging policy-tech gaps for UN/G7/INAI.  The open-source rubric enables global 
 adaptation, potentially accelerating intent to generate veriﬁable commitments for managing 
 frontier risks.

 Appendix C: Dashboard Components 
 C.1 Portfolio Sandbox 
 The Portfolio Sandbox enables users to simulate treaty regimes by combining 1–3 mechanisms. 
 Scoring Logic: 
 ●  Compute the average across four dimensions for the selected mechanism set 
 ●  Visualize via radar chart (North=Hardness, South=Low Burden, East=Robustness, 
 West=Low Intrusion) 
 Gap Analysis (Red Team Alert):  The system identiﬁes blind spots in user-selected portfolios. 
 Example logic: 
 IF selected_locations NOT INCLUDE chip_level THEN 
 Display: "Your portfolio cannot verify digital activity (FLOPs). 
 A bad actor could use legal hardware for illegal training. 
 Consider adding On-Chip Telemetry."

 C.2 Veriﬁcation Advisor 
 The Advisor is an LLM-based chatbot serving as a Technical Auditor persona, prioritizing "Proof" 
 and "Cost" over diplomatic framing. 
 Operational Rules: 
 1.  Always cite speciﬁc scores (e.g., "Remote Sensing has Robustness score: 3.0") 
 2.  Emphasize trade-offs (e.g., "High security but high Infrastructure Burden") 
 3.  Reference Evidence Locations to explain mechanism coverage 
 Example Prompts: 
 ●  "Find me a high-trust (Hardness > 4) mechanism." 
 ●  "What is the cheapest way to verify compute?" 
 ●  "Why does On-Chip Telemetry have a high Intrusion score?" 
 C.3 Stakeholder Submission Interface 
 Users can submit novel veriﬁcation ideas for evaluation against the VMFS framework. 
 Extraction Pipeline: 
 1.  Evidence Location Identiﬁcation:  Map user input to relevant location (e.g., "registry  of 
 researchers" → Institutional Record) 
 2.  Score Prediction:  Apply scoring rubrics to generate provisional dimension scores 
 3.  Output Generation:  Create temporary Mechanism Card with radar chart visualization 
 Example: 
 ●  Input: "A global registry of AI researchers" 
 ●  Predicted Scores: Hardness 1.0, Burden 5.0, Intrusion 2.0, Robustness 2.0 
 ●  Interpretation: "Low-cost but low-trust mechanism with privacy concerns"

 Appendix D:  Limitations & Dual-Use Considerations 
 Please see limitations as listed in the main section of the paper. 
 Dual-use risks involve the potential for malicious actors or AI systems to use this information to 
 design or anticipate ways to avoid veriﬁcation mechanisms, complicate their negotiation, or 
 otherwise undermine coordination efforts within the AI safety and political realms on international 
 agreement utilizing veriﬁcation and enforcement mechanisms. 
 Appendix E: Responsible Use Declaration 
 VMFS is a decision-support tool designed to inform—not replace—expert judgment in treaty 
 negotiations. Users should recognize that: 
 1.  Scores are indicative, not prescriptive.  Feasibility  scores reﬂect technical and institutional 
 characteristics under idealized conditions; real-world deployment requires context-speciﬁc 
 legal, diplomatic, and political analysis beyond this framework's scope. 
 2.  The tool does not endorse mechanisms.  High scores  indicate implementation feasibility, 
 not normative desirability. Ethical, sovereignty, and human rights implications require 
 separate assessment. 
 3.  Intended users are policy professionals.  VMFS assumes  users possess baseline familiarity 
 with international governance; misapplication by untrained actors may yield misleading 
 conclusions. 
 4.  Open-source release supports global adaptation.  We  encourage modiﬁcation for regional 
 contexts while requesting attribution and responsible deployment aligned with cooperative 
 AI governance goals. 
 Appendix F: Ethical Considerations 
 ●  Privacy and Sovereignty.  Several high-scoring mechanisms (e.g., on-chip telemetry, TEE 
 attestation) require intrusive data access. We ﬂag Intrusion scores explicitly to ensure 
 negotiators weigh surveillance risks against veriﬁcation beneﬁts, particularly for 
 jurisdictions with strong data protection norms.

 ●  Equity in Veriﬁcation Burden.  Hardware-dependent mechanisms risk imposing 
 disproportionate compliance costs on Global South nations lacking domestic semiconductor 
 industries. We highlight this asymmetry qualitatively and recommend future work on 
 tiered implementation and technology transfer frameworks. 
 ●  Transparency-Security Balance.  Full methodological disclosure enables scrutiny and 
 reproducibility but may reduce operational security if deployed in sensitive negotiations. 
 Users should adapt transparency levels to the appropriate context.
