1 INTRODUCTION
Global AI Safety Notary: A Decentralised Protocol for
International AI Incident Reporting
Augusta Lina1, Caleb Rudnick 2, and Dennis Kiptanui 3
With Apart Research
Abstract.
International AI governance suffers from a fundamental trust deficit. Nations are reluctant to re-
port AI safety incidents to centralised bodies controlled by potential adversaries, creating dangerous
information silos where critical safety lessons go unshared. We present Global AI Safety Notary, a de-
centralised incident reporting platform that creates immutable, verifiable records of AI safety incidents
on the Ethereum blockchain. Built on the Ethereum Attestation Service (EAS), the platform enables re-
searchers, developers, and organisations to report and track AI failures, including bias, safety violations,
and data leaks, with full transparency and accountability. By leveraging blockchain technology, our
system creates a sovereign-neutral registry where no single government or institution controls the data.
All participants, including geopolitical rivals, can verify that records are tamper-proof and that no party
has privileged access. We demonstrate a working prototype deployed on Ethereum Sepolia that enables
structured incident submission, searchable registry browsing, and independent blockchain verification
through EASScan. Global AI Safety Notary provides the technical infrastructure for evidence-based
international AI policy, enabling cooperation without requiring trust.
1. Introduction
As AI systems become more capable, the risks they pose increasingly cross national borders. A model
trained in one country can cause harm in another; a safety incident at one lab may reveal vulnera-
bilities shared across the industry. The EU AI Act took effect in August 2024, establishing the first
comprehensive regulatory framework for AI systems [5]. However, practical tools for international in-
cident coordination remain scarce. The core problem is trust. When an AI safety institute in one
nation discovers a critical vulnerability, sharing that information with a centralised international body
controlled by a potential adversary creates risks: the information could be weaponised, access could be
asymmetric, or records could be altered. This creates dangerous information silos where critical safety
lessons go unshared, increasing global risk while nations remain blind to incidents elsewhere.
This trust deficit has precedent in other safety-critical domains. Aviation safety reporting faced
similar challenges before frameworks like the Aviation Safety Reporting System (ASRS) established con-
fidential, non-punitive reporting mechanisms. Healthcare incident reporting evolved through systems
like the Patient Safety Reporting System. More recently, researchers have proposed blockchain-based
approaches for construction safety [1], cybersecurity incident management [10], and healthcare adverse
event tracking [6]. We present Global AI Safety Notary, a decentralised platform that addresses the
international AI governance trust deficit through blockchain technology. Our contributions are:
1. A structured schema for AI safety incidents compatible with emerging governance frameworks,
capturing incident type, severity, model identifier, and mitigation status.
2. An implementation using the Ethereum Attestation Service (EAS) that creates immutable, cryp-
tographically verifiable incident records without requiring trust in a central authority.
3. A working prototype demonstrating the complete workflow: incident submission via Web3 wallet,
on-chain attestation, and independent verification through public blockchain explorers.
4. Analysis of how decentralised infrastructure enables international cooperation by removing the
political barrier of centralised control.

2. Background & Related Work
2.1. Blockchain for Safety Incident Reporting
Multiple domains have explored blockchain and distributed ledger technology for safety incident man-
agement. Aktas et al. [2] proposed a blockchain framework for aviation safety reporting, arguing that
distributed ledgers could address confidentiality concerns while maintaining auditability. Morteza et
al. [9] developed a blockchain information management framework for construction safety, demonstrat-
ing how permissioned ledgers enable multi-stakeholder coordination. In healthcare, Marbouh et al. [6]
presented a blockchain-based incident reporting system for patient safety, emphasising the importance
of immutability and provenance for medical error tracking. Milne-Ives et al. [8] proposed DeLIVER,
a distributed ledger infrastructure for adverse event reporting with privacy-preserving features. For
cybersecurity, Olukoya [10] analysed blockchain requirements for digital investigation platforms, and
Preuveneers et al. [11] developed a distributed security framework for threat intelligence sharing. These
systems share common requirements, specifically immutability, decentralised trust and auditability [3].
2.2. Ethereum Attestation Service
The Ethereum Attestation Service (EAS) is an open-source infrastructure for creating and verifying
on-chain attestations. Unlike custom smart contracts, EAS provides a standardised protocol where
attesters can make structured claims about any subject using custom schemas. EAS attestations are
stored on-chain, queryable via GraphQL, and independently verifiable through EASScan explorers.
This infrastructure is deployed on Ethereum mainnet and multiple testnets including Sepolia.

2.3. International AI Governance Context
Current international AI governance relies on voluntary frameworks and national regulations without
robust verification infrastructure. The EU AI Act establishes risk-based requirements but compliance
verification remains challenging. The Bletchley Declaration (2023) acknowledged the need for interna-
tional cooperation on AI safety but provided no technical mechanisms. Proposals for international AI
safety institutes, analogous to the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), face the fundamental
challenge of verification between parties without full trust. How can nations confirm that incident re-
ports are accurate without privileged access? How can they ensure records are not altered or selectively
disclosed? Blockchain technology directly addresses these challenges. A decentralised ledger provides:
•Sovereign neutrality: No nation “owns” the registry
•Immutability: Records cannot be altered retroactively

3. Methods
Global AI Safety Notary implements a three-layer architecture (Figure 1):
1.Presentation Layer: Next.js web application with Web3 wallet integration
2.Blockchain Layer: Ethereum Attestation Service on Sepolia testnet
3.Query Layer: GraphQL API for retrieving and filtering attestations
Users connect via MetaMask or compatible Web3 wallets. Incident submissions create on-chain
attestations through the EAS smart contract. The registry queries attestations via the EAS GraphQL
endpoint and displays them with links to EASScan for independent verification.
4. Implementation
4.1. T echnology Stack
The prototype was implemented using Next.js 16 with React 19 as the frontend framework, providing a
modern web application architecture with server-side rendering capabilities. For blockchain integration,
we deployed on Ethereum Sepolia testnet, enabling cost-free testing while maintaining compatibility
with mainnet deployment. Attestation functionality was implemented using the official @ethereum-
attestation-service/eas-sdk, which provides a standardised interface for creating and querying on-chain
attestations. Web3 wallet connectivity was achieved through a combination of wagmi for React hooks,
viem for low-level Ethereum interactions, and RainbowKit for a polished wallet connection interface
supporting MetaMask and other popular wallets. The user interface was styled using Tailwind CSS,
enabling rapid development of a responsive, accessible design.
4.2. Core Components
The SchemaEncoder from the EAS SDK handles data serialisation. The attestation creation function
connects to the EAS contract and submits the encoded incident data. GraphQL queries retrieve
attestations filtered by schema, enabling the registry to display all incidents matching our custom
schema.

5. Discussion
5.1. Addressing the International T rust Deficit
Global AI Safety Notary demonstrates how blockchain infrastructure can enable international coop-
eration without requiring trust in central authorities. When organisations report incidents to the
decentralised registry rather than a national agency, they contribute to a shared global knowledge
base while retaining sovereignty—they do not cede authority to any single nation. Participants can
independently verify that records are tamper-proof through cryptographic proofs, and they have assur-
ance that no party has privileged access to the data. This creates a credible commitment mechanism
for international AI safety coordination. Even geopolitical rivals can participate in the same registry
because the blockchain’s cryptographic guarantees replace institutional trust.
5.2. Permissioned Attestors: Leveraging the Development Bottleneck
The notary system can theoretically be applied in a context where only authorised organisations are able
to submit based on a database of approved organisation hashes. This prevents against false submissions
which result from permissionless participation. The authorised contributor approach makes use of the
bottleneck of major frontier AI contributors, whereby only a few players are currently contributing to
the advancement of frontier AI models and compute resources. In 2024, fewer than twenty laboratories
worldwide possessed the compute, talent, and capital to train frontier models [4]. This development
bottleneck creates a natural quality control mechanism. Rather than allowing arbitrary wallet addresses
to submit incidents, Global AI Safety Notary is designed to support an allowlist of authorised attestors:
Authorised Attestor Categories:
•AI Safety Institutes: National bodies such as the UK AI Safety Institute, US AI Safety
Institute, and equivalents in other jurisdictions
•F rontier AI Laboratories: Organisations developing models above defined capability thresh-
olds (e.g., 10 25 FLOP training compute)
•Accredited Auditors: Third-party evaluation organisations with demonstrated technical com-
petence
•Academic Research Groups: University-affiliated AI safety researchers with institutional
accountability
This permissioned model provides several advantages. Incidents submitted by authorised parties
carry institutional credibility, as false or frivolous reports risk reputational damage to the submitting
organisation, creating natural incentives for accuracy. Because attestor addresses map to known organ-
isations, the registry becomes a tool for accountability tracking, monitoring which labs are reporting
incidents and which are not. Patterns of non-reporting may themselves be significant signals worthy of
regulatory attention. Authorised attestor status can be linked to regulatory compliance, enabling labs
required to report incidents under frameworks like the EU AI Act to demonstrate compliance through
on-chain attestations from their registered wallet addresses. This approach supports scalable gover-
nance: as more organisations develop frontier capabilities, the allowlist can expand, and the blockchain
infrastructure scales independently of the number of authorised parties.
Implementation uses EAS’s built-in filtering: the registry interface queries only attestations from
addresses on the authorised list, while the underlying blockchain remains permissionless for trans-
parency. Any party can verify that authorised attestors submitted specific records, but only authorised
parties can add records to the curated registry view. This approach transforms the concentrated struc-
ture of frontier AI development from a governance challenge into an advantage. With few players,
comprehensive monitoring becomes tractable. Every major laboratory can be assigned a cryptographic
identity, and their reporting behaviour, or absence thereof, becomes part of the permanent, auditable
record.

5.3. Alignment with Governance F rameworks
Our incident taxonomy aligns with the EU AI Act’s risk categories and emerging frameworks from AI
safety institutes. The structured schema enables aggregation of incidents by type for policy analysis,
allowing regulators and researchers to identify systemic patterns across AI systems. Severity-based
classification supports prioritisation for regulatory attention, ensuring critical incidents receive ap-
propriate scrutiny. The decentralised nature of the registry facilitates cross-border incident tracking
and pattern detection, as all participating nations access the same immutable dataset. Furthermore,
the timestamped record of incidents and their resolution enables longitudinal analysis of mitigation
effectiveness, providing empirical grounding for evidence-based AI policy.
5.4. Limitations
Current limitations of the system should be acknowledged. The prototype is deployed on Ethereum
Sepolia testnet; production use would require mainnet deployment with associated transaction costs
and additional security considerations. While attester addresses provide cryptographic identity, they
do not inherently verify real-world identity, meaning anonymous reporting remains possible without
additional identity verification mechanisms. Large evidence files such as screenshots and logs require
off-chain storage solutions, as the current implementation stores only content hashes rather than full
evidence. The community verification workflow creates new attestations referencing original reports
but lacks formal multi-signature governance for disputed incidents. Finally, schema evolution presents
challenges: changes to the attestation schema require new registration on EAS, and migrating historical
data to updated schemas is non-trivial.
6. Conclusion
We presented Global AI Safety Notary, a decentralised incident reporting platform that addresses the
international trust deficit in AI governance. By leveraging the Ethereum Attestation Service, our
system creates immutable, verifiable records of AI safety incidents without requiring trust in central
authorities. Our working prototype demonstrates the complete workflow from incident submission
through on-chain attestation to independent blockchain verification. The structured schema captures
essential incident information while remaining compatible with emerging governance frameworks. As
AI systems become more capable and their risks more global, the infrastructure for international
cooperation becomes critical. Current proposals for AI safety institutes and international agreements
assume technical verification mechanisms that largely do not exist. Global AI Safety Notary contributes
a practical building block: a sovereign-neutral, tamper-proof registry where any nation or organisation
can report incidents with confidence that records cannot be altered, access cannot be restricted, and
verification is independent. Decentralisation removes the political barrier. When the infrastructure is
trustless, cooperation becomes possible even between parties who cannot trust each other. This is the
technical foundation that international AI governance requires.
