ABSTRACT
International AI agreements that aim to constrain frontier capability growth or re-
duce catastrophic deployment risk face a recurring implementation barrier: the
actors who control critical capability are often non state entities, including fron-
tier labs, cloud providers, universities, and model deployers. Agreements writ-
ten solely as state to state commitments tend to fail when compliance depends
on domestic enforcement that is slow, uneven, or strategically reversible. This
paper proposes a governance architecture that brings non state actors into scope
through insurance and liability, and it formalizes the central data problem that
blocks such an approach today. We model frontier model incidents as a heavy
tailed loss process with strong cross actor correlation and severe under reporting.
Under these conditions, conventional actuarial pricing fails, voluntary insurance
excludes precisely the tail risk of interest, and liability rules can unintentionally
suppress disclosure. We propose a minimal international regime in which ac-
cess to high capability model deployment markets and high end compute supply
chains is conditioned on carrying qualified coverage, and coverage is conditioned
on submitting standardized, privacy preserving risk data. We specify the data
fields needed for underwriting, define a reporting and auditing protocol that can
be adopted by companies and research institutions across borders, and provide a
simulation study that quantifies how missingness and correlation dominate pre-
mium uncertainty and capital load. We conclude with policy recommendations
for treaty text, for domestic implementing legislation, and for regulatory modi-
fications that enable a functioning AI insurance sector without creating perverse
incentives against transparency.

1 INTRODUCTION
Frontier AI risk governance is increasingly constrained by a mismatch between formal authority
and operational control. States can sign agreements, but critical capability is concentrated in pri-
vate firms, research institutions, and cross border cloud platforms. Even when a state endorses an
agreement, it may lack the ability or the will to monitor training and deployment in detail, especially
when the underlying systems evolve rapidly and the relevant evidence is proprietary. Many gover-
nance proposals rely on technical verification of compute or on export controls. These instruments
are valuable but incomplete. They do not directly address the incentives of non state actors that
decide whether to train a new model, how to evaluate it, how to deploy it, and how to respond to
near misses. They also do not provide a durable compliance interface that remains effective when
supply chains and corporate structures adapt Bengio et al. (2025b).
Insurance and liability frameworks are a candidate interface. They are already used in aviation,
maritime shipping, nuclear operations, construction, and cyber security to force adoption of safety
controls when direct government inspection is expensive. Yet AI insurance remains shallow. Most
policies cover narrow risks such as professional liability or intellectual property disputes, and they
often exclude systemic harm, misuse, and model behavior risks. A primary reason is the absence
of insurance grade data: insurers cannot price what they cannot observe, and AI developers cannot
share sensitive information without risking competitive loss or legal exposure.

This paper addresses three goals within one coherent technical governance argument.
Data goal: formalize the data required to price frontier model risk and show why existing public
incident information is insufficient under heavy tail and correlated shock conditions.Inclusion
goal: design an international agreement mechanism that binds non state actors through market and
contract leverage rather than relying entirely on state enforcement.Insurance goal: propose a
liability and insurance structure that makes transparency and control adoption privately rational
while maintaining strong incentives against reckless scaling and negligent deployment.
Our core claim is that the main bottleneck for insurance based frontier AI governance is neither
willingness nor ideology. It is a tractable but neglected measurement problem: without standardized
risk exposure and incident data with known reporting properties, insurers will either withdraw or
offer coverage that does not discipline frontier behavior.
2 RELATED WORK
Our proposal connects three literatures.
First, it builds on international governance mechanisms that achieve compliance by conditioning
market access on standardized documentation and audits, including trade linked environmental
agreements and controlled goods regimes. These mechanisms motivate using qualified coverage
and procurement requirements to include non state actors in practice.
Second, it draws on cyber insurance and safety critical industry experience showing that insurers
can induce adoption of specific controls, but that correlated and systemic losses can cause insurers
to retreat into exclusions without shared data and risk pooling. This motivates modeling correlated
shocks and focusing on insurance grade data rather than only on public incident narratives.
Third, it relates to emerging AI governance proposals that emphasize reporting, evaluations, and
post deployment monitoring as enforceable interfaces for high capability systems. Our contribution
is to frame these interfaces as underwriting relevant data infrastructure that can be embedded into
insurance contracts and liability safe harbors, thereby creating a cross border inclusion channel for
companies and research institutions Wasil (2024); Tweed et al. (2025).
3 PROBLEM STATEMENT
3.1 NON STATE ACTOR INCLUSION IS STRUCTURALLY HARD.
International agreements are usually written for states. Frontier AI capability is increasingly con-
trolled by entities that are not states and that operate across multiple jurisdictions. This creates at
least five failure modes.
Jurisdictional arbitrage: a firm can move training or deployment to a region with weaker enforce-
ment. Corporate fragmentation: capability can be distributed across subsidiaries, contractors, and
research partners. Supply chain mixing: compute can be sourced through intermediaries that are dif-
ficult to monitor. Rapid iteration: even if a model version is audited, post training modifications can
change risk profile on short timescales. Ambiguous responsibility: when harm occurs, it is difficult
to assign causal responsibility between model developer, deployer, and end user.
A governance system that does not include non state actors as first class subjects tends to turn into a
patchwork of uneven domestic controls.
3.2 INSURANCE AND LIABILITY ARE ATTRACTIVE BUT CURRENTLY INFEASIBLE AT
FRONTIER SCALE
Insurance can be a strong incentive mechanism because it is enforced through contract terms, premi-
ums, exclusions, and required controls. Liability can create a floor of responsibility and define safe
harbors that encourage adoption of best practices. However, insurers face two technical challenges
that are especially acute for frontier AI.

Heavy tailed losses: rare catastrophic events dominate expected loss and capital requirements. Cor-
related risk: failures are not independent across policyholders. A single vulnerability class, a single
misuse technique, or a single widely deployed model update can generate simultaneous claims.
A third practical challenge amplifies both issues.
Missingness and selection: incidents are under reported, near misses are rarely disclosed, and ob-
served incidents are biased toward public failures. Without a structured data layer, insurers either
cannot price or they price conservatively and exclude the risk categories that governance most needs
to influence.
3.3 THE DATA PROBLEM AT THE CENTER
To underwrite responsibly, an insurer needs more than counts of publicly known failures. They need
exposure and control data. Exposure data includes what is deployed, where, to whom, with what
tool access, with what distribution channels, and with what update cadence. Control data includes
what evaluations were done, what monitoring exists, what incident response procedures exist, and
what access restrictions exist. The core data problem is to collect a minimal set of exposure, control,
and incident fields in a way that is verifiable, privacy preserving, and compatible with commercial
confidentiality.
4 PRECEDENTS FROM ENVIRONMENTAL AND CYBER GOVERNANCE
This section extracts design patterns rather than analogies.
Environmental governanceSeveral environmental agreements succeed because they combine state
commitments with trade and market mechanisms that pressure private actors to comply.Montreal
Protocol style controls: restrictions on trade in controlled substances created incentives for manufac-
turers and distributors to comply, even beyond signatory states, because access to markets depended
on compliance.CITES style permit systems: trade in controlled goods requires documentation and
audits, which pushes compliance onto private supply chains.
Design lesson: a treaty can create compliance pull by conditioning market access on meeting a
standardized reporting and verifiable control interface.
Cyber governance and cyber insuranceCyber insurance partially succeeded at driving adoption
of certain controls, such as multi factor authentication and backup regimes, because insurers can
refuse coverage or increase premiums when controls are missing. However, cyber insurance also
experienced failures.Systemic correlated risk: ransomware campaigns created correlated losses.
Insurers reacted by restricting coverage, raising deductibles, and demanding stronger controls.Data
scarcity: under reporting and inconsistent incident taxonomies limited actuarial precision.
Design lesson: a reporting and disclosure regime is required for insurance to remain disciplining
rather than degenerating into exclusion and retreat.
Liability and reporting culturesAviation safety improved through mandatory reporting, protected
reporting channels, and standardized incident investigation practices. In high reliability industries,
near miss reporting is treated as a resource for prevention rather than as a reputational liability.
Design lesson: liability can be structured to encourage reporting through safe harbors, while still
punishing negligence and reckless operation.

6 MINIMAL INSURANCE GRADE DATA REQUIREMENTS FOR FRONTIERAI
This section focuses on feasibility. The objective is not a comprehensive model card. It is a minimal
set of fields that allow pricing, auditing, and dispute resolution.
Exposure fields: Model class and version identifier, including post training stage identifiers. Deploy-
ment channel class, including API access, open weights distribution, and internal agent deployment.
Tool access class, including whether the system can call external tools, browse, execute code, or ac-
cess sensitive internal resources. User base and volume proxies, such as request counts, active users,
or internal usage hours, reported in coarse bins. Update cadence, including frequency of weight
updates and policy updates.
Control fields: Evaluation suite coverage, including what categories are tested, at what frequency,
and with what decision thresholds for release. Monitoring and logging coverage, including whether
misuse detection exists and whether incident response playbooks exist. Access controls, includ-
ing rate limits, identity verification level for high risk use, and restrictions for high risk domains.
Incident response capability, including time to mitigate, rollback capability, and escalation process.
Incident fields: Incident type class, including misuse enablement, data leakage, autonomy failures,
and tool misuse. Severity measure, reported as bounded ordinal plus optional monetary estimate
under confidentiality. Discovery channel, including internal monitoring, external report, regulator
notice, or media. Remediation action, including patch, policy change, rollback, user notification,
and third party coordination.
Verifiability and confidentiality: To include non state actors, data reporting must not require re-
vealing training data or proprietary weights. A minimal mechanism is a combination of crypto-
graphic commitments to model version identifiers and evaluation attestations, aggregated reporting
for volume measures in coarse bins, protected reporting to insurers and regulators with safe harbor
provisions.
This paper does not assume perfect cryptographic attestation of all fields. It assumes that incentives
and audit risk can sustain partial verifiability, similar to other compliance regimes Bengio et al.
(2025a).

7 GOVERNANCE ARCHITECTURE:INSURANCE AND LIABILITY AS AN
INCLUSION MECHANISM
We propose a mechanism that can be written into an international agreement and implemented do-
mestically.
7.1 FRONTIER MODEL COVERAGE REQUIREMENT
A signatory state commits to require that any entity that deploys or trains models above a specified
capability or scale threshold must carry qualified coverage to operate in regulated markets and to
access public procurement. Coverage qualification includes: policy terms that cover a defined class
of model behavior harms and misuse harms, with bounded exclusions, adoption of baseline controls
and mandatory participation in standardized risk data reporting.
This pulls non state actors into the regime because operating without coverage becomes commer-
cially costly.
7.2 LIABILITY RULE WITH REPORTING SAFE HARBOR
Liability can destroy reporting incentives if disclosure increases expected damages. We propose
a two layer structure: Negligence based liability with a rebuttable presumption of negligence if
baseline controls and reporting are absent. A reporting safe harbor that reduces damages or limits
punitive multipliers when an organization demonstrates timely reporting, prompt remediation, and
adherence to baseline controls. This mirrors the idea of protected near miss reporting in safety
critical domains while maintaining strong penalties for reckless scaling or repeated negligence.
7.3 INTERNATIONAL COORDINATION WITHOUT DIRECT GLOBAL REGULATOR
The agreement creates an interoperability layer rather than a single global supervisor. A standard
for reporting fields and audit procedures. Mutual recognition of qualified insurance policies and
qualified auditors. A mechanism for cross border information sharing on systemic hazard classes,
without requiring disclosure of proprietary details. This structure is compatible with the reality that
enforcement capacity and legal regimes differ across states.

The exclusion boundary provides a practical design constraint for international agreements that aim
to include non-state actors through insurance and liability. Three implications follow directly from
the stress tests:

1. If a treaty or domestic regime requires insurance but does not require standardized reporting
and auditable controls, the likely result is formal compliance coupled with hollow policies
that exclude core hazards.
2. If a regime requires standardized reporting and conditions coverage on baseline controls,
insurance can become a meaningful lever, rewarding safety investments that are costly in
the short run and underprovided in competitive settings.
3. When correlated risk is high, reporting alone is insufficient. A systemic risk facility (indus-
try pool, reinsurance layer, or state-contingent backstop) is needed to prevent post-incident
market withdrawal. Eligibility for such a facility should be conditioned on reporting par-
ticipation and baseline controls to avoid subsidizing negligent or reckless deployment.
Interpretation of near universal exclusions.Figures 8 and 9 show exclusion rates close to one
under the present synthetic configuration and the illustrative thresholdT= 6. We interpret this
as a design constraint rather than as a forecast about the current insurance market. In regimes
with heavy tailed severity and correlated shocks, qualified coverage requirements that do not also
impose standardized reporting and auditable controls can induce insurers to narrow coverage or exit
precisely for the hazard classes most relevant to catastrophe prevention.
What shifts the exclusion boundary.The simulator highlights two governance levers that can
move the system toward stable coverage. First, richer underwriting information improves risk dif-
ferentiation: moving from Regime B to Regime C effectively adds standardized exposure and con-
trol fields (Table 1), which increases the ability to reward specific practices and reduces reliance on
outcome only signals. Second, correlated risk sharing reduces capital load: introducing a systemic
facility that absorbs a fractionbof losses in systemic shock years reduces tail capital pressure and
therefore reduces the incentive for insurers to impose broad exclusions. These levers are comple-
mentary: reporting and controls make premiums incentive compatible at the actor level, while a
systemic facility addresses correlated portfolio wide shocks that cannot be diversified away.
Concluding remark.The simulator shows that information design (what is reported and how un-
derwriting can condition on auditable practices) meaningfully shifts insurer incentives; however,
correlated heavy tails create parameter regimes where private markets alone will narrow coverage
without explicit systemic backstops or international risk-sharing arrangements.

10 POLICY RECOMMENDATIONS
This section derives implementable recommendations from the exclusion boundary analysis in Sec-
tion 8. Motivated by the exclusion pressures in Figures 8 and 9, we recommend that any qualified
coverage requirement be paired with standardized reporting fields and baseline controls (Table 1)
rather than relying on public visibility or post hoc incident discovery. Motivated by correlated risk
dominance in Section 8.2, we recommend that signatories include an option for a systemic risk fa-
cility or reinsurance layer that activates in systemic shock states and is conditioned on reporting
participation and control compliance.
Treaty level recommendations. Adopt a qualified coverage requirement for frontier model deploy-
ment and large scale training above defined thresholds. Define a minimal standardized reporting
interface for exposure, controls, and incidents. Require that qualified insurers include reporting
as a policy condition. Establish mutual recognition for auditors and qualified insurance terms to
avoid fragmented compliance regimes. Create a systemic risk facility option. This can be a pooled
reinsurance mechanism conditioned on reporting compliance and baseline controls.
10.1 DOMESTIC REGULATORY RECOMMENDATIONS
Clarify liability allocation across developer and deployer. Encourage contractual allocation but pre-
vent evasion through disclaimers that eliminate real incentives. Establish a reporting safe harbor.
The safe harbor should reduce damages when an actor demonstrates prompt reporting and remedia-
tion, and should not protect reckless scaling decisions. Require regulated critical sectors to purchase
only from covered deployments. This is a procurement lever that reaches non state actors directly.
Enable insurer access to confidential risk data under strict confidentiality rules. Without this, insur-
ers cannot price, and the governance mechanism cannot bite.
10.2 RECOMMENDATIONS FOR THE EMERGINGAIINSURANCE INDUSTRY
Standardize baseline controls as underwriting requirements. These should be auditable and should
focus on deployment conditions rather than on claims about alignment. Use correlation stress tests
in solvency analysis and premium setting. Treat systemic failures as the default rather than as an
edge case. Support pooled learning across insurers through anonymized aggregate statistics. If the
market remains fragmented, each insurer will have insufficient data and will respond by excluding.
11 LIMITATIONS AND FAILURE MODES
Strategic manipulation: organizations may under report or misclassify incidents. The proposed
regime relies on audit risk, contractual penalties, and cross validation with external signals. This is
imperfect but consistent with other compliance domains.
Excessive secrecy: confidentiality requirements may prevent public learning. The regime should
separate public aggregate hazard statistics from confidential incident specifics.Innovation suppres-
sion: poorly designed liability can chill legitimate research and safety improvement. Safe harbor
design is essential.Model evolution: risk factors change quickly. Standard fields must include
update cadence and post training modifications, and underwriting must be revision based.Interna-
tional asymmetry: non signatory states can act as havens. Market access and procurement levers
partially mitigate but do not eliminate this risk.

12 CONCLUSION
Non state actors are central to frontier AI capability and risk, and international agreements that fail
to include them will remain unstable. Insurance and liability frameworks can create durable in-
centives, but they are currently blocked by a missing data layer. This paper formalized that data
problem, provided a minimal set of underwriting relevant fields, and showed through a correlated
heavy tail simulation that reporting quality and exposure controls are first order determinants of
premium stability and incentive strength. The proposed mechanism uses qualified coverage require-
ments and safe harbor liability to bring companies and research institutions into a treaty compatible
compliance system without requiring disclosure of proprietary training data. The main practical
implication is that policy design should treat risk data standards and reporting incentives as core
infrastructure rather than as auxiliary transparency measures Reuel et al. (2025).

A.6 REPORTING REGIMESA, B,ANDC
We evaluate three reporting regimes. In the implementation, regimes are represented by shifts to the
reporting intercept and by which covariates are available to underwriting.
Regime A (public visibility only).Reporting probability is low. Underwriting sees a small frac-
tion of true incidents concentrated in the upper severity tail. Exposure and control fields are not
available for risk conditioning.
Regime B (standardized confidential reporting).Reporting probability is higher. Underwriting
sees most incidents above a low threshold. Exposure and control fields remain limited.
Regime C (standardized reporting plus exposure and controls).Reporting probability is higher
as in Regime B, and underwriting additionally observese i andc i (or proxies derived from standard-
ized reporting fields). This enables pricing and audit triggers to condition on auditable practices.

C ADDITIONAL NUMERIC OUTPUTS AND SENSITIVITY CHECKS
This appendix collects additional outputs that are omitted from the main paper for space. All results
are synthetic and are used to evaluate governance mechanisms rather than to estimate real world
premiums.
C.1 SENSITIVITY TO THRESHOLD CHOICE
The main text usesT= 6as an illustrative exclusion threshold. To check robustness, we rec-
ommend varyingTover a plausible range and reporting exclusion rates as a function ofT. This
distinguishes cases where near universal exclusions are driven by the selected threshold from cases
where exclusions persist across thresholds due to heavy tails and correlation.
C.2 SENSITIVITY TO TAIL PERCENTILE CHOICE
The capital load proxy usesCVaR q at a high percentile. Varyingq(for example0.95,0.99,0.995)
tests whether conclusions are driven by extreme tail emphasis. In regimes where correlated risk
dominates, exclusion pressure should remain high even under moderateq.
C.3 SENSITIVITY TO CORRELATED RISK SHARING
The systemic facility is modeled as a shock state payout shareb. Varyingbillustrates how much
correlated risk pooling is required before coverage becomes stable. This sensitivity directly maps to
governance design choices such as pooled facilities, reinsurance layers, or state contingent backstops
with eligibility conditions.
