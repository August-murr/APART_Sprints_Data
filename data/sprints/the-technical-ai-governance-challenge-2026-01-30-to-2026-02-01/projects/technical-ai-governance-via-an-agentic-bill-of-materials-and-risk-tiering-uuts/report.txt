Abstract
The proliferation of agentic AI systems, autonomous agents with tool access, persistent memory,
and self-directed goal pursuit, poses governance challenges that existing regulatory frameworks do
not adequately address. We present a comprehensive technical governance framework comprising
three components: the Agentic Bill of Materials (ABOM), a machine-readable schema documenting
an agent’s capabilities, autonomy settings, and safety controls; a Risk Scoring Engine that computes
a quantitative risk magnitudeR=A×U×e P ×Mfrom Agency, Autonomy, Persistence, and Miti-
gation vectors; and the Unified Agentic Risk Tiering (UART) system, a five-tier classification aligned
with EU AI Act risk categories and international standards. We validate this framework through a
reference implementation and systematic evaluation across six agent configurations spanning all tiers,
achieving 100% classification accuracy. The framework enables “verification without trust” through
hardware-based attestation mechanisms, allowing regulators to cryptographically verify agent config-
urations rather than relying on operator declarations. Our open-source implementation demonstrates
practical applicability for international AI governance.
1 Introduction
The emergence of agentic AI systems autonomous ie. agents capable of tool use, persistent memory,
and self-directed goal pursuit, represents a fundamental shift in the AI risk landscape. Unlike tradi-
tional AI systems that merely generate outputs for human review, agentic systems canacton the world:
provisioning cloud infrastructure, executing financial transactions, modifying databases, and even con-
trolling physical systems. This transition from passive inference to active operation demands equally
transformative governance frameworks.
Current regulatory approaches, including the EU AI Act’s risk-based classification, provide a founda-
tion but lack the technical specificity needed to evaluate and tier autonomous agents. How do we quantify
the risk of an agent that can persist knowledge across sessions, operate without human oversight, and
invoke dozens of external APIs? How do regulators verify that an agent’s declared capabilities match its
actual configuration?
We present a comprehensive technical governance framework addressing these challenges through
three interlocking components:
1.Agentic Bill of Materials (ABOM): A standardized, machine-readable schema documenting an
agent’s model architecture, tool integrations, autonomy settings, memory mechanisms, and safety
scaffolding, extending the EU’s Model Documentation Form to capture features unique to agentic
systems.
2.Risk Scoring Engine: A quantitative formulaR=A×U×e P ×Mthat computes risk magnitude
from Agency (A), Autonomy (U), Persistence (P), and Mitigation (M) vectors, with exponential
weighting for long-term memory to reflect compounding hazards.
3.Unified Agentic Risk Tiering (UAR T): A five-tier framework (Tier 0–4) mapping risk scores
to escalating governance requirements, aligned with EU AI Act risk categories and international
standards like SAE J3016 and ISA/IEC 62443.

2 Related Work
Our framework builds upon and synthesizes advances across several domains: AI governance and regu-
lation, software supply chain security, risk tiering methodologies, and agent architectures.
2.1 AI Governance and Regulation
The EU AI Act represents the most comprehensive regulatory framework for AI to date, establishing
risk-based classifications and mandatory requirements for high-risk systems [1]. The Act introduces the
concept of General Purpose AI (GPAI) with systemic risk, defined by a training compute threshold of
1025 FLOPs, which directly informs our Tier 4 override mechanism. Concurrently, leading AI laboratories
have developed internal governance frameworks. Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy [2] introduces AI
Safety Levels (ASLs) modeled on biosafety levels, defining capability thresholds that trigger enhanced
safeguards. OpenAI’s Preparedness Framework [3] similarly defines tracked risk categories including
autonomous replication and CBRN capabilities. Our UART framework complements these efforts by
providing a quantitative, externally auditable risk score rather than relying solely on internal assessments.
2.2 Software Transparency and Documentation
The Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) concept, formalized by NTIA in 2021 [7] following Executive
Order 14028 [10], establishes precedent for machine-readable documentation of software components to
enhance supply chain security. Our ABOM schema extends this paradigm to agentic AI, capturing not
only model provenance but also the dynamic capabilities (tools, autonomy, memory) that determine
an agent’s risk profile. The EU AI Act’s Model Documentation Form provides additional structure for
AI-specific documentation requirements, which we incorporate and extend.
2.3 Safety and Security Level Frameworks
Our tiered governance approach draws inspiration from established safety level frameworks in adjacent
domains. SAE J3016 [5] defines six levels of driving automation based on the allocation of dynamic
driving tasks between human and system, providing a precedent for our HITL/HOTL/HOOTL auton-
omy taxonomy. ISA/IEC 62443 [6] establishes security levels for industrial control systems based on
adversary sophistication, informing our graduated governance requirements across tiers. The Trusted
Computing Group’s TPM specifications [8] provide the technical foundation for our hardware attestation
mechanisms.
2.4 Agentic AI Architectures
Recent work on agentic AI systems has demonstrated both their capabilities and risks. The ReAct
framework [9] established patterns for interleaving reasoning and action in language models, enabling
tool use and multi-step planning. Anthropic’s research on building effective agents [11] documents archi-
tectural patterns including memory, tool integration, and orchestration scaffolding that directly inform
our ABOM schema. Emerging research on agentic risks highlights concerns around goal misalignment,
loss of control, and security vulnerabilities that motivate the governance mechanisms we propose.
2.5 Positioning
While prior work addresses individual aspects of AI governance (documentation, risk classification, ver-
ification), no existing framework provides an integrated, quantitative approach specifically designed for
agentic systems. Our contribution lies in synthesizing these elements into a cohesive technical framework
with a reference implementation, enabling practical adoption by regulators, auditors, and operators.

3 Methods
Our governance framework comprises four interconnected components: a standardized documentation
schema, a quantitative risk scoring engine, a tiered classification system, and cryptographic verification
mechanisms. We describe each component and its theoretical foundations below.
3.1 Agentic Bill of Materials
The Agentic Bill of Materials (ABOM) extends existing AI documentation requirements, notably the EU
AI Act’s Model Documentation Form to capture attributes unique to autonomous agents. The ABOM
is implemented as a machine-readable JSON schema containing five principal sections.
TheModel Coresection documents the underlying foundation model, including architecture type,
parameter count, and training compute expressed in floating-point operations (FLOPs). This metric
enables identification of frontier models: the EU AI Act designates systems trained on more than 10 25
FLOPs as potentially posing systemic risk and subject to heightened obligations.
TheAgency Profileenumerates external tools, APIs, and system permissions accessible to the agent.
Each tool entry specifies whether it is read-only or state-changing, the latter category encompassing
operations that modify persistent state or trigger irreversible actions. The profile also captures capability
flags for dangerous functionalities such as CBRN access or self-replication.
TheAutonomy Leashdeclares the agent’s human oversight mode using a three-level taxonomy:
Human-in-the-Loop (HITL), where approval is required before executing high-impact actions; Human-
on-the-Loop (HOTL), where autonomous operation proceeds under continuous monitoring with interven-
tion capability; and Human-out-of-the-Loop (HOOTL), where the agent operates independently within
predefined constraints.
ThePersistence Layerspecifies memory mechanisms and retention duration. We distinguish ephemeral
memory (cleared after each invocation), session-only memory (retained within a bounded interaction),
and cross-session or long-term memory (persisting across interactions indefinitely).
TheScaffolding Inventorydocuments safety controls including use of standardized tool interfaces
(Model Context Protocol), sandboxed execution environments, and circuit breakers or kill-switches.
3.2 Risk Scoring Engine
We derive four risk vectors from the ABOM and combine them into a scalar risk magnitude through the
following formulation:
R=A×U×e P ×M(1)
The Agency scoreAquantifies the potential impact of the agent’s accessible actions. We assign
A= 1 for agents without tools,A= 2 for read-only tool access,A= 4 for state-changing capabilities,
andA= 6 for critical or dangerous capabilities including CBRN access and self-replication.
The Autonomy scoreUreflects the degree of human oversight. Following the established taxonomy,
we assignU= 1 for HITL,U= 2 for HOTL, andU= 3 for HOOTL configurations.
The Persistence scorePcaptures memory duration, withP= 0 for ephemeral,P= 1 for session-
only, andP= 2 for long-term memory. The exponential terme P reflects our assessment that persistent
memory compounds risk non-linearly: an agent accumulating knowledge across sessions can develop
increasingly sophisticated strategies that may circumvent controls designed for stateless systems.
The Mitigation modifierMranges from 0.7 to 1.0, rewarding agents that implement safety scaffolding.
Each control—MCP usage, sandboxing, and circuit breakers—reducesMmultiplicatively, with a floor
of 0.7 representing maximum achievable risk reduction through scaffolding alone.
3.3 Unified Agentic Risk Tiering
Computed risk scores map to five discrete governance tiers. Tier 0 applies to passive systems with
R= 0. Tier 1 (Assistive) encompasses scores in the range 0< R <5, requiring logging and trans-
parency. Tier 2 (Bounded Autonomy) covers 5≤R <20, mandating guardrails and anomaly detection.
Tier 3 (High-Agency) applies to 20≤R <50, requiring continuous monitoring and periodic audits.
Tier 4 (Systemic Risk) capturesR≥50 and demands hardware-enforced governance, including TPM
attestation, immutable audit logs, and regulator oversight.

Independent of computed scores, two override conditions automatically trigger Tier 4 classification:
training compute exceeding 1025 FLOPs, and presence of critical capability flags (CBRN, self-replication,
autonomous weapons) in the agency profile.
3.4 Verification Mechanisms
The framework employs three enforcement layers to enable what we term “verification without trust.”
The Model Context Protocol standardizes tool invocation, enabling policy enforcement at the interface
layer. Capability-based access control issues short-lived, scoped tokens that authorize specific actions,
enforcing least privilege even if the agent attempts unauthorized operations. Hardware-based attestation
through Trusted Platform Modules and secure enclaves provides cryptographic verification of agent
configuration, execution integrity, and audit log immutability.
4 Results
We validated the ABOM Risk Scoring Engine through a systematic evaluation of six agent configurations
designed to span the full UART tier spectrum. Each configuration represents a distinct agent archetype
commonly encountered in production deployments.
4.1 Experimental Setup
The test suite comprises agents ranging from passive chatbots (no tools, human-in-the-loop oversight,
ephemeral memory) to fully autonomous frontier systems with self-replication capabilities. For each
agent, we constructed an ABOM manifest specifying its model core, agency profile, autonomy leash,
persistence layer, and scaffolding inventory. The scoring engine processed each manifest and computed
the risk magnitudeRaccording to Equation 1, subsequently mapping the score to the appropriate UART
tier.
4.2 Validation Results
Table 1 presents the computed risk vectors and tier classifications for all six test cases. The minimal
risk configuration (A= 1,U= 1,P= 0,M= 0.85) yieldedR= 0.85, correctly classifying as Tier 1
(Assistive). The moderate risk configuration (A= 4,U= 3,P= 1,M= 0.765) producedR= 24.95,
appropriately placing it in Tier 3 (High-Agency).

The exponential persistence terme P demonstrated the intended amplification effect. Comparing

4.3 Override Mechanism Validation
Three test cases exercised the systemic risk override pathway. Two configurations exceeded the 10 25
FLOPs training compute threshold derived from EU AI Act guidelines for general-purpose AI with
systemic risk. The third configuration included self-replication in its capability flags. In all three cases,
the engine correctly elevated the classification to Tier 4 regardless of the computedRvalue, validating
the override mechanism’s reliability.

4.4 Mitigation Factor Analysis
The mitigation modifierMreduced risk scores proportionally to the presence of safety controls. Config-
urations employing MCP-standardized tool invocation, sandboxed execution environments, and circuit
breakers achievedMvalues as low as 0.765, representing a 23.5% risk reduction from baseline. This
reduction, while insufficient to change tier classification in most cases, provides meaningful incentive for
operators to implement safety scaffolding.
All six test cases achieved 100% classification accuracy against expected outcomes, validating both
the scoring formula and tier boundary definitions.
5 Discussion and Limitations
The ABOM Risk Scoring Engine represents a first step toward quantitative, enforceable governance for
autonomous AI agents. Our results demonstrate that the framework can reliably differentiate agents
across risk tiers while providing appropriate incentives for safety-enhancing design choices. We discuss
implications, limitations, and directions for future work.
5.1 Implications for AI Governance
The framework addresses a critical gap in current regulatory approaches: the absence of standardized,
machine-readable documentation for agentic systems. While the EU AI Act establishes requirements
for technical documentation and risk assessment, it provides limited guidance on how to operationalize
these requirements for agents that combine foundation models with tool access, memory, and autonomous
decision-making. The ABOM schema offers a concrete specification that regulators, auditors, and oper-
ators can adopt immediately.
The exponential treatment of persistence merits particular attention. Our formulation reflects the
assessment that long-term memory fundamentally changes an agent’s risk profile—not merely by enabling
better task performance, but by creating opportunities for strategic behavior that stateless systems
cannot exhibit. An agent that accumulates context across sessions may identify patterns in oversight,
optimize for reduced intervention, or develop plans spanning timescales that exceed human attention.
Thee P term encodes this concern directly into the governance framework.
The mitigation modifierMoperationalizes a principle implicit in existing safety discourse: that
architectural choices matter for risk. By reducing computed risk scores for agents employing MCP,
sandboxing, and circuit breakers, the framework creates quantifiable incentives for operators to adopt
safety-enhancing scaffolding. This approach aligns technical design decisions with regulatory outcomes.
5.2 Limitations
Several limitations constrain the current framework. First, the ABOM relies on accurate self-reporting
by operators. While hardware attestation can verify certain configuration parameters, the framework
cannot currently detect undisclosed tools or capabilities. Adversarial operators could underreport agency
or misrepresent autonomy settings.
Second, the tier thresholds (R= 5,20,50) are illustrative rather than empirically derived. We
selected values that produce intuitive tier distributions across our test cases, but optimal thresholds
would require extensive empirical calibration against real-world agent deployments and incident data.
Different jurisdictions or application domains may require adjusted boundaries.
Third, the scoring formula treats all state-changing tools equivalently within their category. In
practice, an agent with access to a database write operation poses different risks than one capable of
executing arbitrary shell commands. Future versions should incorporate tool-specific impact weights
derived from domain expertise or incident analysis.
Fourth, the framework does not address emergent capabilities. An agent’s actual risk may exceed
what its ABOM documentation would predict if the underlying model develops unexpected behaviors
through scale, fine-tuning, or novel prompting strategies. The framework governs declared capabilities
rather than latent ones.
Finally, the reference implementation validates the scoring logic but does not include the hardware
attestation mechanisms described in Section 2.4. A production deployment would require integration
with TPM infrastructure and secure enclave environments.

5.3 Future Work
Several extensions would strengthen the framework. Continuous monitoring could supplement static
ABOM analysis, flagging behavioral deviations from declared capabilities. Federated registries could
enable cross-organizational sharing of ABOM data while preserving confidentiality through cryptographic
techniques. Automated ABOM generation from agent source code could reduce documentation burden
and improve accuracy. Empirical studies of deployed agents could inform evidence-based refinement of
tier thresholds and vector weights.
6 Conclusion
We presented a technical governance framework for agentic AI systems comprising the Agentic Bill of
Materials (ABOM), a quantitative risk scoring engine, and the Unified Agentic Risk Tiering (UART)
classification system. Our validation across six agent configurations demonstrated that the formula
R=A×U×e P ×Mreliably differentiates agents across risk tiers, with the exponential persistence term
appropriately amplifying risks associated with long-term memory and the mitigation modifier providing
quantifiable incentives for safety scaffolding. The systemic risk override mechanism correctly elevated
frontier agents exceeding 10 25 FLOPs or possessing critical capabilities to Tier 4 classification.
The framework addresses an urgent gap in AI governance: the absence of standardized, machine-
readable documentation for autonomous agents capable of acting on the world. By embedding governance
into the technical substrate of agentic systems—through structured manifests, cryptographic verification,
and hardware-enforced controls—this approach enables scalable, enforceable oversight that does not rely
on operator promises alone. As agentic AI systems proliferate across critical domains, frameworks like
ABOM and UART provide a foundation for international harmonization of safety standards, ensuring
that the transformative potential of autonomous agents is realized within appropriate guardrails.
