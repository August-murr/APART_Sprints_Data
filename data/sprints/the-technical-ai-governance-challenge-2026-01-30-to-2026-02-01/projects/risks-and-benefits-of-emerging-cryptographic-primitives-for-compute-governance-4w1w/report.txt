Risks and Beneﬁts of Emerging Cryptographic
Primitives for Compute Governance
Harry Powell
Independent Researcher
Submitted to The Technical AI Governance Challenge (Apart Research)
Abstract
International cooperation on AI safety requires veriﬁcation without surveillance. We examine how emerging
cryptographic primitives - Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) and Indistinguishability Obfuscation (iO) - could
enable privacy-preserving compute governance.
We present two protocols of increasing strength. The ﬁrst uses FHE to allow compute providers to enforce safety
policies on encrypted programs, preserving user privacy while requiring hardware veriﬁcation for provider
compliance. The second adds obfuscation to provide cryptographic guarantees against user-provider collusion,
reducing hardware veriﬁcation to boundary monitoring.
Neither protocol requires users to disclose their programs in plaintext. Both point toward a future where
cryptography and physical veriﬁcation work together to govern powerful computation.
To validate this capability, we include a reference implementation of the second protocol’s architecture. This proof-
of-concept utilizes Zero-Knowledge proofs (Schnorr) and ElGamal encryption to demonstrate that boundary
veriﬁcation can be enforced cryptographically without ever decrypting the user's workload.
1. Introduction
Compute governance faces a fundamental tension: veriﬁcation requires visibility, but visibility compromises
privacy. Current proposals typically require users to disclose what they're running, or require inspectors to observe
computation directly. This creates resistance from users with legitimate conﬁdentiality interests and from nations
wary of surveillance.
Emerging cryptographic primitives oﬀer a diﬀerent approach. Rather than choosing between privacy and
veriﬁcation, we can construct protocols that provide both - with diﬀerent primitives oﬀering diﬀerent tradeoﬀs.
This paper examines two primitives:
• Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE): Allows computation on encrypted data. Practical applications exist
today; general-purpose FHE is approaching feasibility.
• Indistinguishability Obfuscation (iO): Allows programs to be made unintelligible while preserving
functionality. Less mature than FHE, but theoretically powerful.
We present protocols using each primitive and analyze their threat models. The goal is not to propose

2. Fully Homomorphic Encryption
2.1 What is FHE?
FHE allows computation on encrypted data without decryption.
A user encrypts input, sends it to an evaluator, and receives an encrypted result. The evaluator performs
computation on ciphertext, never seeing plaintext. Only the user, holding the secret key, can decrypt the result.
The core operations:
• KeyGen(): Generate a public/secret keypair
• Encrypt(PK, m): Encrypt plaintext m under public key
• Decrypt(SK, c): Decrypt ciphertext c with secret key
• Eval(PK, f, c): Evaluate function f on ciphertext, producing encrypted result
The key property: decrypting the evaluated ciphertext yields f(m). The evaluator learns nothing about m or
f(m).
2.2 Current State
FHE is not practical for general computation. Current implementations impose 1,000x to 10,000x overhead
compared to plaintext computation.
However, limited applications are deployed today: private set intersection, encrypted database queries,
conﬁdential machine learning inference. The boundary of practicality continues to expand.
2.3 Trajectory
Several trends suggest general-purpose FHE may become practical:
• Hardware acceleration. Custom ASICs and FPGAs for FHE operations are under development.
• Algorithmic improvements. Successive schemes (BGV, BFV, CKKS, TFHE) have brought signiﬁcant
performance gains.
• Convergent infrastructure. The compute infrastructure driving AI capabilities may accelerate FHE feasibility.
3. The Risk: Invisible Computation
FHE allows users to submit encrypted programs, have them executed, and receive encrypted results. The
compute provider learns nothing about what ran.

===== page 3 =====

This creates a governance challenge:
• Regulators cannot inspect what programs execute
• Safety policies cannot be enforced if content is invisible
• International agreements cannot be veriﬁed if computation is opaque
If computation becomes invisible, how can we govern AI development?
4. The Opportunity: Privacy-Preserving Veriﬁcation
The key insight: users want privacy, but they still need compute providers. Providers control what they run.
FHE does not allow users to bypass providers. It allows providers to enforce policy without seeing the content
they process - and crucially, it allows users to submit to safety veriﬁcation without surrendering their privacy.
This is the core contribution: safety checking without plaintext disclosure.
Without FHE, veriﬁcation requires users to reveal their programs. With FHE, users can prove compliance while
keeping their code conﬁdential. Neither party compromises what they seek to protect.
5. Protocol 1: FHE-Based Safety Veriﬁcation
5.1 Overview
The provider publishes a safety-checking circuit. Users submit encrypted programs. The provider evaluates the
circuit homomorphically, returning encrypted results only if safety checks pass.
5.2 Participants
• User: Wants to run a program privately
• Provider: Enforces agreed safety policy
• Observer: International body verifying compliance
5.3 Key Management
The user generates the FHE keypair and retains the secret key exclusively. Neither provider nor observer holds
decryption capability.

2. Provider deploys circuit:  f(x) = is_safe(x) ? exec(x) : ⊥

5.8 Limitations
The protocol requires continuous internal inspection of compute facilities. Observers must verify that the correct
circuit is deployed and that all computation routes through it. This is a signiﬁcant operational burden.
Additionally, the protocol does not prevent a colluding user and provider from bypassing the safety checker
entirely - they could simply agree to run unveriﬁed code. Defense against collusion rests entirely on physical
oversight.

6. Indistinguishability Obfuscation
6.1 What is iO?
Indistinguishability obfuscation transforms a program into an equivalent program that is unintelligible - an
adversary cannot extract the original logic or any embedded secrets.
Formally, an obfuscator O satisﬁes: for any two programs P1 and P2 that compute the same function, O(P1) and
O(P2) are computationally indistinguishable. An adversary looking at an obfuscated program cannot tell which
equivalent source it came from.
The key property for our purposes: secrets embedded in a program before obfuscation cannot be extracted
afterward. The obfuscated program can use the secret internally, but no adversary can recover it.
6.2 Current State
iO is less mature than FHE. Theoretical constructions exist, but practical implementations remain distant. The
cryptographic assumptions underlying iO are less battle-tested than those for FHE.
However, iO is a subject of active research. If realized practically, it would be a powerful primitive for constructing
secure protocols.
6.3 Why It Matters for Governance
iO allows us to construct programs that enforce policies even against the party running them. A compute provider
running an obfuscated safety checker cannot bypass the safety check or extract embedded keys - they can only
execute the program as a black box.
This shifts trust requirements. Instead of trusting the provider to run the correct code, we trust the obfuscation to
make cheating impossible.
7. Protocol 2: Obfuscation-Based Safety Veriﬁcation
7.1 Overview
An obfuscated safety checker is constructed via multi-party ceremony. The checker contains an embedded
private key that no party knows. Users encrypt submissions to the corresponding public key. Only the obfuscated
checker can decrypt and process submissions. Even colluding users and providers cannot bypass the safety
check.
7.2 Participants
• User: Wants to run a program privately
• Provider: Runs the obfuscated checker
• Observer: Monitors network boundary

7.4 Ceremony
The ceremony uses standard multi-party computation (MPC) techniques. Multiple parties jointly generate a keypair
such that no party learns the secret key. The secret key is embedded into the safety checker program via MPC,
then the combined program is obfuscated. After obfuscation, key shares are deleted. The secret key exists only
inside the obfuscated program, inaccessible to any party.
This approach has precedent. The Zcash trusted setup used a similar ceremony to generate parameters where
security holds if any single participant behaved honestly.

7.6 Execution with Boundary Veriﬁcation
Users attach a zero-knowledge proof that their submission is encrypted under CPK. Observers at the network
boundary verify these proofs, rejecting any submission that cannot be proven to target the obfuscated checker.
Zero-knowledge proofs of correct encryption are standard for discrete-log-based schemes (e.g., ElGamal) and an
active research area for lattice-based schemes.

7.10 Remaining Physical Veriﬁcation
The obfuscation protocol does not eliminate physical veriﬁcation entirely. Observers must still verify:
• Boundary integrity: The monitored network path is the only path into and out of the facility
• No oﬀ-protocol computation: The provider isn't ignoring the obfuscated checker and running unrelated
computation
However, observers no longer need to verify that the safety checker runs correctly. If traﬃc enters the facility
encrypted to CPK, it can only be processed by the obfuscated checker. The cryptography enforces correct
execution.
Periodic audits verify that nothing else is happening. Boundary monitoring veriﬁes that all I/O is protocol-
compliant. Continuous internal inspection is not required.
8. The Safety Checker
Both protocols enforce  is_safe()  without specifying its implementation. The safety function is a matter for treaty
negotiation; the protocols provide infrastructure to enforce whatever policy is agreed.
8.1 Implementation Approaches
Practical implementations could combine:
• Dependency veriﬁcation. Hash submitted dependencies against an approved registry. Unknown
dependencies are rejected.
• Structural analysis. Measure code complexity. Simpler programs are easier to verify; obfuscated code is
penalized or rejected.
• Language model review. Analyze code for dangerous patterns or suspicious intent.
• Conservative rejection. When uncertain, reject.
8.2 Fundamental Limits
No safety checker can be complete. Suﬃciently complex programs can encode arbitrary behavior; suﬃciently
obfuscated code can evade static analysis. This is a consequence of undecidability (Rice's theorem), not a ﬂaw in
the protocols.
The response is conservatism: reject what cannot be veriﬁed. A safety checker that rejects all uncertain programs
is sound, even if incomplete. The cost is false negatives (safe programs rejected), not false positives (unsafe
programs approved).
This biases the system toward simpler, more transparent code - arguably a feature for safety-critical computation.

9. Limitations
9.1 Cryptographic Maturity
FHE imposes 1,000-10,000x overhead. General-purpose FHE is not yet practical, though the trajectory is
promising.
iO is less mature still. Theoretical constructions exist but practical implementations are distant. The obfuscation
protocol is more speculative than the FHE protocol.
9.2 Safety Veriﬁcation
No protocol can verify arbitrary programs. The safety checker must be conservative, accepting incompleteness to
ensure soundness. This limits the expressiveness of programs that can be approved.
9.3 Physical Veriﬁcation
Neither protocol eliminates physical veriﬁcation entirely:
• FHE protocol: Requires internal inspection to verify provider compliance
• Obfuscation protocol: Requires boundary monitoring and periodic audits to prevent oﬀ-protocol
computation
Physical oversight aligns with existing international veriﬁcation regimes. The novelty is reducing what must be
physically veriﬁed, not eliminating veriﬁcation.
9.4 Ceremony Trust
The obfuscation protocol trusts the ceremony to be conducted honestly. If all ceremony participants collude, they
could retain the secret key and undermine the protocol. Mitigations include:
• Large number of participants from diverse, adversarial entities
• Veriﬁable ceremony protocols with public transcripts
• Multiple independent ceremonies with threshold schemes
10. Conclusion
Emerging cryptographic primitives create both risks and opportunities for compute governance.
The risk: FHE and iO make computation opaque. Programs can execute without inspection. Traditional
veriﬁcation fails.
The opportunity: The same primitives can enforce safety policies without compromising privacy. Users submit
encrypted programs. Safety is veriﬁed without disclosure. Neither party sees what the other protects.
We presented two protocols:

===== page 15 =====

1. FHE-based veriﬁcation: Provider enforces safety checks on encrypted programs. Cryptographic guarantees
against malicious users. Physical veriﬁcation for provider compliance. More conservative assumptions; closer
to practical.
2. Obfuscation-based veriﬁcation: Safety checker embedded in obfuscated program with ceremony-
generated key. Cryptographic guarantees against both malicious users and collusion. Physical veriﬁcation
reduced to boundary monitoring. Stronger guarantees; less mature foundations.
Both protocols demonstrate that privacy and safety are not fundamentally opposed. The question is not
whether to verify, but how to verify without surveillance.
The required components:
• Practical FHE (emerging)
• Practical iO (further out, for the stronger protocol)
• Physical veriﬁcation infrastructure (exists today in other domains)
• International agreement on safety policies (political, not technical)
Given these, we can govern powerful computation without requiring users to sacriﬁce privacy or nations to
accept surveillance.
Neither party sees what the other protects. Both get what they need.

A.6 Signiﬁcance
This implementation validates the veriﬁcation envelope of Protocol 2. While the execution core relies on future
obfuscation capabilities, we have validated that the surrounding infrastructure - zero-knowledge proofs for
submission compliance, boundary enforcement without decryption, and the secure transport lifecycle - is
functional today.
The proof-of-concept conﬁrms that we can successfully govern the entry and exit of a compute facility using

===== page 18 =====

cryptography rather than surveillance. The missing link is not the architecture, but the maturity of a speciﬁc
primitive (iO).
The path from this demo to production follows three parallel tracks:
1. Cryptographic Ceremony: Replacing the local key generation with a distributed MPC ceremony to
permanently seal the decryption capabilities.
2. Execution Hardening: Transitioning from the simulated black box to practical iO or cryptographic hardware
equivalents as the technology matures.
3. Governance Deﬁnitions: Augmenting the heuristic safety checker (currently an LLM) with formal veriﬁcation
suites and internationally agreed safety standards.
This working system validates the following core insight:
If you can generate an obfuscated program via ceremony containing an inaccessible private key, encrypted
packets can advertise a proof that they will only ever execute via that program, meaning they can
cryptographically prove they will undergo safety validation before execution.
This architecture measures compliance with safety protocols at the network level, enabling multiple parties to
perform cheap distributed inspection without compromising privacy.
