SafetyGap: Coordination Infrastructure, Auditing and Tools
for Multilingual AI Safety
Martin CL Alyssia J
With Apart Research
Abstract
The EU AI Act requires evaluation of general-purpose AI models, yet compliant evaluation for
bias detection is currently impossible in 19 of 24 official EU languages. The International Network
of AI Safety Institutes needs shared visibility into what evaluation infrastructure exists to coordi-
nate effectively. We audited multilingual coverage for 15 major safety benchmarks, verifying claims
against primary sources (papers, GitHub repositories, HuggingFace) and cataloging language avail-
ability across 7 risk categories. We found a stark divide: truthfulness and toxicity benchmarks extend
to 17–21 languages, but bias detection, adversarial robustness, and over-refusal benchmarks remain
almost entirely English-only. Models serving over 6 billion non-English speakers have never been
tested for these risks in local languages. We release SafetyGap, a public database and dashboard
covering all languages in our audit. The open-source infrastructure is available to all members of
the International Network—the US, UK, Japan, Singapore, Canada, France, Kenya, South Korea,
and the EU among them—to check coverage before commissioning translations and coordinate on
filling gaps. It is built to be easily extendable.
1 Introduction
A model deployed in India may refuse harmful requests in English while complying in Hindi. No
benchmark exists to systematically test this. Research shows that safety properties often fail to transfer
across languages [Deng et al., 2024, Yong et al., 2024], yet Hindi and Arabic speakers (over 1.5 billion
people combined) use AI models that have never been evaluated for bias, over-refusal, or adversarial
robustness in their languages.
The EU AI Act requires evaluation of general-purpose AI models. Our data shows that compliant
evaluation for bias detection is currently impossible in 19 of 24 official EU languages (Figure 1). This is
both a compliance risk for AI developers and a coordination opportunity for the International Network
of AI Safety Institutes.
No systematic map exists showing which safety benchmarks cover which languages. Without shared
visibility into what evaluation infrastructure already exists, institutes risk duplicating effort or assuming
coverage exists when it doesn’t.
We conducted an audit of multilingual safety benchmark coverage. Our contributions:
1.A verified databaseof 15 major safety benchmarks with language coverage confirmed against pri-
mary sources (papers, GitHub, HuggingFace)
2.A systematic auditshowing that three risk categories (bias, over-refusal, adversarial robustness)
have near-zero multilingual coverage, roughly 40% of documented safety risk categories with no
evaluation capacity outside English
3.A deployed dashboardwhere AI Safety Institutes can query coverage by language or risk category
before allocating resources
*Research conducted at the Technical AI Governance Challenge, 2026

2 Related Work
Multilingual safety benchmarks have started to appear. XSafety [Wang et al., 2024] evaluates safety
across 10 languages including English, Chinese, and Arabic. MultiJail [Deng et al., 2024] tests jailbreak
resistance in 10 languages. PolygloToxicityPrompts [Jain et al., 2024] addresses toxicity in 17 languages
using native content collection rather than translation. The EU20 benchmark suite [Thellmann et al.,
2024] provides machine translations of TruthfulQA and other benchmarks for 20 European languages.
M-ALERT [Friedrich et al., 2024] extends the ALERT taxonomy to 5 languages with 75,000 prompts.
But coverage information is scattered across papers and repositories. SafetyPrompts.com catalogs
safety datasets but does not track language availability. No existing resource answers a simple question:
for a given language and risk category, what benchmarks exist?
This matters for coordination. Without shared visibility, AI Safety Institutes will duplicate transla-
tion efforts or miss gaps by assuming someone else has already built the infrastructure.

3 Methods
3.1 Benchmark Selection
We surveyed 30 candidate benchmarks from SafetyPrompts.com, ACL Anthology, and arXiv. We kept
benchmarks that were publicly available, targeted safety-relevant risks (jailbreaks, toxicity, bias, truth-
fulness, over-refusal, adversarial robustness), and factored citation frequency. This left us with 15 bench-
marks spanning 7 risk categories.
3.2 Verification Methodology
For each benchmark, we verified language coverage against primary sources:
1.Original paper: Checked stated language coverage and methodology
2.Official repository: Verified actual data files and language directories on GitHub
3.HuggingFace listing: Cross-referenced dataset cards for language tags
4.SafetyPrompts.com: Compared against catalog entries where available
We recorded the original language, all verified languages with translation method (machine transla-
tion vs. native collection), risk category, verification date, and source links.
3.3 Dashboard Implementation
We built a dashboard that lets users answer (“What benchmarks exist in German?”) or by risk category
(“What multilingual coverage exists for bias detection?”). The underlying database (benchmarks.json)
uses a structured schema for programmatic access.

5 Discussion and Limitations
5.1 Theory of Change
Immediate: AI Safety Institutes preparing for EU AI Act compliance can see exactly which evaluations
are currently impossible in which languages. An institute in France planning bias evaluation no longer
needs a literature review to discover that BBQ has no French version. SafetyGap shows this directly,
along with what alternatives exist.
Medium-term: The International Network can use SafetyGap to coordinate benchmark develop-
ment. Right now, ten countries could independently commission BBQ translations without knowing
the others are doing the same. SafetyGap makes current coverage visible so institutes can divide the
work: one takes bias benchmarks for Romance languages, another handles adversarial robustness for
East Asian languages.
SafetyGap is designed to be extended. The database schema and verification methodology are open-
source. As new benchmarks appear or institutes develop translations, contributors can add them. The
same infrastructure pattern (systematic audit, structured data, public access) could apply to other co-
ordination problems the Network faces: tracking red-teaming methodologies, sharing incident reports,
cataloging evaluation protocols. More broadly, this pattern fits other technical AI governance challenges:
hardware verification registries, compliance infrastructure for compute thresholds, and capability eval-
uation catalogues all face similar problems of scattered information and duplicated effort. SafetyGap
demonstrates one approach to solving them.
Long-term: The goal is AI systems that are actually safe for everyone, not just English speakers.
A model can pass every major safety benchmark and still be untested for bias, adversarial attacks, or
over-refusal in Hindi, Arabic, or 19 EU languages. SafetyGap makes these gaps visible. Visibility is the

first step. Coordinated benchmark development is the second. Models evaluated in the languages they
are deployed in is the third.
Effective AI governance requires knowing what safety infrastructure exists, where, and for whom.
That information is currently scattered across papers, repositories, and institutional knowledge. Safe-
tyGap is a proof of concept for what technical governance coordination could look like: verified data,
open access, shared across jurisdictions. If the International Network is going to function as more than
a mailing list, it needs shared infrastructure. We hope this is a step toward that.
5.2 Validation - Case Study
To test utility, we simulated a plausible task: an AI Safety Institute planning multilingual bias evaluation
for EU deployment. Without SafetyGap, a literature review would be needed to discover that BBQ has
no official multilingual version, only scattered cultural adaptations (KoBBQ, JBBQ, MBBQ) covering 4
of 24 EU languages. SafetyGap surfaces this gap in one place.
5.3 Limitations
Scope: We cataloged 15 benchmarks. More exist, especially from non-English research communities
that our methodology likely undercounted.
Quality vs. existence: We verified that benchmarks exist in claimed languages but did not assess
translation quality or cultural validity.
Point-in-time: The field moves fast. New multilingual benchmarks appear regularly. Our verifica-
tion dates let users track how current the data is.
Selection bias: Benchmarks from non-English-language venues may be underrepresented.
5.4 Future Work
Next steps include expanding to more benchmarks, adding quality and methodology ratings, automating
tracking via arXiv and HuggingFace monitoring, and letting the community contribute with a verification
workflow.
6 Conclusion
AI Safety Institutes need coordination infrastructure to avoid duplicating effort and to see where the
gaps are. SafetyGap is a first attempt at mapping multilingual safety benchmark coverage.
The picture is uneven. Truthfulness and toxicity benchmarks extend to 17–21 languages. Bias de-
tection, over-refusal, and adversarial robustness remain almost entirely English-only. Models deployed
globally have never been tested for these risks in most languages. Hindi speakers, nearly 600 million
people, have access to just 5% of the safety evaluation infrastructure available in English. Compliant
bias evaluation under the EU AI Act is impossible in 19 of 24 official EU languages. These gaps are
both a safety problem and a coordination opportunity.

Appendix: Limitations & Dual-Use Considerations
Limitations
• Catalog is growing; more exist, especially from non-English research communities
• We verified existence, not translation quality or cultural validity
• English-centric search methodology may undercount non-English-origin benchmarks
Dual-Use Risks
Minimal. SafetyGap maps defensive evaluation infrastructure, not attack vectors. All cataloged bench-
marks are already public.
Ethical Considerations
Multilingual coverage gaps create differential risk: non-English speakers face higher exposure to AI
systems that were never evaluated in their languages. Making these gaps visible is a step toward fixing
them.
