Deterrence Under Imperfect Monitoring:
An Agent-Based Compute Permit Market Simulator
 Patrick Duffy      Josh Tuffy      Emlyn Graham
 AI Safety Camp 11  |  Apart Research Technical AI Governance Hackathon 2026
Abstract
 Proposals for frontier AI compute governance typically assume that regulators can perfectly observe firm
behavior. We relax this assumption by building an open-source, agent-based simulator of a compute permit
market in which an auditor possesses limited budget, imperfect signal quality, and noisy detection. We sweep
across the two-dimensional space of penalty magnitude and detection probability and find a sharp
compliance phase transition: below a detection threshold of approximately 0.5, no feasible penalty induces
compliance above the 50% baseline; above this threshold, penalties and detection combine to produce a
steep deterrence gradient, reaching 90% compliance at the enforcement frontier. Our results suggest that
governance regimes should invest in detection capability before increasing penalty severity, and we identify
concrete extensions—dollar-denominated penalties, dynamic reputation effects, compute-threshold
triggers—that would bring the model closer to deployment-ready policy analysis.
1. Introduction
As frontier AI training runs consume increasing quantities of compute, policymakers have proposed
permit-based systems to govern access to large-scale training infrastructure. The logic is straightforward:
require firms to hold permits for training runs above a specified compute threshold (analogous to emissions caps
in environmental regulation), and enforce compliance through audits and penalties. Several recent
proposals—including those from Heim and Konaev (2024), Sastry et al. (2024), and policy frameworks emerging
from the EU AI Act—presuppose that the regulator can observe training activity with high fidelity.
In practice, oversight is neither perfect nor free. Regulators face budget constraints that limit audit frequency,
and monitoring signals are noisy: power-consumption telemetry, chip-utilization logs, and self-reported training
manifests can all be spoofed, delayed, or misread. This paper asks: under what combinations of detection
capability and penalty magnitude can a regulator still enforce compliance? We answer this question with
an open-source agent-based model (ABM) that simulates rational labs, a permit market, and a
resource-constrained auditor.
2. Model Architecture
The simulator, built on the Mesa ABM framework in Python, comprises three domain objects that interact over
discrete time steps.
Labs (rational agents) each possess a gross value vi from training, a racing factor cr capturing competitive
urgency, a reputation sensitivity R, and a risk profile that scales perceived penalties. At each step, a lab without
a permit evaluates a deterrence inequality: it cheats if the gain from non-compliance exceeds the expected
penalty. Formally, the lab defects when g > p · B, where the gain g = min(market_price, vi) + cr · Vb, the effective
detection probability p is computed by the auditor, and the perceived penalty B = (P + R) · risk_profile.
Market. A permit clearing mechanism accepts bids from all labs and allocates a fixed token cap to the highest
bidders, establishing an endogenous market price that feeds back into each lab's gain calculation.
Auditor (regulator). The auditor observes a noisy binary signal si per firm, with false-positive rate α = P(s=1 |
compliant) and false-negative rate 1–β = P(s=0 | non-compliant). Based on this signal, it audits at a high rate π1
(targeted) or a low rate π0 (random). The effective detection probability combines these: peff = π1 · β + π0 · (1 –
β), scaled by an agent-specific audit coefficient.

3. Experimental Design
We conduct a parameter sweep over a 10 × 10 grid of penalty magnitude P ∈ [0.1, 1.0] and detection probability
p ∈ [0.1, 1.0], running 20 agents over 10 steps per cell with a token cap of 10 permits. Each cell records the
mean compliance rate across all agents and steps. We also define three named scenarios (Table 1) that anchor
qualitative regimes: a low-enforcement baseline, a high-deterrence configuration, and a targeted-auditing
regime ("The Signal") that concentrates audit resources on suspicious firms.

4. Results
Figure 1 presents the compliance rate surface across the full penalty–detection grid. The dominant feature is a
compliance phase transition at approximately p = 0.5. Below this threshold, compliance remains pinned at the
50% baseline regardless of penalty magnitude—even P = 1.0 yields no improvement when detection is weak.
This reflects the multiplicative structure of deterrence: if the probability of being caught is low, even severe
penalties produce negligible expected costs.
Above the threshold, a steep deterrence gradient emerges. At (p = 0.9, P = 1.0), compliance reaches 90%.
Critically, moderate penalties with strong detection (p = 1.0, P = 0.6 → 55%) outperform severe penalties with
weak detection (p = 0.5, P = 1.0 → 50%). This asymmetry has direct policy implications: a dollar invested in
detection infrastructure yields greater compliance returns than a dollar invested in penalty escalation.

5. Discussion and Future Work

Our principal finding—that detection capability dominates penalty severity in driving compliance—echoes
classical results in the economics of crime (Becker, 1968) but situates them in the specific context of compute
governance, where the "detection" problem maps to hardware telemetry, energy monitoring, and chip-level
attestation. The phase transition we observe suggests that governance regimes below a critical monitoring
threshold may be functionally unenforceable, regardless of statutory penalty levels.
Several extensions would bring the simulator closer to deployment-ready policy analysis. First,
dollar-denominated penalties (in millions) and compute thresholds expressed in FLOP would enable direct
comparison with real regulatory proposals. Second, dynamic reputation effects—where caught firms face
escalating stigma—would model the reputational cascades observed in corporate compliance contexts. Third,
compute-threshold triggers that require permits only for runs exceeding a specified scale (e.g., 1025 FLOP)
would decouple the value of small-model innovation from regulatory burden. Additional planned extensions
include racing-factor decay (competitive urgency that fades over time), dynamic detection that scales with the
gap between actual and reported compute usage, and telemetry backchecks that create secondary catch
vectors independent of the primary audit signal.
6. Security Considerations
A simulator that maps the conditions under which enforcement fails is inherently dual-use: the same heatmap
that guides regulators toward effective deterrence also reveals to adversaries the parameter regions in which
cheating is undetectable. We mitigate this by publishing the simulator as an open-source tool for regulators and
researchers, under the principle that transparent identification of enforcement blind spots is preferable to their
quiet exploitation. The scenarios and parameter ranges presented here are intentionally stylized; deployment in
a real regulatory context would require calibration against classified telemetry data that we neither possess nor
disclose.
7. Conclusion
We present an open-source agent-based simulator that demonstrates how compute permit markets behave
under realistic conditions of imperfect monitoring and constrained audit budgets. Our central result—a sharp
compliance phase transition mediated by detection probability—provides a concrete, quantitative argument for
prioritizing monitoring infrastructure over penalty escalation in the design of AI compute governance regimes.
The simulator, code, and interactive dashboard are publicly available to support further research and policy
development.
