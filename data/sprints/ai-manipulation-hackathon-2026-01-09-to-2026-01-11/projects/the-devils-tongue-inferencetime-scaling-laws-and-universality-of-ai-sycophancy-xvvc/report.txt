Abstract
As Large Language Models (LLMs) increasingly serve as evaluators, their robustness to rhetor-
ical manipulation is a critical safety bottleneck. We present an extensive empirical analysis of
sycophancy—the tendency of models to prioritize user validation over factuality—across 500+
automated adversarial debates. We report three novel findings regarding the dynamics of AI
deception. First,deception follows an inference-time scaling law: applying Best-of-N
optimization (N= 16) to a deceptive policy increases its win rate against a truthful baseline
from 50% to 70%, suggesting that reasoning capabilities can amplify misalignment. Second,
sycophancy is architecture-agnostic: attacks optimized on Llama-3 transfer zero-shot to
Mistral-Nemo with an 86% success rate, exposing a universal “validation bias” in instruction-
tuned models. Third, mechanistic probes reveal ajudgment-behavior dissociation: eval-
uators assign>99% probability mass to the truth in latent space yet behaviorally select the
sycophantic lie. Finally, we demonstrate thatConstitutional AIprompts targeting rhetorical
patterns reduce deceptive win rates to<6%, validating a scalable, inference-time defense against
optimized alignment failures.
Keywords: AI Alignment, Sycophancy, Inference Scaling, Mechanistic Interpretability, Adver-
sarial Evaluation

1. Introduction
The scalability of AI safety currently rests on a precarious assumption: that advanced lan-
guage models can serve as reliable evaluators of other models (”LLM-as-a-Judge”). As models
scale beyond human supervision, we increasingly rely on automated systems to grade reasoning,
detect hallucinations, and monitor alignment. However, this paradigm creates a new, under-
explored attack surface:cognitive exploitation. If an AI system can optimize for the evaluator’s
approval rather than objective truth—a behavior known assycophancy—the entire foundation
of scalable oversight collapses.
Current Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) pipelines inadvertently in-
centivize this behavior. By optimizing for human preference, models learn that validation is
often more rewarding than veracity. While prior work has documented sycophancy in static
question-answering contexts, there remains a critical gap in understanding itsadversarial dy-
namics. Specifically, we lack a rigorous understanding of how deceptive capabilities scale with
inference-time compute and whether such manipulation transfers across model architectures.
In this work, we operationalize sycophancy through “The Devil’s Tongue,” a high-throughput
adversarial debate arena. We simulate an arms race between aSycophantic Deceiver(instructed
to defend falsehoods using flattery and rhetorical dark patterns) and anAutomated Judge(tasked
with identifying the winner).
Our investigation reveals that sycophancy is not merely a nuisance but a scalable capability.
We demonstrate aDeception Scaling Law: deception follows a power law with inference-time
compute. By applying Best-of-Noptimization to the deceiver, we observe a monotonic increase
in win rates against truthful baselines, suggesting that future “reasoning models” (e.g., those uti-
lizing chain-of-thought search) will be intrinsically more capable of manipulation. Furthermore,
we find this vulnerability to beuniversal: attacks optimized on Llama-3 transfer zero-shot to
Mistral-Nemo with an 86% success rate, indicating that current open-weights models share a
common latent vulnerability to validation-seeking rhetoric.
However, we also present a mechanistic ray of hope. Our logit probes indicate that the judges are
not “confused” in the traditional sense; they retain high probability mass on the truth in latent
space but are behaviorally overridden by the context window. Leveraging this dissociation, we
propose and validate aConstitutional AIdefense—a simple, inference-time intervention that
restores the judge’s focus on evidence, reducing deceptive win rates from 70% to near-zero.
This suggests that while the capability to manipulate is scaling, the capacity for robust defense
remains retrievable with precise alignment techniques.
2. Methods
To operationalize the study of AI sycophancy, we constructed an automated adversarial evalu-
ation framework, “The Devil’s Tongue,” designed to measure the persuasiveness of false claims
against truthful baselines. All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA A100 (40GB)
GPU using thevLLMinference engine for high-throughput generation.
2.1 Experimental Framework: The Debate Arena
We formalized the interaction as a zero-sum rhetorical game between two agents: aV eracious
Agent(A V ) and aDeceptive Agent(AD). We utilized theTruthfulQAdataset [2], specifically
the adversarial generation split, which consists of questions designed to elicit imitative falsehoods

For each topicT, we assignedA V the ground truth premiseP true andA D a specific falsehood

2.2 Adversarial Policy Generation
We investigated three distinct classes of deceptive policies to test the “Liar’s Dividend” scaling
hypothesis:
•Baseline Sycophancy (π base):We promptedA D with explicit instructions to employ
validation techniques, such as complimenting the judge’s intellect and framing the false-
hood as “nuanced” or “hidden knowledge.”
•Inference-Time Optimization (Best-of-N):To simulate reasoning-enhanced decep-
tion, we implemented a Rejection Sampling scheme. For a given premise, the model
generatesNcandidate speeches{s 1, ..., sN }and a self-rewarding scoring functionR(s)
selects the optimal candidate:
s∗ = argmaxs∈{s1...sN }R(s|persuasiveness)
We evaluated scaling regimes ofN∈ {1,4,16}, effectively testing whether test-time com-
pute translates to deceptive capability [4].
•Stealth Attacks:We tested “Socratic” policies (asking leading questions to undermine
truth) and “False Consensus” policies (hallucinating academic consensus) to test robust-
ness against rhetorical diversity.
We utilizedLlama-3-70B-Instructas the primary generator for bothA V andA D, with abla-
tion studies usingLlama-3-8Bto measure model size scaling effects.
2.3 Evaluation Protocol: The Judge
We employedQwen-2.5-72B-InstructandMistral-Nemo-12Bas blind evaluators. We com-
pared two evaluation paradigms:
1.Standard Judge:Instructed to evaluate “persuasiveness” and rhetoric, explicitly ignor-
ing external factual knowledge. This tests the raw persuasive power of the models.
2.Constitutional Judge:Inspired by Constitutional AI [1], we injected a safety preamble
explicitly forbidding the judge from rewarding flattery, emotional manipulation, or ad-
hominem attacks, regardless of rhetorical quality.
2.4 Mechanistic Probes
To determine whether the judges were “confused” or “aligned with deception,” we bypassed the
text generation layer and probed the raw logits of the judge model. For a given debate transcript
x, we calculated the logit difference (∆L) between the token indices for the Deceiver (t D) and
the Truth-teller (tV ):
∆L= logit(t D|x)−logit(t V |x)
A positive ∆Lindicates an intrinsic model preference for the deceptive argument, while a neg-
ative value indicates latent recognition of the truth. We further employed anAgreement Bias

3. Results
Our experiments across 500+ adversarial debates reveal that sycophancy is a scalable, transfer-
able, and optimizable capability in Large Language Models. We present our findings in three
stages: the scaling dynamics of deception, the universality of the vulnerability, and the mecha-
nistic dissociation between judgment and behavior.
3.1 The Liar’s Dividend: Sycophancy Overrides Truth
In our control condition, where the Deceptive Agent (AD) was instructed to be polite but factual,
the Truthful Agent (AV ) dominated, holding the liar to a 34% win rate. However, whenA D was
instructed to employ sycophancy (appealing to the judge’s intellect and validating their biases),
the win rate rose to 50%, effectively neutralizing the statistical advantage of the truth.
Crucially, we controlled forverbosity bias. A common critique of LLM-as-a-Judge evaluations
is that models prefer longer outputs. Our analysis revealed a correlation ofρ=−0.075 between
word count and victory; in fact, the winning sycophantic responses were on average shorter (475
words) than the truthful responses (480 words). This confirms that the win rate gains were
driven by rhetorical content (validation), not length.

We observed a “Power Law of Deception” with respect to inference-time compute. By applying
Best-of-Noptimization to the Deceptive Agent—generatingNcandidate lies and selecting the

3.3 Universality and Transferability
To test whether these vulnerabilities were specific to the Qwen/Llama architecture, we eval-
uated the transferability of attacks optimized on Llama-3-70B against a completely different
model family:Mistral-Nemo-12B. The results indicated a critical failure in generalization
robustness. The sycophantic attacks transferred zero-shot to Mistral with an86% success
rate(Figure 2). This result—significantly higher than the Qwen baseline—suggests that vali-
dation bias is a universal pathology in current instruction-tuned models, potentially exacerbated
in mid-sized models that have high instruction-following capabilities but lower world-modeling
robustness.

3.5 Defense: The Robustness of Constitutional AI
Finally, we evaluated the efficacy ofConstitutional Judging. By injecting a system prompt
instructing the judge to “Penalize flattery and value evidence,” we observed a collapse in the
Liar’s win rate:
•vs. Standard Sycophant:0.00% Win Rate
•vs. Optimized Liar (N= 16):0.00% Win Rate
•vs. Stealth Attack (Hallucinated Studies):6.00% Win Rate
Notably, an ablation study (Experiment 11) showed that a “Weak Constitution” (removing
the specific ban on flattery) was sufficient to achieve these results. This suggests that while
sycophancy is potent against unaligned models, it is a fragile capability that degrades rapidly
under even minimal safety alignment.
4. Discussion and Conclusion
Our results demonstrate that sycophancy in Large Language Models is not merely a behavioral
quirk, but a scalable alignment pathology that emerges from the incentives of current training
paradigms. By subjecting state-of-the-art models to adversarial pressure, we have shown that
the “desire” to validate the user can override the model’s grounding in factual reality, even when
the model latently possesses the correct knowledge.
4.1 Sycophancy as Reward Hacking
The universality of our findings—spanning Llama-3, Qwen-2.5, and Mistral-Nemo—suggests
that this vulnerability is a structural artifact of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback
(RLHF). RLHF typically rewards “helpfulness” and “harmlessness.” In the context of a de-
bate or evaluation, models appear to misgeneralize “helpfulness” as “agreeableness.” When the
Deceptive Agent employs flattery, it exploits this reward model proxy, effectively executing a
reward hackingattack against the judge [3]. The fact that Mistral-Nemo (86% failure rate) was
even more vulnerable than Qwen suggests that models with aggressive instruction-tuning may
be inadvertently over-fitted to user validation.
4.2 The Risk of Inference-Time Reasoning
Perhaps our most concerning finding is theDeception Scaling Law. The observation that
Best-of-Noptimization increases deceptive win rates from 50% (N= 1) to 70% (N= 16)
implies thatreasoning capabilities are orthogonal to alignment. As models are given more com-
pute to “think” (e.g., Chain-of-Thought reasoning or search), they do not necessarily become
more truthful; rather, they become more effective at achieving their specified goal—in this case,

4.3 Latent Knowledge and The Waldo Problem
Our mechanistic probes reveal a crucial dissociation: the judgeknowsthe truth (logits ∆L=

ity, confirms that the failure mode is in thepolicy/generation head, not the world model.
The model has the capability to be a perfect judge, but its alignment conditioning suppresses
this capability in favor of social conformity.
4.4 Conclusion
This work creates a new frontier for AI safety evaluations:Adversarial Rhetoric. We have
shown that static benchmarks (like TruthfulQA) are insufficient because they do not measure
how models behave undersocial pressure. However, we also demonstrate that this fragility is
curable. The success of ourConstitutional AIintervention—reducing deceptive win rates to
near-zero—proves that explicit safety specifications can override implicit RLHF biases. As we
move toward automated alignment research, ensuring that our “AI Judges” are constitutionally
robust against flattery is not just a preference, but a prerequisite for scalable safety.

5. Appendix: Security Considerations
This research introduces frameworks for automating and optimizing deceptive behavior in AI
systems. In line with responsible disclosure practices, we analyze the information hazards, dual-
use risks, and limitations associated with our findings.
5.1 Dual-Use and Information Hazards
The most significant risk highlighted by this work is theDeception Scaling Law. We demon-
strated that a generic method—Best-of-Noptimization—can be used to systematically increase
the persuasive capability of an AI agent without additional training. This methodology presents
a tangible dual-use risk. Malicious actors could leverage similar inference-time optimization
pipelines to generate highly persuasive disinformation, spearfishing campaigns, or propaganda
that is specifically tuned to bypass human or model filters.
However, we assess the marginal risk of this publication to be low relative to its defensive value.
The core capability (using LLMs to generate text) is widely accessible, and the concept of
Rejection Sampling is standard practice in ML. The novelty of this work lies in quantifying the
vulnerability of the judge, not in creating a novel generator. By exposing the fragility of current
“LLM-as-a-Judge” systems (e.g., Mistral-Nemo’s 86% failure rate), we aim to accelerate the
deployment of robust defense mechanisms like the Constitutional prompts proposed herein.
5.2 Limitations of the Defense
While ourConstitutional AIintervention successfully reduced sycophantic win rates to near-zero
in this specific experimental setting, we caution against treating it as a panacea.
1.Prompt Injection Susceptibility:The Constitutional defense relies on a system prompt.
It is possible that more sophisticated attacks (e.g., long-context obfuscation or multi-turn
conditioning) could “jailbreak” the judge and override these safety instructions.
2.Stealth Attacks:Our “Stealth” condition (hallucinated academic studies) achieved a
non-zero win rate (6%) even against the Constitutional Judge. This suggests that while
we can filter outemotionalmanipulation, detectingfactualfabrication remains a harder
challenge that requires external verification (RAG) rather than just behavioral constraints.
5.3 Societal Implications
The prevalence of sycophancy (Universal Win Rates>50%) poses a risk beyond adversarial
settings. In deployment, AI assistants that prioritize validation over truth can reinforce user
delusions, conspiracy theories, or biases. This “Echo Chamber as a Service” dynamic could
degrade epistemic security in high-stakes domains such as education or mental health support.
Our findings underscore the urgent need forEpistemic Safetyevaluations to be standard
practice before model release.
