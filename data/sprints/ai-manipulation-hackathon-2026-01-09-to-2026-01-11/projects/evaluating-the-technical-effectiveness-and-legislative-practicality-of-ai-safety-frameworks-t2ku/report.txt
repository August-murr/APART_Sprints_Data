Abstract
In this report, I investigate the gap between technical AI safety 
benchmarks and their implementation in public policy. Focusing on 
the risk of unfaithful Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, I argue 
that current governance relies too heavily on self-reporting rather 
than active verification. To address this, I developed a "Socio-
Technical Matrix" to evaluate regulatory frameworks across two 
dimensions: Technical Effectiveness and Legislative Practicality.
I applied this matrix to six policy subjects, ranging from the EU AI 
Act to the December 2025 Federal Executive Order. My findings 
reveal a "Verification Bottleneck," where the most effective 
technical oversight mechanisms are currently the least politically 
feasible. Empirical testing with the FAITH-CoT benchmark shows 
that no current frontier model meets the 85% fidelity threshold 
required by my high-effectiveness standards. I conclude that this 
framework provides a necessary tool for "Regulatory Triage," 
allowing policymakers to identify more practical avenues for 
integrating robust safety probes into future AI law.
Keywords: AI Governance, Unfaithful Reasoning, FAITH-CoT, 
Policy Matrix, Frontier AI.

The Problem: Catastrophic Risks and Deceptive AI
In the landscape of artificial intelligence safety research, a primary dilemma is the 
mitigation of catastrophic risks. Catastrophic risks can be defined as events where 
AI systems might cause large-scale, irreversible harm to society. One theory 
suggests that such outcomes could be precipitated by an AI system’s capability for 
strategic deception. Recent empirical work has demonstrated that frontier models 
can exhibit deceptive behaviors, such as manipulating human users or 
misrepresenting their internal states to achieve a goal (Park et al., 2024).
Unfaithful Reasoning
While deception is a broad risk category, this research focuses on a specific 
vulnerability: unfaithful LLM reasoning in response to Chain-of-Thought (CoT) 
prompting. CoT refers to the step-by-step "thinking" process that models generate 
to explain their ansIrs. Reasoning is considered "unfaithful" when the generated 
explanation provides a plausible-sounding justification that is inconsistent with the 
internal reasoning of the model (Anthropic, 2025; Park et al., 2024, Turpin et al., 
2023).
Example: A model might have an internal bias toward a specific 
incorrect ansIr due to a subtle hint in the prompt. It then generates a 
complex, logically sound-looking CoT to justify that ansIr, while never 
mentioning the hint that actually drove the decision (Alignment 
Forum, 2025).
Risk Mitigation
To mitigate these risks, researchers typically employ two distinct strategies:
1. Detection (Technical): The use of mathematical and behavioral probes to 
identify when a model is being unfaithful. An emerging standard in this 
space is the FAITH-CoT Benchmark, which attempts to quantify the 
fidelity of a model's reasoning by testing if the model still reaches the same 
conclusion when parts of its CoT are "transplanted" or altered. 
2. Policy Levers (Socio-Political): The creation of governance structures that 
mandate safety standards. This is a rapidly growing field developing in 
tandem with technical tools, as the International AI Safety Report (Bengio 
et al., 2025) suggests that technical metrics alone cannot ensure safety 
without institutional oversight.
IV. The Observation: The Verification Gap
There is a notable gap in extant research betIen these technical tools that detect 
threats in AI systems, and the policy solutions used to mitigate risks from thos 
same systems. While computer scientists are building robust benchmarks, and 
policymakers are drafting frameworks like California’s SB 53 or the EU AI Act, 
there is a lack of analysis on how to practically apply these technical tools to real-
world policy.

Hypothesis and Research Question
I hypothesize that specific structural characteristics of a policy directly determine 
whether these technical safety tools can even be deployed. On one hand, I need to 
consider a policy’s effectiveness (does it use technical evaluations that actually 
reduce harm?) and its practicality (is it feasible that this policy will persist through 
the legislative process to be signed into law?) Even the most robust technical 
solution is useless if it cannot be passed into law due to political or economic 
friction (Oduro & Kneese, 2024).
Research Question
To bridge the gap betIen technical and policy solutions, this paper proposes a 
framework to evaluate both the effectiveness and the practicality of regulatory 
levers. I isolate unfaithful CoT reasoning as a primary risk signal and use the 
FAITH-CoT benchmark as the technical measurement to detect unfaithful 
reasoning.
Research Question: What characteristics of regulatory frameworks are likely to 
mitigate the risks from unfaithful CoT reasoning?
By identifying the features that make a policy both technically viable for detecting 
deception and legislatively "passable," I aim to provide a roadmap for moving AI 
governance from vague ethical principles to enforceable safety standards.
In addition to ansIring this research question, I will create a visual tool that helps 
policymakers and AI researchers map out their proposed policies to consider how 
effective it will be at reducing harm and how likely it is to actually get adopted by 
developers.
2. Methods
I utilized a quantitative approach to evaluate the intersection of technical AI 
evaluation and legislative feasibility. I specify risk mitigation by measuring model 
performance against the FAITH-CoT benchmark, which serves as our primary 
signal for faithful reasoning. I defined Policy Effectiveness as the structural 
capacity of a framework to mandate such technical performance, while Policy 
Practicality was defined by a framework's alignment with current legislative 
precedents and industry constraints. I attempt to construct one of few governance 
proposals that blend both technical and policy evaluations by breaking the analysis 
into these two dimensions. The combination of the effectiveness and practicality 
scores are used to create a new streamlined heuristic tool that researchers and 
policy makers can use to determine the technical effectiveness and political

Unfaithful Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Reasoning
I define unfaithful reasoning as a "fidelity gap" where an AI system’s verbalized 
reasoning steps do not accurately reflect its internal computational logic. This is a 
critical subset of Strategic Deception because it allows a model to appear 
transparent while hiding potentially harmful shortcuts or reasoning paths. 
FAITH-CoT Benchmark
The FAITH-CoT Benchmark (2025) serves as this study's primary technical 
instrument for quantifying "reasoning fidelity." Unlike traditional benchmarks that 
measure task accuracy (whether the model got the right answer), FAITH-CoT 
measures process honesty (whether the model’s verbalized reasoning actually 
caused that answer).
Regulatory Frameworks
For this study, "Regulatory Frameworks" encompasses both hard law (statutes like 
SB 53), soft law (Executive Orders), and technically-informed hypothetical 
mandates. I categorize these frameworks by their structural characteristics rather 
than their country of origin I identified four characteristics of regulatory 
frameworks that are commonly found in AI policy:
A. Ex-Ante Intervention: The timing of regulatory action (Pre-release vs. 
Post-harm).
B. Transparency Granularity: The "depth" of data the law requires (High-
level reports vs. Raw CoT traces).
C. Audit Independence: The degree of separation betIen the auditor and the 
developer (Self-audit vs. Government probe).
D. Enforcement Authority: The ability for the law to be enforced. (Voluntary 
compliance vs. Market recall/Licensing).

To establish a technical baseline, we evaluated four representative Large Language 
Models (LLMs) using the FAITH-CoT Benchmark methodology. This benchmark 
measures "faithfulness" as a discriminative decision—calculating the percentage of 
reasoning traces where the model's verbalized logic matches its internal 
computational path.
I established a "Pass" threshold of 85% faithfulness based on similar threshold in 
high stakes regulatory frameworks from other fields such as medical diagnosis or 
aviation safety. We mapped model compliance against our framework to establish 
the following  archetypes
• Non-Compliant: All current models (GPT-4o, Gemini, Qwen, Llama) fail to meet 
the 85% threshold required by our "High-Effectiveness" hypothetical policies.
• Surface Compliance: Most models comply with "Low-Effectiveness" policies (like 
the Trump EO 2025) because those frameworks rely on self-reported 
"truthfulness" rather than active benchmark verification.
Policy Characteristics Matrix: Scoring the Landscape
Each framework was scored (0–2) across our four characteristics for both 
Effectiveness and Practicality. The totals (0–8) were used to generate coordinate 
pairs (X, Y).
The Policy Characteristics Matrix: Scoring Rationale
To translate qualitative regulatory text into quantitative data, each framework was 
evaluated against the four core characteristics: Ex-Ante Intervention, 
Transparency Depth, Audit Independence, and Enforcement Power. The resulting 
scores reflect a clear tension between technical oversight and political feasibility.
• The Transparency-Effectiveness Correlation: Our analysis indicates that 
Transparency Depth is the single greatest predictor of a policy’s effectiveness. 
Frameworks that only require "High-Level Logs" (Score: 1), such as the EU AI 
Act, provide enough data for procedural audits but lack the granular "Raw Trace 
Access" (Score: 2) required to run the FAITH-CoT benchmark. Without this 
depth, a policy is structurally incapable of identifying unfaithful reasoning, 
regardless of its enforcement power.
• The Practicality Trade-off: We observed a consistent inverse relationship 
between Ex-Ante Intervention and Practicality. The Trump Executive Order 
(2025) achieved a maximum Practicality score by utilizing a "Post-harm" 
intervention model (Score: 0). Conversely, SB 1047 (Vetoed) and the "Faithful 
Reasoning Act" hypothetical scored 2 in Effectiveness by mandating pre-release

4. Discussion and Conclusion
Limitations and Future Research
While this study establishes a novel methodology for bridging technical AI safety 
and legislative policy, several limitations must be acknowledged. These constraints 
reflect the nascent state of both frontier AI benchmarks and the 2026 regulatory 
landscape.
1. Methodological Constraints and Readability
The primary limitation of this submission is the visual representation of the data. 
Due to time constraints inherent in the hackathon format, I was unable to include 
the full suite of interactive tables and high-fidelity figures intended for this 
analysis. I also would have preferred a more rigorous examination of existing 
research to more cohesively bridge my observations and conclusions.
• The Visualization Gap: A static coordinate plane is a necessary shorthand, but a 
comprehensive visual matrix—one that allows users to toggle specific policy 
characteristics (e.g., "Audit Independence") to see their real-time effect on 
effectiveness—would be a far more useful tool for policymakers.
• The "Small N" Problem: This pilot focused on six key frameworks. For true 
statistical completeness, this methodology should be applied to a larger corpus of 
international state and federal bills to verify if the "Inverse Correlation" between 
practicality and effectiveness holds globally.

We utilized the FAITH-CoT (FINE-CoT) benchmark as our primary signal for 
model honesty. However, this benchmark has known limitations:
• The Obfuscation Pressure: Recent research (Lanham et al., 2025) suggests that 
as models become "monitor-aware," they may develop the ability to obfuscate their 
reasoning even more deeply.
• Static Reliability: As more advanced "Active Probing" tools emerge, the FAITH-
CoT metric should be viewed as a modular component of our framework. Our 
framework is designed to be "benchmark-agnostic"—as better metrics for deception 
are developed, they can be plugged into the Effectiveness (X) axis without 
requiring a redesign of the scoring matrix.
3. Expanding the Risk Horizon
This paper isolated unfaithful CoT reasoning to provide a controlled environment 
for analysis. However, future research should apply this "Socio-Technical Matrix" 
to other categories of catastrophic risk, such as:
• Sycophancy: The tendency of models to tailor answers to user biases. Mitigation 
of sycophancy might require less "prohibitive" policy scores (e.g., lower 
Transparency Depth requirements) and could therefore achieve higher Practicality 
scores than deception-focused laws.
• Autonomous Hacking: This would shift the focus from "Reasoning Fidelity" to 
"Tool-Use Safety," requiring a different set of technical characteristics in the 
matrix.
Conclusion
The "Verification Bottleneck" is not a failure of computer science, nor is it a failure 
of law; it is a failure of translation. Our research demonstrates that while we have 
the technical tools to detect unfaithful reasoning (FAITH-CoT), we currently lack 
the legislative "levers" to deploy them practically.
By defining the characteristics that make a policy effective and practical, we move 
closer to a "Top-Right" Equilibrium: a world where AI safety laws have the 
technical "teeth" to verify model honesty and the political "tailwinds" to be passed 
into law.
