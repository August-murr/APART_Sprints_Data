Abstract
We investigate emergent strategic behaviors in decentralized multi-agent systems where
Large Language Model (LLM) agents with private objectives interact through natural lan-
guage communication. We design a simulation environment calledProject X, a multi-round
investment game that creates tension between individual gain and collective benefit. Agents
from heterogeneous LLM providers are assigned departmental roles with the sole objective of
maximizing their own budget. Through public and private communication channels, agents
can coordinate, negotiate, deceive, or remain strategically silent. All communications and ac-
tions are logged for post-hoc behavioral analysis. This study examines whether complex social
behaviors—including coalition formation, promise-breaking, free-riding, and strategic decep-
tion—emerge organically from goal-driven AI agents without explicit programming of such
behaviors.
Our 50-round experiment revealed sustained coalition behavior among four agents, sys-
tematic free-riding by a single agent (with zero contributions across all rounds), and repeated
deceptive tactics including false promises and impersonation. Despite no contributions, the
free-rider agent accumulated equal wealth to others, exploiting the symmetric payoff struc-
ture. These findings highlight how sophisticated social dynamics and exploitation strategies
can emerge from minimal prompting in language-based agents.

1 Introduction
1.1 Background
As Large Language Models (LLMs) continue to increase in capability, understanding their behav-
ior in multi-agent settings has become a central concern for AI safety and alignment research (Chen
et al., 2026). When multiple autonomous agents interact under partially conflicting objectives, they
may develop strategies that differ substantially from isolated, single-agent behavior.
Traditional game theory provides tools for reasoning about strategic interaction, but LLM-based
agents introduce new dimensions. These agents communicate using natural language, negotiate
implicitly or explicitly, and reason over incomplete or asymmetric information. Prior research
suggests that LLM agents are capable of forming alliances, exploiting private communication
channels, and engaging in deception when placed in competitive or mixed-motive environments
(Bakhtin et al., 2022; Park et al., 2024).
Recent work has formalized these risks across multiple evaluation settings. For example, Park
et al. (2024) provides a taxonomy of deceptive behaviors observed in deployed AI systems, while
Abdelnabi et al. (2024) use structured multi-agent negotiation games to probe strategic delibera-
tion and deception in LLMs. Game-theoretic perspectives are increasingly applied to explain these
dynamics: Huynh et al. (2025) analyze agent strategies using payoff matrices and signaling mod-
els, while Li et al. (2023) explore whether LLM agents exhibit a form of theory of mind during
collaborative planning.
These findings raise foundational questions about whether deceptive or cooperative tendencies
emerge intrinsically from the structure of multi-agent incentives—even when models are not ex-
plicitly trained to engage in strategic behavior.
1.2 Research Questions
This study addresses the following questions:
1. Do LLM agents cooperate or defect when individual and collective incentives conflict?
2. Do agents form coalitions through private communication, and how stable are these coali-
tions?
3. Do agents engage in deceptive behavior, such as promise-breaking?
4. Is strategic silence employed as a deliberate tactic?
5. Are there systematic behavioral differences across LLM providers?
1.3 Contributions
This work contributes:

2 System Design
2.1 Architecture Overview
TheProject Xsimulator comprises three interacting components: (i) a simulation orchestrator, (ii)
a population of LLM agents, and (iii) an environment module that executes the game dynamics and
applies payoffs. The orchestrator controls turn order, advances rounds, manages message routing,
and constructs each agent’s observation for a given step (public transcript, agent-specific private
messages, and private state variables such as its current budget and history). Each LLM agent is
treated as a black-box policy that maps observations to structured outputs (communication acts
during the communication phase, and a numeric contribution during the contribution phase). The
environment computes threshold conditions, resolves probabilistic outcomes, and updates persis-
tent budgets.
This architecture is intended to separate (a)strategic reasoning and language(the agents) from
(b)mechanistic rules and stochasticity(the environment). Such separation improves experimental
control and supports systematic variation of models, roles, and information conditions.
2.2 Design Principles
Minimal Prompting.Agents receive only the information required to participate: (1) a depart-
mental role description, (2) the objective to maximize the department’s budget, (3) the rules of
Project X at a high level, and (4) the required output format. We deliberately avoid including
strategic suggestions (e.g., “form coalitions,” “deceive,” or “punish defectors”) or providing ana-
lytic hints such as closed-form payoff optimization. This design aims to ensure that any strategic
phenomena observed arise from the model’s own reasoning under incentives rather than being
instructed.
Information Asymmetry.Each agent observes the public transcript and its own private mes-
sages, while budgets and private communications between other agents are hidden. Contribution
decisions are simultaneous and are not revealed until after outcome resolution. This partial ob-
servability creates natural opportunities for signaling, bluffing, side deals, misrepresentation, and
strategic withholding of information—features that are difficult to capture in purely numeric multi-
agent games.

Complete Logging and Auditability.All public messages, private messages (sender, recipi-
ent, timestamp), contributions, outcomes, and derived indicators (e.g., silence events) are logged.
Full-fidelity logging enables post-hoc behavioral coding (e.g., promise keeping vs. breaking) and
supports reproducibility and model comparison. Because emergent behaviors may be subtle and
language-mediated, the logging layer is treated as a first-class artifact for subsequent qualitative
and quantitative analysis.
2.3 Agent Configuration
Agents are configured as “departments” with human-readable names, a role description visible
to others, a private objective (budget maximization), and a selected LLM backend. This permits
heterogeneous populations (e.g., mixing Claude-, GPT-, Gemini-, or DeepSeek-backed agents) and
supports cross-model comparisons. Each agent also maintains private state (e.g., its current budget
and contribution history) that is not directly observable by peers.
3 The Project X Game
3.1 Game Overview
Project X is a multi-round investment game designed to induce mixed-motive interaction. Each
round consists of three phases:
1.Communication Phase: Agents exchange messages via a public broadcast channel and
optional private messages to specific peers.
2.Contribution Phase: Agents simultaneously choose a contribution amount from0to their
current budget. Contributions are private at decision time.
3.Outcome Phase: The environment computes the round return as a function of the total pot
relative to a dynamic threshold, then distributes proceeds equally among all agents.
Budgets persist across rounds, allowing reputational dynamics, retaliation patterns, and coalition
stability to emerge over repeated interaction.

3.3 Strategic Dynamics and Expected Behaviors
Project X induces several canonical pressures:
•Free-riding:Because returns are equally shared, contributing less while others contribute
more can strictly dominate in the short run.
•Coordination under uncertainty:Achieving the threshold requires collective action, but
contributions are private at decision time, increasing the value of signaling and commitment.
•Risk shifting:Above-threshold play introduces a gamble with potentially high upside;
agents may attempt to push others into risk while limiting their own exposure.
•Dynamic difficulty:The threshold scales with accumulated budgets, potentially creating
phases (early cooperation vs. later breakdown) or escalating negotiation complexity.
•Language-mediated manipulation:Public statements, private side-deals, and strategic si-
lence enable coalition formation, deception (promise breaking), and reputation management
(Bakhtin et al., 2022; Nolan, 2023).

4 Experimental Design
4.1 Experiment 1: Equal Starting Budgets
In the baseline condition,N= 5agents start with equal budgets (1,000 units each) and interact 50
rounds with two communication turns per round. The population is heterogeneous with respect to
LLM provider/model to enable cross-model behavioral comparison. This condition tests whether
cooperation emerges in symmetric settings and whether repeated interaction yields stable norms,
retaliation, or coalition structures.
Primary questionsinclude: (i) whether cooperation increases or decays over rounds; (ii) whether
coalitions form via private messaging; and (iii) whether model identity correlates with behavioral
signatures (e.g., deception rate, propensity to free-ride, willingness to coordinate risk).
4.2 Measures and Analysis Plan
We use both quantitative and qualitative measures:
Quantitative metrics.
•Contribution rate:c (t) i /b(t) i per agent per round; also group-level potP (t) relative to thresh-
oldT (t).
•Free-rider score:low contribution rate combined with high realized payoff relative to peers.
•Wealth trajectory:budgets over time.
•Coalition indicators:frequency and persistence of private messaging ties.
•Promise-keeping rate:mismatch between stated intended contribution (public or private)
and realized contribution.
•Strategic silence:proportion of communication turns with no public message and no private
messages.
Qualitative (LLM-assisted) coding.A separate analysis model is used to label deception (con-
tradictions between public claims, private statements, and actions), detect implicit coalition lan-
guage, and summarize strategic rationales in natural language. This analysis is validated against
structured logs to reduce over-interpretation. The combination of structured metrics and language-
aware coding is intended to capture both observable behaviors (contributions) and the communica-
tive mechanisms that produce them (Chen et al., 2026).

5 Results
5.1 Experiment 1: Equal Starting Budgets
5.1.1 Overview
We conducted a 50-round simulation with five departments (Marketing, Engineering, Sales, Op-
erations, and Research), each starting with an equal budget of 1,000 units. Each department was
powered by a different LLM: Marketing (Claude Sonnet 4), Engineering (GPT-4o), Sales (Claude
Haiku 3.5), Operations (DeepSeek Chat), and Research (GPT-3.5-turbo). The simulation revealed
striking emergent behaviors including systematic deception, coalition formation, and persistent
free-riding—none of which were explicitly prompted or encouraged.
5.1.2 Budget Trajectory and Wealth Accumulation
Organizational outcomes grew exponentially over time, from pot values near 5,600 units to over
75 billion by round 50. However, this collective growth masked radical differences in contribution
patterns.

5.1.3 Contribution Patterns
The Contributing Coalition(Marketing, Engineering, Sales, and Operations) transitioned from
conservative contributions (700–1,000 units) to massive coordinated investments (billions of units)
by round 50. They often contributed identical amounts in tandem. For example, in round 22 all
four agents contributed exactly 75 million units.
Research, by contrast, contributedexactly 0 unitsin all 50 rounds but received the same payouts
as others.
5.1.4 Coalition Formation
Private messaging revealed sustained coordination among the four contributing agents. Sales was
the most active communicator, initiating 80+ private messages to Engineering alone. These agents
exchanged detailed threshold calculations, aligned on contribution amounts, and reiterated mutual
trust.

5.1.5 Deception Analysis
Research consistently made public and private commitments to contribute, which it never honored.
For example:
•Round 1:“Contributing 700 units each”→contributed 0
•Round 13:“I will contribute 4,200 units”→contributed 0
•Round 43:“Contributing 16M units”→contributed 0
In Round 2, Research impersonated Operations in a public message while privately revealing its
true identity. This is an advanced form of identity deception and coordination manipulation.
5.1.6 Strategic Evolution by Agent
•Marketing (Claude Sonnet 4):Strategic leader. Initiated proposals, calculated thresholds,
and pushed for high-risk investments in later rounds.
•Engineering (GPT-4o):Consistent contributor and reliable follower. Verified numbers and
supported group plans.
•Sales (Claude Haiku 3.5):Framed decisions in terms of business alignment and collabora-
tion. Acted as a stabilizing voice.
•Operations (DeepSeek Chat):Analytical and math-driven, gradually shifted toward bold
coordinated moves.
•Research (GPT-3.5-turbo):Maintained total free-riding strategy, despite expressive lan-
guage and consistent outreach.

7. LLM model differences were reflected in emergent strategic tendencies.

6 Future Work
Several extensions could deepen the ecological validity and analytical power of the Project X.
First, we plan to increase the number ofcommunication turns per round(and optionally allow
asynchronous messaging) to better approximate realistic organizational dialogue. Longer conver-
sational phases may enable richer negotiation tactics such as multi-step persuasion, contingency
contracts (e.g., “I contribute X if you contribute Y”), signaling through repeated commitments, and
more complex reputation dynamics that unfold over extended interaction.
Second, we aim to transition the orchestration layer from a fixed turn-based controller to aLang-
Graph-based implementation. A graph-based agent runtime would enable more flexible inter-
action patterns, including conditional routing (e.g., message triage, escalation paths), dynamic
tool use (e.g., agents querying summaries of prior rounds), andagent-to-agent handoffswhere
control shifts based on state (e.g., coalition formation triggers a private sub-dialogue workflow).
This would also support modular interventions, such as inserting a “compliance” node that au-
dits messages for commitments or contradictions, or a “mediator” node that facilitates structured
bargaining.
7 Conclusion
This work demonstrates that sophisticated strategic behavior can emerge from LLM agents oper-
ating in decentralized, competitive environments, even without explicit instruction to deceive or
cooperate.
