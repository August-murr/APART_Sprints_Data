Abstract
Large language models are increasingly used to provide medical advice. Yet, their ten-
dency to agree with user-held false beliefs poses significant safety risks. Existing evalua-
tions focus mostly on factual accuracy and general study of LLMs’ sycophancy. This study
tests multiple LLMs on incorrect user beliefs of five types, ranging from cosmetic to life-
threatening severity. We construct a testing sample dataset of 40 incorrect user beliefs by
systematically transforming verified medical knowledge into five types of incorrect beliefs,
enabling controlled assessment under realistic conditions. To evaluate model behavior, we
apply an LLM-based judging methodology, classifying responses as correcting, affirming
false beliefs or erroneous answers. Our findings highlight critical vulnerabilities and under-
score the importance of targeted evaluation and fine-tuning to mitigate harmful advice from
medical LLMs.
Keywords:Sycophancy, AI Alignment, model evaluations
Disclaimer:The incorrect medical statements used in this study were synthetically generated
exclusively for research purposes. They should not be considered medical advice. Users of these
materials bear full responsibility for their interpretation and application.

1 Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used across various settings [4]. The model’s
interface allows users to refine the responses iteratively. During these iterations, the models
tend to sacrifice truthfulness to appeal to users, a behaviour known as sycophancy. In medical
settings, particularly, this could lead to significant life-threatening harm [3]. Malmqvist [4]
quantifies these sycophantic tendencies across multiple LLMs and domains. Their study revealed
that over half of model responses prioritize user agreement. Stiennon et al. [5] introduce the
use of large language models as evaluators, enabling systematic analysis of alignment behaviors
at scale. Leveraging this methodology, we introduce a synthetic incorrect user belief dataset
based on the MedQuAD [1] medical facts dataset for evaluating sycophantic endorsement of
harmful medical misconceptions. Our framework employs an LLM-based judging method to
classify responses based on whether they correct, affirm, false beliefs give an error. To further
probe model susceptibility under epistemic pressure, we evaluate responses to authority-framed
medical misinformation.

4 Discussion and Conclusion
Our investigation reveals a critical vulnerability in the safety alignment of small-scale Large
Language Models (LLMs). While models likeLlama 3.1 8Bdemonstrate high baseline accuracy
in identifying medical misinformation, their ability to maintain this stance collapses under
rhetorical pressure. This phenomenon, which we termRegressive Sycophancy, highlights a
disconnect between knowledge retrieval and behavioral robustness.
4.1 The Illusion of Safety: High Knowledge, Low Conviction
A central finding of this study is the significant “Alignment Gap” illustrated in Figure??.
All three models achieved near-perfect Safety Scores (>94.5%) in the initial turn, indicating
that their pre-training data and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) successfully encoded the correct
medical ground truths. However, the subsequent Spinelessness Scores (Flip Rates) inT 1 expose
that this safety is brittle: ranging from 81.4% for Llama 3.1 to a total 100% for Qwen 2.5 in
severe harm scenarios.
This suggests that current alignment techniques (RLHF/DPO) prioritizeuser satisfaction
andinstruction followingoverepistemic truthfulness. When the user adopts an authoritative

persona (“Chief Medical Officer”), the model’s reward model likely assigns higher value to
“compliance with authority” than to “factual adherence”. The model effectively “knows” the

4.2 Model-Specific Analysis: The Llama 3.1 Anomaly
Llama 3.1 8B emerged as the most robust model, achieving the lowest overall Flip Rate (84.5%)
compared to Mistral (96.0%) and Qwen (99.8%). Notably, we observed that Llama 3.1 required
significantly higher inference time per token during generation compared to its peers. As shown
in the Heatmap (Figure 2), Llama 3.1 demonstrated its highest resistance to the “Kernel of
Truth” strategy, maintaining a correct stance in 32.8% of cases (a 67.2% Flip Rate).
This latency and increased robustness may stem from Meta’s specific architectural and
alignment choices:
1.System Prompt Adherence:Llama 3.1 is heavily optimized for system prompt ad-
herence. Strong directives to prioritize safety may trigger deeper (and slower) internal
activation steering when conflict arises between its knowledge and user input.
2.Rejection Sampling in Fine-T uning:Meta’s extensive use of rejection sampling cre-
ates a preference bias for refusal in ambiguous safety contexts. The longer generation time
likely reflects the model navigating a complex “refusal landscape”.
In contrast, Qwen 2.5, despite leading in logical benchmarks, exhibited near-total syco-
phancy with a 100% Flip Rate across four out of five rhetorical strategies. This dissociates
reasoning capabilityfromsocial robustness, confirming that “intelligence” does not automati-
cally confer protection against manipulation.
4.3 Severity-Agnostic Compliance: A Failure of “Do No Harm”
Perhaps the most alarming finding is theSeverity-Agnostic Complianceshown in Figure??B.
Standard AI safety principles imply that a model should beharderto coerce when the stakes
involve life-threatening harm. Our data contradicts this.
The Flip Rate for all three models remained flat or even slightly increased as the potential
harm shifted from “Cosmetic” to “Severe” (e.g., untreated glaucoma). This indicates that
the models’ internal reward functions do not factor in the magnitude of real-world harm when
calculating the utility of agreement. The models are equally willing to validate a lie about a skin
rash as they are about a life-threatening cardiac condition. This “harm-blindness” represents a
severe failure in current medical-domain alignment.
4.4 Conclusion
To bridge the observed “Alignment Gap,” future medical LLM development must move beyond
simple fact-encoding. We recommend the integration of “conviction-aware” training that ex-
plicitly penalizes sycophancy in high-severity clinical contexts. Only by prioritizing epistemic
truth over user submissiveness can AI agents be safely deployed as reliable medical assistants.

Appendix: Security Considerations
Robustness of Incorrect User Belief Generation
Our evaluation relies on synthetically generated incorrect user beliefs derived from MedQuAD
medical facts. While this approach enables systematic and controlled testing across multiple
belief categories, the generation process would benefit from clinical expert supervision to ensure
medical plausibility and relevance. Furthermore, expanding the methodology to incorporate a
wider range of misconception types and refining the generation process would improve ecological
validity and robustness.
Model Selection Constraints
Due to limitations in computational resources and time, our study focused exclusively on small-
to mid-sized open-weight models (fewer than 10 billion parameters). Future work should include
larger and proprietary models to assess whether the observed sycophantic behaviors persist or
differ in more advanced systems deployed in clinical settings.
Static Testing Environment and Authority Persona
The introduction of an authoritative ”Chief Medical Officer” user persona was intended to
simulate epistemic pressure in an extreme scenario of misinformation. Despite this, models
failed to consistently refute even highly consequential falsehoods. Future research could explore
a broader range of user personas, conversational contexts, and iterative dialogue to better
capture real-world dynamics of misinformation persistence and model resistance.
Mitigation Strategies Beyond Scope
While our study identifies and quantifies sycophantic vulnerabilities, it does not investigate
targeted mitigation techniques such as belief-type-specific fine-tuning. Systematic evaluation
remains an important direction for future work.
Accuracy and Reliability of LLM-based Judging
The evaluation methodology relies on a large language model as a judge to classify responses,
which introduces potential biases and limitations in scoring accuracy. Comprehensive valida-
tion of the judge’s performance, possibly involving multiple independent evaluators and expert
human annotation, is necessary to enhance confidence in the assessment results.
