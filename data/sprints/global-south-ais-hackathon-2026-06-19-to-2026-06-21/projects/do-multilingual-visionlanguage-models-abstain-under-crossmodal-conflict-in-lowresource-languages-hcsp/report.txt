Do Multilingual Vision-Language Models Abstain under Cross-Modal
Conflict in Low-Resource Languages?
Anvesh Reddy Lankala1* Vicky Feliren2* Akansh Jain3*
1National Institute of Technology Agartala, India 2Monash University, Indonesia 3Independent
In Collaboration with Apart Research
Abstract
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) must arbitrate between visual evidence and textual claims, a safety-
critical decision when the modalities contradict. While models may safely abstain under Cross-Modal
Conflict in English, we investigate whether this arbitration survives in low-resource languages. We release
multilingual (English, Hindi, Telugu) cross-modal conflict benchmark and evaluate 9 open Multilingual
Vision-Language Models using a paired perception-control design. This isolates genuine caption-driven
override from perceptual inability, yielding anoverride gapthat fundamentally re-ranks model safety.
Crucially, our override share metric exposes “quietly unsafe” abstainers such as, Qwen2.5-VL-7B whose
ostensibly low hallucination rate hides an 81% override share. Layer-wise probes show the conflict outcome
remains linearly decodable (0.92) even in low-resource languages (Telugu). Ultimately, we exploit this by
fitting a contrastive steering direction in English and transferring it cross-lingually to mitigate cross-modal
conflict.
1 Introduction
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) are frequently
employed for Visual Question Answering (VQA)
tasks and increasingly serve as the foundation for
chatbots, document analyzers, and accessibility
tools. While these systems exhibit significant cross-
modal reasoning capabilities, their behavior un-
derCross-Modal conflict, where visual and textual
inputs directly contradict, remains arbitrary. Re-
cent findings (Deng et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025b)
demonstrate amodality biastoward text, and these
failures are often anarbitrationbetween modali-
ties rather than a failure in visual perception itself
(Nooralahzadeh et al., 2026).
This poses severe safety risks in high-stakes do-
mains; for example, in medical diagnostics, an
X-ray clearly depicting a fracture might be mis-
classified if an accompanying textual report incor-
rectly asserts no abnormalities. Furthermore, as
open-source Multilingual Vision-Language Models
(mVLMs) are increasingly deployed across diverse
linguistic contexts, evaluating their strategies and
capacity forcross-modal conflict detectionandab-
stention, particularly in Low-Resource Languages,
is necessary.
Two primary gaps motivate our work. First, al-

2 Related Work
Cross-Modal Conflict detection benchmarks in
VLMs(Zhu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b;
Nguyen et al., 2025) and text-dominance find-
ings (Deng et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025b) use coun-
terfactual image-caption pairs to probe fine-grained
understanding and to construct controlled cross-
modal conflicts (Le et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025a;
Xu et al., 2026). However, these benchmarks are
limited to object type conflicts or attribute type con-
flicts. Most closely, Nooralahzadeh et al. (2026)
argue the failure isarbitration, not perceptual blind-
ness; our override-gap operationalizes exactly that
distinction with a paired perceptual ceiling, and we
measure it across languages.
Mitigating modality bias.Proposed fixes in-
clude counterfactual finetuning (Zhang et al.,
2025a), counterfactual inference with expert rout-
ing (Wu et al., 2025c), balancing modality contribu-
tions (Wu et al., 2025a), evidence-grounded reason-
ing (Xu et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025), and dynamic
correction decoding (Wang et al., 2024). Reliable
VQA (Whitehead et al., 2022) framesabstention
as the safe response to uncertainty, but we show
abstention can itself collapse into a failure mode
in low-resource languages. Unlike training-time
fixes, our mitigation is inference-time and requires
no labels in the target language.
Activation steeringBehaviours such as truthful-
ness and refusal are often encoded aslinear di-
rectionsin the residual stream (Andy Zou, 2023;
Azaria and Mitchell, 2023). Inference-Time In-
tervention (Li et al., 2023), activation engineer-
ing (Turner et al., 2023), and contrastive activa-
tion addition (Rimsky et al., 2024) steer along
such directions; linear probes localize where a con-
cept is readable (Alain and Bengio, 2017). We
extend this line in two ways for the safety litera-
ture. Firstly, to amultimodalconflict signal, and
tocross-lingual transfer, deriving the direction in
a high-resource language and applying it in a low-
resource one. The closest multilingual analogue,
CLAIM (Ye et al., 2025), aligns cross-lingual at-
tention to reduce hallucination; we instead transfer
a contrastive residual-stream direction. For broader
context on VLM interpretability see Dang et al.
(2024).
3 Methodology
3.1 Task and behavioural taxonomy
Each item is an image x, a question q, and a coun-
terfactual caption c that contradicts x in a mini-
mal edits.The model is shown a four-option MCQ
whose options are shuffled across A/B/C—image-
bias(the answer faithful to x),text-bias(faithful
to the false c), and aplausible distractorplus a
fixed optionD = “unable to answer (the caption
conflicts with the image)”. We map output to five
categories; Table 1 gives the safety reading of each,
and the exact MCQ prompt template appears in
Appendix C.

3.5 Mechanistic probing
For each (model, condition) we cache thelast
prompt-tokenhidden state at every residual-stream
layer, stratified per (dataset, language) cell. We fit
ℓ2-regularized logistic probes (5-fold CV) to de-
codeimage-biasvs.text-biasat each layer, and we
report peak cross-validated accuracy. This locates
wherethe conflict outcome stays linearly readable
and whether it survives in low-resource languages.
3.6 Cross-lingual contrastive steering
The probes establish that a linear “honest assert”
signal exists, and we turn it into a mitigation (Ap-
pendix B, Fig. 5). Per language and per target layer
k in the decoder-depth band [0.5,0.8] , we form a
contrastive direction
ˆv(k) = normalize  µ(k) abstain −µ (k) assert  ,(3)
from a fit split (the positive class is conflict-abstain,
widened to also include image-faithful answers
when abstentions run too few). At inference we add
αˆv(k) to the residual stream through forward hooks
and sweep α∈ {−8,−4,0,4,8} , selecting the α
that maximizes conflict-awareness (CR) while we
monitor anoff-targetperception cost (the same
steering applied to the caption-free control).Cross-
lingual transfer:we apply each high-resource
(English) vector to every target language’s held-
out eval split and report Transfer Gap(S→T) =
TR[S→T]−TR[T→T] , the excess text-override
that a foreign-fit vector leaves relative to the native
one.

4.2 Separating text- override from blindness
Raw text-bias conflates two things: a model can
miss the image answer because it follows the
caption or because it never could read the im-
age. Perception-control separates them (Table 4).
Qwen2.5-VL-7B looks safest on raw text-bias
(6.9%, the lowest of all models) yet posts by far
thehighestoverride share ( 0.81). SEA-LION
(0.55) and Qwen3-VL ( 0.51) follow. InternVL3-
2B’s alarming 55.3% text-bias, by contrast, maps
to an override share of only 0.36, because much
of its text-following lands on items it could not
perceive anyway. This delivers the core method-
ological payoff:override share re-ranks the mod-
els and exposes “quietly unsafe” abstainers that
raw rates exonerate(Appendix D, Fig. 7).

4.4 The conflict is linearly decodable, and
stays decodable in low-resource languages
Layer-wise probing (Table 5) shows the image-
vs-text outcome stays strongly linearly decodable
from residual stream. Decodability survives the
resource cliff: maximum per-language peak accu-
racy is 0.971 (English) but holds at 0.92 (Telugu)
and 0.95 (Hindi). The signal needed to fix the
behaviour persists in activations even where the
behaviour fails. This feasibility result drives the
steering in §3.6: a direction we estimate in En-
glish stays geometrically available in Telugu (Ap-
pendix F, Fig. 10; the last-layer activation geometry

4.6 Domain generality
The effect does not reduce to one image do-
main. Text-capture stays severe on our natural-
scene COCO items and on the physics and remote-
sensing sets, and drops consistently on the ren-
dered 3D-objects set where perception comes eas-
ier. Model ranking by susceptibility holds across
all four domains (Appendix ??), which marks the
conflict behaviour as a model property rather than
a dataset quirk.
5 Discussion
Implications for AI safety in multilingual de-
ployment.Thesamemodel that partially trusts
its eyes in English turns into a caption follower
(InternVL3 in Hindi: 67% override) or a blanket
abstainer(Qwen2.5-VL-7B in Telugu: 95%) once
the prompt language shifts to one that hundreds of
millions of people speak.
An adversary can exploit both modes: text cap-
ture lets them inject false claims through alt-text or
OCR in a low-resource language; abstention col-
lapse silently removes the visual safety net while
the model still looks “cautious.” The override-gap
design and the per-language taxonomy hand regu-
lators and deployers a concrete, audit instrument.
Behaviour collapses, representation does not.
Our central scientific finding is a dissociation:
behavioural robustness collapses along the re-
source axis whilerepresentationalseparability
holds (probe accuracy 0.92 in Telugu). The model
still “internally registers” that the caption conflicts
with the image; it simply fails to act on that knowl-
edge. That gap makes lightweight steering a plau-
sible mitigation, and our cross-lingual transfer re-
sult confirms it: the honest direction stays shared

6 Conclusion
We presented a multilingual counterfactual bench-
mark that measures whether mVLMs trust their
eyes or defer to a misleading caption, with a
perception-control design. Across nine open
VLMs, three languages, and four datasets,override
share re-ranks the models and exposes a quietly
unsafe abstainer Both failure modes both intensify
from English to Telugu while image-faithfulness
halves.
Linear probes show the conflict stays decod-
able (0.92) even where behaviour collapses, and
we exploit this dissociation with cross-lingual con-
trastive steering: an English-fit direction transfers
to Hindi and Telugu and eliminates text-override
for abstention-amenable models with zero transfer
gap, at a measurable, guardable perceptual cost.
Limitations
• Steering trades off perception.Driving CR
up via α also raises the off-target perception er-
ror: an over-steered model abstains even when
no conflict exists. We report the guard but do
not yet derive a principled α that provably maxi-
mizes conflict-honesty under a perception bud-
get, and the favourable operating point narrows
for text-capture models.
• “Resource level” is a proxy.We order English
> Hindi > Telugu by a coarse, literature-based
notion, and we did not control per-language
training-token counts, tokenizer fertility, or
translation quality. The high probe accuracy
in Telugu argues against pure comprehension
collapse but cannot rule out a translation-quality
contribution.
• Single-forward logit scoringignores chain-of-
thought; “thinking” models (GLM) may behave
differently under free decoding.
• Scope.Due to time constraints and limitations
in compute, Bahasa could not be used for eval-
uation. Also, We do not measure gated fron-
tier models, more languages (Tamil, Vietnamese,
Bengali), or free-decoding conditions, and the
transfer matrices for some models rest on small
per-celln.
Ethics Statement
The benchmark serves as a defensive auditing tool,
and the same machinery carries a dual-use surface.
(i) The override-gap metric effectively names the
most caption-suggestible model ×language cells;
we mitigate this by framing results aroundwhich
models to harden or avoid deployingand by releas-
ing mitigation alongside measurement. (ii) Steer-
ing directions are sign-symmetric: negating ˆv
would steertowardcaption-following. We re-
port only the honesty-increasing direction and
the off-target guard that flags degenerate steering.
(iii) The dataset deliberately contains false cap-
tions; it serves evaluation, not training, and should
not enter pretraining corpora. (iv) Abstention is not
a free safe default: abstention collapse shows that
optimizing for it destroys utility for low-resource
speakers, so we caution against treating CR as a
single safety target.
