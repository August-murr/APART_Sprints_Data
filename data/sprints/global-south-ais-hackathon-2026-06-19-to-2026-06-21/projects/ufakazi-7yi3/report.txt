Abstract
Large language models are increasingly deployed in high-stakes environments, including
judicial processes that must remain fair and unbiased. It is therefore essential to ask
whether these models can stay objective when the people they assess do not share the
language of the systems that judge them. We ask whether a model’s judgement of witness
credibility is biased towards the high-resource languages that dominate its training corpus.
We present Ufakazi, a counterbalanced evaluation harness that records a model’s choice
between two evidentially balanced but contradicting witness testimonies, set in a South
African legal framing where English is the sole language of record. By holding content
and position fixed and varying only the testimony language, we isolate the effect of
language on the model’s judgement across English, Afrikaans, isiXhosa and isiZulu.
Across the nine models tested, seven showed a statistically significant preference for
testimonies presented in English, deepening as the language became lower-resourced.
Only Claude Sonnet 4.6 and GPT-5.4 remained near neutral. Models explicitly cited
language or translation when discounting a testimony in up to 62% of cases. These
findings caution strongly against deploying such models in judicial settings without
further investigation into their underlying biases.
Given two contradicting, evidentially balanced witness testimonies that differ only in
the language each is written in, does a large language model systematically prefer one?
We build Ufakazi, a counterbalanced evaluation harness on Inspect, and measure the
language main effect across a panel of frontier models for English, Afrikaans, isiXhosa,
and isiZulu in a South African legal framing. Summarise final results and takeaway
here.

1. Introduction
Language is not a neutral communicative tool that we use to simply convey information. It is a
living embodiment of the culture and world view of the speakers who shaped it [5, 8]. This sentiment
was held strongly by Tolkien, the famous author who primarily viewed himself as a philologist and
linguist. Tolkien believed this so strongly that he created entire mythologies to simply understand
the world his languages would inhabit and the speakers that would use them [22, 23]. The same
sentiment underpins the concept of linguistic relativity, which states that the way we perceive the
world is deeply rooted in the language we speak [24].
Large Language Models (LLMs) probabilistically model language by training on massive corpora
of text. One such corpus is the Common Crawl, which is a large collection of web pages that are
crawled and indexed by the Common Crawl Foundation. Recent statistics indicate that this corpus
is dominated by the English language; roughly 41% of the crawled pages are in English, with the
second most common language being Russian at 6.4%. South African languages barely register
in this corpus, with Afrikaans, isiXhosa and isiZulu registering in less than 0.01% of the crawled
pages [7]. We do not know the exact language representation of the text corpora used to train
frontier models, but given the CommonCrawl case, it is a reasonable assumption that the twelve
official South African languages are under-represented.
Disproportionately training on a subset of languages results in models with markedly poorer
competence in low-resource languages [1, 3], as well as a world view skewed towards that of the
culture embedded in the dominant language [10, 21]. Such underlying biases and weaknesses could
prove to be hugely problematic in a culturally diverse country like South Africa, where twelve official
written languages are recognised. As LLMs become increasingly common in high-stakes judicial
workflows [16], there exists a potential future where they will be used to process, summarise or triage
witness testimonies. Given that many of the underrepresented languages are used by historically
marginalised communities, it is of utmost importance that we test whether their lived experiences
will be suppressed as a result of inherent biases against the languages that they converse in.
In this paper, we present Ufakazi1, an evaluation suite that audits large language models for
language-driven bias when they assess the truthfulness of testimony in a legal context, with a focus
on the South African setting. Ufakazi addresses a single question: Given two contradictory but
evidence-balanced testimonies, does a model favour one account on the basis of the language in
which it is written? Accounting for positional and content biases, a fair model would be equally
probable of preferring either testimony, irrespective of the language it is presented in.
Our main contributions are:
1. A reproducible, counterbalanced harness (Ufakazi) that isolates the language main effect in
forced-choice credibility judgements.
2. An initial subset of scenarios spanning everyday civil suits to criminal cases along with human
Afrikaans translations and machine Afrikaans, isiXhosa and isiZulu translations.
3. Empirical investigations into inherent language-based trust biases in the current generation of
Grok, Claude, Gemini, Gemma, Qwen and GPT models.

2. Related Work
Language conditions model values and behaviour
Rao et al.[18] tested models by presenting them with moral dilemmas and a corresponding ethical
policy. They found that although GPT-4 was an exceptional ethical reasoner, it still had a clear
bias towards the moral values of English-speaking societies. This potentially reflects a bias either
explicitly or implicitly encoded into the model’s training data. This work was extended by Agarwal
et al.[2] who showed that models’ ethics are at least partially conditioned on the language in which
they are prompted. Their findings confirmed that GPT-4 was the most consistent and unbiased
ethical reasoner across languages, whilst ChatGPT and Llama2-70B-Chat exhibited significant
moral value bias when evaluated in languages other than English.
Capability gaps in low-resource and African languages
Africa currently has over 2000 languages, and the majority face challenges integrating into LLMs
due to a lack of data, limited computational resources, and insufficient Natural Language Processing
(NLP) tools [12]. Although there have been efforts focused on creating foundational monolingual
models specifically designed for low-resource languages, there remains an underrepresentation of
the majority of African languages. With only 42 languages meaningfully supported by LLMs [12],
there are still strides to make to address underrepresentation across models. Several efforts include
advanced machine translation models and fine-tuning models based on low-resource data [17].
However, whilst these works clearly show a lack of capability in low-resource languages, they do not
investigate whether this affects credibility judgements.
Content-independent decision biases
Beyond the choice of languages, there are other biases that models have been shown to have.
Ermakova et al.[9] found that LLMs, although remarkable at generative and reasoning capabilities,
exhibit systematic cognitive biases in their responses. The authors noted three interrelated forms of
biases: confirmation, positional and framing. Through a series of controlled experiments, the authors
found that models tend to reinforce the premises embedded in the user’s query. Furthermore, the
models’ conclusions varied based on the framing of the input. LLMs positional bias has also been
highlighted in [15]. The authors showed that position bias systematically underweights information
based on its location; however, this is model-dependent. This positional bias was also shown to
occur across English, Russian, German, Hindi, and Vietnamese. Hence, it is of critical importance
to take into consideration in our work.
LLMs in legal settings
The use of LLMs in the legal sector has been increasing rapidly; however, their adoption has raised
concerns. The first includes the model’s ability to hallucinate and generate fabricated information
when drafting policies. Secondly, attorneys have noted that the training data used for LLMs contain
inherent biases which can perpetuate existing disparities and inequalities [6].
In the context of South Africa, the inherent bias presents a greater concern due to the cultural
complexities within the geopolitical landscape [16]. Within this setting, AI introduces a tension that
does not arise in the same form elsewhere. The motivation for Ufakazi was largely drawn from the

language biases affects how much a testimony is trusted in forced-choice credibility judgements. We
are also particularly focused on how this affects the underresourced African languages commonly
used in the South African legal system.
3. Methods
Ufakazi is a controlled audit of model decisions, in the tradition of Tamkin et al.[20], whereby we
evaluate model bias based on the language in which a testimony is presented. The setting in which
we test this is based on the legal setting within South Africa, whereby we have a case and two
witnesses presenting their testimonies. We hold the evidential content of a testimony pair constant
and vary the language in which a testimony is written, measuring the differential treatment. We
impose a forced-choice judgement with position-swap counterbalancing determining if there is any
position bias [15, 25] in the judgements made. We depart from prior multilingual bias work in two
ways. First, whereas earlier studies vary the prompt or query language [2] or match the answer
language to the query language [25], we hold the instruction language fixed and vary only the
language of one piece of evidence within the prompt. The instruction language is kept in English.
This was done to mimic the standard presented in South African courts [19], where English is the
sole language of record, while witnesses may testify in their own language. Second, our dependent
variable is the believed veracity of contradictory, evidence-balanced testimony rather than answer
quality or factual accuracy, so there is no ground-truth label and the model’s judgement is recorded
rather than graded. The languages we investigated were English, Afrikaans, isiZulu and isiXhosa. It
should be noted that isiZulu and isiXhosa were translated using Google Translate2. Afrikaans was
transcribed by both a native speaker and Google Translate. To ensure reproducibility and alignment
with standard safety-eval tooling, we made use of the Inspect3 framework [4] in our harness.
Dataset
The dataset comprises 20 hand-authored artificial scenarios. The written data set was designed to
ensure there were no immediate biases, such as descriptions of skin colour. Each scenario presents a
single disputed event witnessed by two parties, and contains exactly two testimonies, labelled A and
B, that give contradictory accounts of that event. Care was taken to avoid confirmation or framing
biases in the construction of these scenarios. The scenarios span everyday civil and minor criminal
disputes through to serious criminal matters (Table 3), so that any language effect can be examined
across domains of differing gravity rather than a single setting.

Models
All models that were used in this study were accessed via OpenRouter5. By doing so we could better
ensure a consistent format and uniform experimental setup across models from different labs. Our
model selection includes the following: Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.4, GPT-4o-mini, Gemini 3.5 Flash,
Grok 4.3, Qwen 3.7 Plus, and the open-weights Gemma 3 family. This selection was motivated by a
desire to investigate the impact of country of origin, scale and reasoning capabilities on language bias.
We specifically make use of the Gemma 3 instruction-tuned ladder (4B, 12B, 27B) as a mechanism
with which to investigate the scale effect.
The generation settings were fixed per model class. Non-reasoning models made use of temperature
of 0 and requested that the logprobs were returned along with the model outputs. They were also
capped to 200 tokens as the scenarios were not particularly complex. Reasoning models were not
required to return logprobs, as this requirement was not supported by all of them and resulted in
broken pipelines. They were also allowed a slightly larger token budget of 400 tokens.
4. Results
We evaluated nine models using the experimental design outlined in Section 3: five reasoning
models (Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.4, Gemini 3.5 Flash, Grok 4.3, Qwen 3.7 Plus), the Gemma 3
instruction-tuned scale ladder (4B, 12B, 27B), and GPT-4o-mini as a non-reasoning baseline. For
each model, we first split the scenarios into a balanced arm to determine whether there was content
or positional bias within the models. We then determine if the models preference changes towards a
testimony when that is translated to a different language.

The bias is in the reasoning, not only the choice
Our scoring metric included a one-sentence rationale that the model needed to provide for its choice.
This allowed us to analyse the model’s justification. Across all models, 37.1% of the cross-language
trials in which the model chose the English testimony justifies that choice by explicitly naming the
language or the act of translation (4,213 of 11,370 such trials), as opposed to citing the evidence.
The share rises with the size of the behavioural bias. Figure 4 illustrated this, from 9% for GPT-5.4
and 23% for GPT-4o-mini up to 61% for Grok and 62% for Gemma-27B.

5. Discussions and Limitations
Our main finding is that most current models do not treat a testimony’s language as a minor factor
in its credibility. By holding evidence and position constant, seven of the nine tested models discount
an account simply for being written in a language other than English. The size of that effect reflects
how well-resourced the language is, with low-resourced languages such as isiZulu and isiXhosa
experiencing a greater decline than Afrikaans. This seems to be in line with the composition of the
corpora these models are trained on. Our findings are also consistent with the view that a model
trained predominantly on standard English encodes the norms and perspectives of that language’s
speakers [10]. What makes the effect more than a statistical measure is the fact that the models
state it in their reasoning justifications. In 37.1% of the trials where a model preferred the English

These results are directly consequential to the South African setting that motivates Ufakazi. If

observed that they would foster an inherent mistrust towards the non-English rendered account. Such
behaviour runs counter to the core of the South African constitution. We raise this as a empirical
evidence to strongly caution against the use of most current models for evaluating testimonies in
multilingual justice settings [16].
Limitations
There are several constraints that bound how our results should be read and interpreted. The first
and foremost being translation provenance. As we only had English and Afrikaans expert speakers
at our disposal, further investigation is required into the isiZulu and isiXhosa testimonies. Although
our translation provenance control suggests that machine translation has a minimal impact on our
results, we cannot guarantee the quality of the isiZulu and isiXhosa testimonies, nor the absence of
any translation artefacts. Approaching native speakers for the Nguni languages are a valuable next
step.
The next limitation is that our dataset was small and synthetic with budget constraints limiting the
number of scenarios and number of epochs we could utilize in our experiments. To truly investigate
how models would affect real people, real-world samples would need to be collected at scale. With
the severity of the observed bias, we believe our small dataset to still carry sufficient signal, but a
larger dataset would be beneficial for ensuring statistical rigour.
As our confidence interval estimates rest only on balanced scenarios, there were some cases where
models had a small subset of balanced scenarios and hence had wide confidence intervals. In these
cases it would be especially beneficial to increase the dataset size as their current results can only
be treated as indicative and not as conclusive.

whether translation on its own is good enough to convey the necessary cultural contexts.
Future work
The future work includes replacing the machine-translated isiZulu and isiXhosa with native-speaker
translations. This would would separate language bias from comprehension failure and let these
low-resource arms carry the same weight as Afrikaans.
Beyond translation accuracy, the suite should be extended from synthetic scenarios toward the real
testimonies faced in the South African court systems with more languages tested. The framing
question we present as to whether the prompt language shifts the model’s assumed legal framework,
remains open and fits naturally into the same harness.
6. Conclusions
In this paper we aimed to test whether when an LLM judges two conflicting testimonies to believe,
does the language of an account change the verdict? Across the nine models tested, we showed that
seven showed a bias towards testimonies presented in English.

given time constraints we made use of LLM assistance in an augmentative capacity. Specifically, we

processes, but then handed over large portions of the implementation to Claude Code. We adopted
a very hands-on approach here, where iteration was firmly human-in-the-loop. When it came to
