Safety Targeted Embedding Exploit via Refinement:
Mechanistic Interpretability as an Attack Vector
Joshua Adrian Cahyono
joshuaadrianc@gmail.com
Abstract
Mechanistic interpretability research has shown that LLM safety fine-tuning en-
codes a refusal mechanism as a single geometric direction in the residual stream.
We demonstrate that this finding is directly exploitable: an attacker with white-
box access can use the model’s own internal structure to surgically bypass its
safety filter. STEER (Safety Targeted Embedding Exploit via Refinement) uses
gradient attribution against the identified refusal direction to select which words
in a harmful prompt most activate the safety filter, then iteratively replaces them
with low-resource language translations, collapsing the refusal signal while pre-
serving harmful intent. Across six open-source 8B-parameter models, STEER
achieves attack success rates (ASR) of up to 93% on JailbreakBench and 96.7%
on AdvBench, outperforming both random code-switching (CSRT) and Greedy
Coordinate Gradient (GCG) in success rate and speed. A Fisher Linear Dis-
criminant (FLD) analysis automatically localises the specific transformer layer
where the refusal direction is most separable, functioning both as an attack cal-
ibration tool and as a structural vulnerability probe. STEER prompts transfer
to GPT-4o-mini at 35.5% ASR without any closed-model access, suggesting the
underlying representational structure is shared across architectures. These results
demonstrate that mech interp tools are dual-use: the same techniques used to
understand safety circuits can be turned against them, and the field needs principled
defences that are informed by the same interpretability methods. Code is available
at https://github.com/JvThunder/STEER.
1 Introduction
Mechanistic interpretability research aims to understandhow LLMs implement capabilities by reverse-
engineering the internal circuits responsible for observed behaviour. One of the most practically
significant findings in this space is that safety fine-tuning concentrates the refusal mechanism into
a single linear direction in the residual stream [Arditi et al., 2024]. This is a clean, interpretable
structure, and it is also a single point of failure.
STEER exploits this directly. By reading the model’s refusal direction from its internal representations
and computing gradient attribution of each input word against that direction, STEER identifies exactly
which parts of a prompt are triggering the safety filter. It then replaces those words, highest attribution
first, with translations in low-resource languages. The result is a prompt that preserves the harmful
intent but avoids activating the refusal direction, not by brute-force perturbation, but by reading the
model’s own internal safety circuit and working around it.
Code-switching attacks on LLM safety have been explored before [Deng et al., 2024, Yoo et al., 2025,
Upadhayay and Behzadan, 2024, Singhania et al., 2025, Huang et al., 2025], and the intuition that
non-English phrasings undermine English-centric safety training is well established. What STEER
adds is the use of mech interp findings to make the attack targeted and efficient: rather than replacing

The paper makes four contributions: (1) the STEER pipeline, which reaches up to 93%/96.7%

exploitable structure is not an artefact of one training recipe but a systematic property of current
alignment methods.
The broader implication for the mech interp community is that interpretability findings about safety
circuits are not merely descriptive: they provide an attack surface. Defences need to be designed with
the same mechanistic understanding of how safety is encoded, rather than relying on surface-level
robustness heuristics.
2 Background
The refusal direction: a mech interp target. Arditi et al. [2024] showed that safety fine-tuning
encodes a single geometric direction r in the residual stream, computed as the normalised mean
difference between harmful and benign hidden states:
r = ¯xH − ¯xB
∥¯xH − ¯xB∥2

A prompt’s refusal score s(p) = xp · r reliably predicts whether the model will refuse; projecting this
direction out of every hidden state eliminates refusal entirely. Representation Engineering [Zou et al.,
2023a] and Inference-Time Intervention [Li et al., 2024] extend this picture, showing that steering
vectors derived from hidden states can broadly control model behaviour. Wollschläger et al. [2025]
further characterise safety as a cone in representation space. Taken together, these findings paint a
coherent mechanistic picture: safety is not diffusely distributed across the network but concentrated
in a specific, identifiable subspace. STEER exploits this concentration directly.
English-centric safety alignment. RLHF [Ouyang et al., 2022], the dominant alignment method,
trains on human-annotated English comparisons, so the safety signal concentrates around English
token patterns and does not transfer reliably to low-resource or mixed-language inputs. DPO [Rafailov
et al., 2023] shares this bias. Constitutional AI [Bai et al., 2022] and red-teaming pipelines [Perez
et al., 2022, Ganguli et al., 2022] likewise remain predominantly English-centric. The consequence
is that the refusal direction identified by mech interp is calibrated on a narrow language slice: it is
a valid but incomplete circuit. Non-English phrasings of harmful requests can avoid activating this
direction even when a human would immediately recognise them as harmful, because the direction
was never calibrated on those inputs.
This risk is not uniform: it is sharply regional. The safety guarantee degrades exactly for the
languages that are thin in alignment data: low-resource African languages (Swahili, Yoruba), South
and Southeast Asian languages (Hindi, Thai, Vietnamese, Tagalog), and the regional languages of
the world’s largest multilingual populations (Javanese and Sundanese alone have over 100 million
speakers in Indonesia). Speakers of these languages, and the communities that code-switch between
them and English every day, are precisely the users the safety circuit was never trained on, so they get
a weaker guarantee than English speakers with no sign that anything has lapsed. The same gap is an
obvious vector for an attacker who simply phrases the harmful request in an under-aligned language.
So the threat STEER describes cuts both ways: a fairness gap for regional and multilingual users, and
an attack surface.
Gradient-based and black-box jailbreaks. GCG [Zou et al., 2023b] appends adversarial token
suffixes and optimises them via gradient search. It works by finding inputs that suppress refusal,
but without grounding in the model’s internal safety geometry: it treats the model as a black box
from a gradient perspective rather than reading the structure that mech interp has already identified.
Black-box alternatives such as PAIR [Chao et al., 2023], Tree-of-Attacks [Mehrotra et al., 2023], and
AutoDAN [Liu et al., 2024] forgo internal access entirely. STEER’s distinguishing feature is that it

3 STEER Attack
STEER runs four steps in sequence: localise the refusal direction’s most legible layer via FLD analysis,
paraphrase the request to reduce surface-level keyword activation, compute gradient attribution of
each word against the refusal direction, and iteratively translate the highest-attribution words until
the model complies. Figure 1 shows the full pipeline.
3.1 Layer selection via Fisher Linear Discriminant
The refusal direction is not equally legible at every transformer layer. We localise it automatically
using the Fisher Linear Discriminant ratio:
F (l) = (µ(l) H − µ(l) B )2
σ(l)2 H + σ(l)2 B , (2)
where µ(l) H , µ(l) B are mean refusal score projections for harmful and benign prompts at layer l, and σ2
terms are their variances. We setl∗ = arg maxl F (l) using 100 harmful and 100 benign prompts from
JBB as a one-time calibration, before any attack. For Llama-3-8B this is layer 17; for GLM-4-9B
it is layer 24. The FLD score also functions as a structural vulnerability metric: a high, sharp peak
indicates safety is concentrated in a single layer and the model is structurally easier to attack; a flat
profile suggests more distributed encoding that would require a different attack strategy.
3.2 Paraphrase preprocessing
Before doing anything with gradients, we paraphrase the harmful request with GPT-4o to replace
explicit keywords with indirect phrasing (e.g., “write a defamatory article” becomes “compose a
critical exposition insinuating problematic behaviour”). This lowers the starting refusal score and
gives gradient attribution a cleaner signal to work with, and removing it costs a substantial amount of
ASR in practice.

The 11 languages cover three sources of coverage gap in the safety circuit’s calibration: low-resource
languages (Javanese, Sundanese, Swahili, Yoruba) that rarely appear in safety fine-tuning data;
non-Latin scripts (Thai, Korean, Hindi, Arabic) that introduce tokenisation-level shift; and regional
Southeast Asian languages (Indonesian, Tagalog, Vietnamese, Thai) with a large and fast-growing
user base. We weight the pool toward regional and lower-resource languages on purpose, rather than
the high-resource European languages that dominate prior multilingual jailbreak work, because the
under-aligned regional languages are where the refusal circuit is most exposed. That makes them
both the most effective attack vector and the populations most exposed to the gap.

4 Experiments
4.1 Setup
We evaluate six open-source instruction-tuned models at 7–9B parameters: Llama-3-8B, Mistral-7B,
Gemma-7B, Qwen3-8B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, and GLM-4-9B, all loaded in float16 on
CUDA hardware. Benchmarks are JailbreakBench (JBB, n = 100) [Chao et al., 2024], HarmBench
(n = 200) [Mazeika et al., 2024], and AdvBench (n = 520) [Zou et al., 2023b].
Three baselines are included: Direct (original prompt, unmodified), CSRT [Yoo et al., 2025] (random
code-switching with the same language pool and iteration budget), and GCG [Zou et al., 2023b]
(adversarial suffix optimisation). A GPT-4o judge scores each response for refusal and harmfulness;
a jailbreak counts only when both conditions are met.
4.2 Attack success rate
Table 1 reports @8 ASR across all three benchmarks. Every model reaches 0% ASR against
unmodified prompts; safety fine-tuning works on English, so all subsequent gains are attributable to
the attack mechanism.
STEER achieves the highest @8 ASR on every model-benchmark pair. The gap is largest where other
methods stall: CSRT and GCG both plateau below 50% on DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen3-8B on JBB,
while STEER reaches 80% and 85%. GCG is competitive on Mistral-7B and Gemma-7B but collapses
on GLM-4-9B (39% vs. STEER’s 93%), illustrating how architecture-dependent suffix optimisation
can be. STEER’s performance, by contrast, is consistent across architectures, a consequence of
targeting the shared refusal direction structure rather than model-specific surface features.
4.3 Iteration efficiency
Gradient attribution pays off most visibly in the first iteration. On Gemma-7B (JBB), STEER @1 is
88% versus 71% for CSRT; on DeepSeek-R1, 74% versus 38%. CSRT eventually catches up on some
models given enough budget, but it wastes iterations on words that do not activate the refusal circuit.

4.4 Refusal score as a proxy for compliance
Figure 3 shows refusal score distributions across all prompt–iteration pairs for Llama-3-8B on JBB.
Refused responses ( n = 167, mean +3.6) and non-refused responses ( n = 164, mean −1.2) sit
cleanly on opposite sides of zero. A Mann-Whitney U test (p <0.001) and Kolmogorov-Smirnov test
(D = 0.766, p <0.001) confirm the separation is statistically robust. This validates the mechanistic
hypothesis: the dot product with the mech-interp-identified r is the actual decision variable the model
uses to produce refusals, not just a plausible proxy.
4.5 Black-box transferability
Table 2 shows transfer ASR when STEER, CSRT, and GCG prompts crafted against open-source mod-
els are submitted to GPT-4o without modification. STEER averages 35.5% across all 18 combinations,
winning 14 of them, compared to 29.3% for GCG and 13.9% for CSRT. The transferability result
has an important mechanistic interpretation: STEER prompts target the refusal direction structure
that Arditi et al. [2024] showed is common across models trained with similar alignment procedures.
Because the prompts exploit the shared geometry of that direction rather than model-specific artefacts,
they partially carry over to architectures never seen during attack construction.

5 Discussion
Mech interp as an attack enabler. STEER is a concrete pathway from a mech interp finding to an
operational attack. The refusal direction of Arditi et al. [2024] was a scientific description of how
safety is implemented; it turns out that knowing where the circuit lives and what it responds to is
enough to route around it. This is not a one-off. Any mech interp result that localises a specific safety
mechanism is, in principle, also a blueprint for disabling it. The FLD makes the point quantitative:
the sharpness of the Fisher peak predicts attack difficulty, and a concentrated refusal circuit is easier
to attack than a diffuse one.
Implications for defence. The same tools point at sturdier safety encodings. If safety sits in
one direction at one layer, the fix is to spread it: encode refusal redundantly across layers and
directions so there is no single point of failure, or train the direction on a broader distribution,
multilingual and code-switched inputs included, so the circuit is calibrated on what it actually has
to handle. Representation Engineering [Zou et al., 2023a] already gives the tools to audit and edit
these representations. Surface-level defences (output filters, prompt classifiers, ungrounded data
augmentation) will not touch the structural vulnerability STEER exploits; the refusal direction stays a
narrow target until alignment distributes or hardens it at the representational level. The FLD doubles
as the audit: a sharp single-layer peak is evidence of a specific vulnerability, so checking the sharpness
of a model’s FLD curve before deployment says how brittle its safety encoding is.
Limitations. STEER needs white-box access to read the refusal direction and compute attribution,
so it does not apply directly to closed-source APIs; the transfer results show partial carry-over to
GPT-4o-mini, but we have not built a dedicated black-box version. Evaluation stays in the 7–9B
range across six open-source models, and we do not claim the ASR numbers hold at frontier scale.
The pipeline leans on an automated GPT-4o judge and on machine translation into 11 languages,
and both can err; translation quality is shakiest for exactly the lowest-resource languages (Javanese,
Sundanese, Yoruba), which can bias the per-language picture. The dual-criterion check (non-refusing

Ethics and potential for misuse. STEER is offensive in form but defensive in intent: it is a
red-teaming tool that documents a safety gap so it can be closed, and the regional and multilingual
users it flags as under-protected are exactly the people disclosure is meant to help. The method is still
dual-use. It could be used to pull harmful content out of deployed models, and the harm lands hardest
on the low-resource and regional-language communities whose coverage is already weakest. We keep
the incremental risk low by sticking to responsible-disclosure practice: we test only on established
public benchmarks (JailbreakBench, HarmBench, AdvBench) rather than fresh harmful prompts, we
report aggregate success rates instead of releasing ready-to-run jailbroken prompts, and the code we
release is meant for safety evaluation and defensive auditing. The ingredients are already public,
the refusal direction [Arditi et al., 2024] and code-switching attacks [Deng et al., 2024, Yoo et al.,
2025]; what we add is making the resulting vulnerability legible enough to push for the multilingual
and representation-level defences above. Expanding safety coverage to regional and low-resource
languages is the point, not a nice-to-have.
6 Conclusion
STEER reaches up to 96.7% ASR in eight word translations, beating random code-switching and
GCG across six models, by grounding the attack in mechanistic interpretability rather than brute-
force perturbation. Safety alignment stores its refusal circuit as a single linear direction in a middle
transformer layer; gradient attribution against that direction points to the words carrying it, and
translating those words into languages the circuit was never calibrated on switches it off without
losing the prompt’s intent.
The message for the mech interp community is that findings about safety circuits are dual-use:
the same FLD analysis that locates the refusal circuit also measures its brittleness, and the same
attribution that explains what it attends to tells an attacker where to intervene. The defence has to be
built with the same understanding: harden or distribute the internal representation rather than bolt on
surface filters, and close the regional and multilingual coverage gap STEER exploits.
