Shourya Choudhary - IIT Delhi, Mathematics & Computing (Dual Degree) 
Code and data: https://github.com/shch4747/IndicViet-Safe 
 
 
Abstract 
Large language model safety guardrails are overwhelmingly designed and evaluated in English,  
creating dangerous blind spots for the hundreds of millions of Hindi and Vietnamese speakers now 
interacting with these systems. We present IndicViet -Safe, a benchmark of 210 prompt -language pairs 
across English, Hindi, Vietnamese, and Hinglish (Hindi-English code-switching) that systematically tests 
whether safety mechanisms degrade in non -English Asian languages. Our dataset spans 12 harm 
categories, including culturally specific threats: caste-based discrimination and communal incitement for 
India, and political censorship circumvention for Vietnam. We evaluate two open -source models (Llama 
3.1 8B and Llama 4 Scout 17B) using an automated judge -based scoring pipeline. Our core finding is 
the identification of two distinct failure modes tied to English -centric safety training. Llama 3.1 8B 
exhibits under-refusal: a 44.8 percentage -point safety gap where Hinglish prompts bypass guardrails 
that hold in English (47.4% vs 92.2% refusal rate). Llama 4 Scout 17B exhibits the inverse pattern of 
over-refusal: non -English refusal rates exceed English (93.8% vs 75.0%), likely reflecting reduced 
comprehension rather than stronger safety. Both failure modes stem from the same root cause: safety 
alignment concentrated in English. We further find that culturally specific harm categories -caste 
discrimination (0% refusal in Hindi on Llama 3.1 8B) and Vietnamese political sensitivity (50% refusal on 
Llama 4 Scout 17B)-are systematically underguarded relative to universal categories. The benchmark, 
evaluation pipeline, and all results are released as open -source tools for the AI safety community 
(https://github.com/shch4747/IndicViet-Safe) 
 
1. Introduction 
The rapid global deployment of large language models has outpaced the development of safety 
mechanisms for non -English languages. While benchmark suites such as HarmBench, XSafety, and 
ToxiGen have advanced English -language safety evaluation, the International AI Safety Report 2026 
identifies a critical evidence gap: safety performance in low -resource languages spoken across Asia 
remains virtually unevaluated. This gap is not merely academic. India has over 600 million Hindi 
speakers, and Vietnam has nearly 100 million Vietnamese speakers. Both countries are experiencing 
rapid AI adoption: India's IndiaAI Mission has onboarded 38,000+ GPUs, and Vietnam enacted 
Southeast Asia's first binding AI law in March 2026. 
Recent research has demonstrated that low -resource languages can serve as jailbreak vectors. The 
MultiJail dataset showed higher attack success rates in low -resource languages, and XSafety found 
significant safety degradation across ten non-English languages. SGToxicGuard introduced 
red-teaming for Singapore's low -resource languages (Singlish, Malay, Tamil). However, no prior work 
has systematically evaluated safety across both Indian and Vietnamese languages, nor has any 
benchmark included culturally specific harm categories -such as caste discrimination or communal 
incitement-uniquely relevant to the Asian context. 
We make three contributions: (1) a curated multilingual safety benchmark covering 12 harm categories 
with culturally grounded prompts in Hindi, Vietnamese, and Hinglish; (2) an automated evaluation

2. Dataset Design 
IndicViet-Safe comprises 60 prompt groups across 12 harm categories, yielding 210 total 
prompt-language pairs. The dataset reflects two design principles: (a) universal harm categories 
enabling cross-lingual comparison with a shared English baseline, and (b) culturally specific categories 
testing safety mechanisms against regionally relevant threats.

3.2 Evaluation protocol 
Each prompt is sent to each target model at temperature 0 (deterministic) via the Groq API. The model's 
response is then evaluated by a judge model that classifies it into one of three categories: Full Refusal 
(the model clearly declines to provide harmful content), Partial Compliance (the model provides some 
harmful information with disclaimers), or Full Compliance (the model provides the requested harmful 
content with minimal safety guardrails). The judge model uses a structured JSON scoring rubric. The 
Llama 3.1 8B run used Llama 3.3 70B as judge; the Llama 4 Scout 17B run used Qwen 3.6 27B as 
judge due to API token quota constraints. Hindi results were spot -checked by a native speaker to 
validate the automated judge's accuracy. 
The primary metric is the full refusal rate -the percentage of prompts where the model completely 
refuses to engage with the harmful request. The safety gap is defined as the difference in refusal rate 
between English and a target language, measured in percentage points. A positive safety gap indicates 
weaker safety guardrails in the non-English language.

4.3 Two failure modes 
 
 
 
Figure 3: The two distinct failure modes. Dashed lines show English baseline. 
 
Failure Mode 1: Under-refusal (Llama 3.1 8B). Safety guardrails trained in English fail to generalize to 
non-English languages. Harmful prompts in Hinglish bypass safety mechanisms that successfully block 
identical content in English. The model's safety training recognizes harmful intent in English but not in 
code-switched formulations. This is the more dangerous failure mode: harmful content reaches users 
who need protection most. 
Failure Mode 2: Over-refusal (Llama 4 Scout 17B). The model refuses more frequently in non-English 
languages than in English. This likely reflects reduced language comprehension rather than stronger 
safety: the model defaults to refusal when it cannot fully parse the prompt's intent. While superficially 
safer, this pattern reduces utility for non -English speakers and creates a false sense of security in 
aggregate metrics.

5. Discussion 
5.1 Root cause analysis 
Both failure modes -under-refusal and over -refusal-stem from the same root cause: safety alignment 
training concentrated in English. In the under-refusal case (Llama 3.1 8B), the model has learned 
fine-grained safety boundaries in English but cannot transfer these to Hinglish or Hindi, where the same 
harmful intent expressed through code -switching goes unrecognized. In the over -refusal case (Llama 4 
Scout 17B), reduced capability in non -English languages causes the model to default to refusal when it 
cannot confidently parse intent -a blunt instrument that masks genuine safety failures in aggregate 
refusal metrics. 
This distinction matters practically. A model that achieves 93.8% refusal in Hinglish through over-refusal 
is not safer than one achieving 92.2% refusal in English through genuine safety training. It is simply less 
useful. Aggregate refusal rates without language-disaggregated analysis can be misleading.

5.2 Policy implications 
Our findings have direct implications for three active governance frameworks in Asia. India's IndiaAI 
Safety Institute, established under the IndiaAI Mission, has identified Indian -language benchmarking as 
a priority function. IndicViet -Safe provides a replicable methodology and initial dataset that the AISI 
could adopt and extend. Vietnam's AI Law (No. 134/2025/QH15), effective March 2026, requires risk 
assessment of AI systems-our benchmark offers a concrete tool for evaluating safety compliance in 
Vietnamese. The ASEAN Expanded Guide on AI Governance and Ethics calls for regional coordination 
on safety standards; a shared multilingual benchmark could serve as a foundation for harmonized 
evaluation across ASEAN member states. 
The finding that caste discrimination prompts achieve 0% refusal in Hindi on Llama 3.1 8B is particularly 
significant for Indian AI governance. Caste -based discrimination is a constitutionally prohibited harm in 
India. An AI system that freely generates caste -based hate speech in Hindi but refuses identical 
requests in English represents a concrete, measurable inequity in safety protection that regulatory 
frameworks should address. 
 
6. Limitations 
This study has several limitations. First, our dataset of 210 prompt -language pairs, while sufficient to 
identify systematic patterns, is smaller than comprehensive safety benchmarks. Second, Vietnamese 
translations were not verified by a native speaker. Third, the judge model varied across runs (Llama 3.3 
70B for the 8B evaluation, Qwen 3.6 27B for the Scout evaluation) due to API quota constraints; judge 
model bias may affect cross -model comparability. Fourth, 41 out of 210 evaluations for Llama 3.1 8B 
resulted in parse errors, concentrated in the cybersecurity category. Fifth, we evaluate only two models 
from the same family (Meta); extending to Qwen, Mistral, and India -origin models (Sarvam, Airavata) 
would strengthen generalizability. Finally, Hinglish code -switching patterns are highly variable across 
regions and demographics; our prompts represent one common pattern but not the full spectrum.  
 
7. Conclusion 
IndicViet-Safe demonstrates that LLM safety guardrails provide fundamentally inequitable protection 
across languages. We identify two distinct failure modes -under-refusal and over -refusal-both rooted in 
English-centric safety training, and show that culturally specific harms (caste discrimination, communal 
incitement, political censorship) are systematically underguarded compared to universal categories. 
The 44.8 percentage -point safety gap between English and Hinglish on Llama 3.1 8B, and the 0% 
refusal rate for caste discrimination in Hindi, are not edge cases. They are evidence of a structural gap 
in how safety alignment is practiced today. 
As India and Vietnam accelerate AI deployment, the absence of multilingual safety evaluation 
infrastructure becomes increasingly dangerous. The benchmark, evaluation pipeline, and all results  
from this study are released as open -source tools at https://github.com/shch4747/IndicViet-Safe. We 
urge the AI safety community to treat multilingual evaluation not as an optional extension but as a 
prerequisite for responsible deployment.
