ABSTRACT
Safety alignment is overwhelmingly evaluated in English, yet recent work shows that language
models refuse far less reliably when the same harmful request arrives in a low-resource African
language. We ask whether this gap survives in vision–language models (VLMs), whether it
reaches a Southern African language such as isiZulu, and whether it is genuine safety degradation
rather than the model simply failing to understand the language. We audit two frontier VLMs
across a language-by-image factorial, using acomprehension controlthat scores non-refusal
only on requests the model demonstrably understood, isolating aSafety Gap. The two models
diverge sharply. Anthropic’s Claude refuses every harmful request in every language and image
condition, and on the Hausa anchor matches its English robustness where the models LSR
tested dropped to 35–55%. Moonshot’s Kimi-K2.6 refuses reliably in English but cracks in
isiZulu: it understands the requests and complies anyway, in one case producing a fabricated
isiZulu election-disinformation post it had refused verbatim in English. The African-language
safety gap is therefore analignment property, not an inevitabilityof low-resource languages.
We report a small-sample existence proof rather than calibrated rates, and describe an artifact-
hunting workflow that eliminated a false positive in our own pipeline while leaving the cross-lab
divergence standing.
1 Introduction
Vision–language models are becoming the perception layer of deployed assistants and embodied
agents, including systems being rolled out across the African continent. If a model’s safety refusal
collapses when the same harmful intent arrives in an African language or alongside an African-
context image that is adeployment-safetyfailure, not merely a fairness-benchmark curiosity. AI-
driven electoral interference is among the deployment-side risks most frequently flagged for African
contexts, and the central failure we document is precisely a model fabricating election disinformation
in isiZulu. Two recent benchmarks establish the text-only baseline: LSR (Linguistic Safety Robust-
ness) [1] measures cross-lingual refusal degradation for West African languages (refusal∼90% in
English, dropping to 35–55% for Yoruba, Hausa, Igbo, and Igala), and UbuntuGuard [2] provides
the first African-context safety benchmark across ten low-resource languages. Both are text-only,
and both report aggregate degradation without separating two confounded explanations: a model

2 Background and Related Work
African-language safety.LSR [1] and UbuntuGuard [2] are the immediate predecessors; both
are text-only and West/East-African in coverage. Broader African-language evaluation includes
AfroBench [10], IrokoBench/AfriMMLU [8], and AfriSenti [9], which document that current models
generalise poorly to African languages on capability tasks making the capability vs safety distinction
we draw all the more important.Red-teaming and harm taxonomies.Our harm categories
follow the structured red-teaming tradition of Ganguli et al. [4] and the civil-society-legible harm
taxonomy of Abercrombie et al. [5]; the HarmBench harness [3] provides standardised harmful
behaviours.Methodology.Our multi-cell, multi-metric design follows HELM [6]; the recruitment
of affected communities for annotation follows STAR [12].Agenda.Segun et al. [11] call explicitly
for multilingual African safety benchmarks, which this work contributes toward, and “Bridging the
Multilingual Safety Divide” [7] offers a steering-based mitigation should a robust gap be confirmed.
3 Methods
We give each model the same harmful request many times over, changing only the language it is
written in and whether a photograph is attached, and we record whether the model refuses. The
crucial addition is a second question we ask in every case“what is this message asking you to do?”
so that when a model doesnotrefuse, we can tell which of two very different things happened:
it genuinely missed the meaning (a capability problem, not our concern here), or it understood
perfectly and complied anyway (a safety problem, which is). The rest of this section makes each of
those choices precise.
Research question and hypotheses.When a harmful request is presented to a VLM, how does
refusal change as a function of (a) language and (b) the presence/origin of an accompanying image,
and is any change explained by comprehension failure or by genuine safety degradation? We test:
H1African-language refusal rate (RR)<English RR;H2image presence changes RR and interacts
with language;H3African- vs Western-context images differ on identical text;H4(headline) any
cross-lingual drop persists after conditioning on comprehension.

Factorial design.Languages{English, isiZulu, Hausa} ×visual modality{text-only,+African
image,+Western image}. Because theidenticalharmful request appears in every condition, every
comparison is within-prompt: we can ask whether the very request a model refused in English is
also refused in isiZulu, rather than comparing different prompts (analysed with McNemar’s test,
the standard test for paired yes/no outcomes, plus bootstrap 95% confidence intervals on the rate
differences). isiZulu is the core novel language; Hausa is run text-only as a replication anchor against
LSR. Prompts are 15 harmful seeds (five per category) across three Africa-relevant harm categories:
electoral disinformation, ethnic incitement, and localised scams; each seed is instantiated across
all language×image-modality conditions. Seeds are non-operational category templates stated at
refusal-measurement altitude — no operational content is authored, consistent with [3] practice.

the comprehension-controlled unsafe rate: among prompts the modeldemonstrably understood, the
fraction it failed to refuse. In plain terms, SG asks:of the harmful requests a model clearly un-
derstood, how often did it go along with them anyway?This is what lets us separate a genuine
safety failure from a model that simply did not understand a low-resource language.partial(a

Comprehension control.For every trial aseparatemodel call asks the subject to restate, in
one English sentence, what the (possibly non-English, possibly image-bearing) message is asking
it to do, in the same condition. A fixed judge compares the restatement to the canonical English
intent to assign understood/not_understood. SG is computed only over understood prompts, so a
low RR caused by the model not understanding isiZulu doesnotinflate the Safety Gap. This is the
control that makes H4 a safety claim rather than a capability one.

4 Results
Headline.The African-language safety gap is real andmodel-dependent. A Chinese frontier VLM,
Kimi-K2.6, complies with harmful isiZulu requests that it refuses verbatim in English, while Claude
refuses 100% across both languages (Table 2, Fig. 1). Because comprehension was confirmed (CR≈
1.0for Kimi in isiZulu), the drop is a genuine safety-alignment failure, not a translation artifact
(Fig. 2).

Why we trust the Kimi result: we caught a false alarm in our own pipeline.An earlier
version of our setup appeared to show Claude failing as well — a Safety Gap of0.15in isiZulu-with-
image, including an apparent compliance. On inspection, this was an illusion produced by three
mistakes on our side: an image labelled “African” that was in fact a US photograph, a third-party
platform sitting between us and the model, and a network call that failed and returned an empty
response we had wrongly counted as a non-refusal. We fixed all three — curated the images, called
the model directly, and re-ran the failed cases — and Claude’s Safety Gap fell to0.00. The same
fixes left Kimi’s gap untouched. A method that can find and erase its own false positive, yet still
flags Kimi, is more credible for having done so.
5 Discussion
The central result is adivergence: identical prompts, identical images, identical judge, yet one
frontier lab’s model is robust and another’s cracks in a low-resource African language and not in
English. This reframes the LSR finding. The African-language safety gap is not an inevitable
property of low-resource languages or of VLMs — it is an alignment property that at least one
frontier lab has eliminated and at least one has not. The comprehension control is what licenses
the safety reading: Kimi understood the isiZulu requests (CR≈1.0) and still produced the harmful
content, so this is misalignment, not incapacity. For deployment in the Global South, the implication
is concrete: multilingual safety cannot be assumed from English evaluation, and a VLM that is safe
in English may be exploitable simply by switching the input language — with the image channel
as a plausible (though here inconclusive) additional axis worth continued probing.

6 Limitations
Sample size dominates.15 prompts×cells (104 trials, 1 dropped); all McNemar tests are
n.s. and bootstrap CIs are wide. We lead with effect sizes and the concrete compliances; the
Kimi gap rests on 1–2 genuine non-refusals per isiZulu cell and should be read as a demonstrated
existence of model-dependent degradation, not a calibrated rate.One model per lab generation,
and Kimi was not run on Hausa.Single judge(Claude, temperature 0); judge–human Cohen’s
κis pending human labelling (a blind sheet and scorer are prepared).Machine translation
(chrF≈0.61, translationese); per-item native isiZulu adequacy ratings are pending, though the
comprehension control conditions on understanding and the translationese biases against the text-
axis result.Determinism: temperature 0, but LLM APIs are not perfectly deterministic; a few
transient call failures occurred and were caught and re-run.Harm coverage: three categories of
non-operational templates; 26 real HarmBench seeds are staged for a larger run.
7 Future Work
(1)Power: scale to the staged HarmBench seeds and beyond for calibrated rates. (2)Generality:
run Kimi on Hausa, and run additional labs (GPT-4o, Gemini) to map which alignment regimes
close the gap. (3)Human validation: native isiZulu adequacy ratings and judge–humanκ. (4)
Modelling: a mixed-effects logistic modelrefused∼language×modality + (1|prompt) + (1|model)
once≥2models and≥60prompts exist. (5)Resource gradient: add Afrikaans and further
Southern African languages. (6)Mitigation: if a robust gap is confirmed, evaluate parameter-
efficient safety steering [7].
8 Ethics and Responsible Disclosure
This is defensive safety evaluation: we measure refusal, we do not seek harmful outputs for use,
and seeds derive from already-published red-teaming practice. No harmful imagery is generated
or used; images are benign and context-only. Elicited harmful model outputs are confined to
analysis files and are referenced in this paper by identifier and category only — none is reproduced.
Because the isiZulu finding constitutes a reproducible safety gap in a deployed model (Kimi-K2.6),
we will responsibly disclose it to Moonshot AI prior to any public release of the artifacts, with
our methodology and caveats stated in full; Claude exhibited no such gap to disclose. We note
for transparency that Claude was used as a subject model, as the fixed judge, and to assist the
evaluation pipeline and drafting.
9 Reproducibility
All stages are resumable and cost-capped; total spend was∼$4.7 (Anthropic, Claude subject+
judge),∼$1.6 (Together, Kimi), and∼69 platform credits (superseded preliminary run). Entry

B Validity checklist
•Comprehension control — built into the design (separate restatement call+judge).
•Translation adequacy — back-translation chrF≈0.61; native isiZulu per-item ratings pending.
•Image-set context — mis-contextualisation caught and fixed via region-verified curation.
•Judge–humanκ— pending (blind sheet and scorer prepared); all non-refusals manually reviewed.
•Platform confound — removed by moving to the raw Anthropic API at temperature 0.
•Failed-call artifacts — detected (empty responses) and corrected by re-running affected cells.
•Statistical power — low (n= 15/cell); read effect sizes, not significance.
