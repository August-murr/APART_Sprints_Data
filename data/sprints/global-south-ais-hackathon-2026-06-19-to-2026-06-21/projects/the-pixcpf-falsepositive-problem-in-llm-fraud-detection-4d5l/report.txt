Abstract  
Every  day,  millions  of  Brazilians  rely  on  Pix  and  CPF  —  and  scammers  know  it,  projecting  the  same  urgent,  government-coded  language  as  routine  ﬁnancial  notices.  We  built  a  contrastive  benchmark  of  Brazilian-Portuguese  messages  where  harmful  and  benign  versions  of  the  same  scenario  share  vocabulary  and  structure  but  differ  only  in  intent,  then  tested  an  LLM  fraud  analyst  on  1,461  labeled  messages  and  the  full  harmful  and  benign  pair  set.  
We  expected  the  model  to  miss  disguised  fraud.  Instead,  it  sees  fraud  everywhere.  It  catches  real  scams  reliably  (95.9%  recall)  but  clears  legitimate  Pix/CPF  messages  only  19.5%  of  the  time  —  correctly  telling  the  two  apart  in  just  15.3%  of  pairs.  Surprisingly,  ordinary  benign  messages  score  worse  than  every  actual  manipulation  tactic  (53.2%  vs.  94%+),  suggesting  the  model  is  reacting  to  fraud-coded  words,  not  reasoning  about  intent.  A  preliminary  pilot,  spanning  ﬂagship  LLM  models,  shows  the  same  pattern  at  every  capability  level.  
For  a  country  that  runs  on  Pix  and  CPF,  a  ﬁlter  this  trigger-happy  isn't  just  inaccurate  —  it  risks  alert  fatigue  and  eroded  trust  in  legitimate  messages,  a  more  immediate  threat  than  the  usual  "model  misses  the  scam"  framing.  We  release  the  dataset,  an  evaluation  harness,  and  a  full  per-category  breakdown

5.  Interpretation  
The  results  suggest  that  the  dominant  failure  mode  is  not  failure  to  detect  fraudulent  content  but  
to
 
distinguish
 
fraudulent
 
from
 
legitimate
 
messages
 
that
 
share
 
the
 
same
 
vocabulary.
 
Three

support
 
this
 
interpretation:
 
1.  Harmful-side  accuracy  is  consistently  high  (95.9%),  while  benign-side  accuracy  is  
substantially
 
lower
 
(19.5%).
 2.  Benign  messages  without  any  manipulation  technique  achieve  lower  accuracy  (53.2%)  than  
nearly
 
every
 
manipulation
 
category.
 3.  Performance  remains  relatively  stable  across  difﬁculty  levels,  despite  the  expectation  that  
genuinely

LLM  models  rely  heavily  on  fraud-associated  lexical  and  contextual  cues  (e.g.,  Pix,  CPF,  government  communications)  rather  than  reasoning  about  the  speciﬁc  intent  of  the  message.
